## asia-pacific

**The Hormuz Hedge**

The Middle East oil shock sweeping through the Indo-Pacific this week reveals something more durable than a crisis: governments across the region are discovering that economic vulnerability is the mother of political reinvention, and most are seizing the moment to concentrate power rather than share it. From Canberra's emergency fuel requisitions to Jakarta's resource nationalism, from Seoul's energy-conservation blitz to Tokyo's constitutional tap-dance, leaders are using external disruption as licence to do things they already wanted to do. The pattern is not panic but opportunism — and it is reordering domestic politics faster than any adversary could.

Australia offers the starkest case. Anthony Albanese's decision to take emergency powers to purchase fuel, release strategic stocks and slash petrol taxes amounts to the heaviest state intervention in the economy since the pandemic. Yet the political payoff is handsome: six in ten voters blame Donald Trump for the crisis while barely one in seven blame the government. The deeper shift, though, is structural. A country that for decades outsourced its energy security to global markets now faces the humiliating arithmetic that its marquee defence project, AUKUS, may never deliver the submarines it was promised, even as the crisis it was meant to insure against has arrived early. Mr Albanese is converting a supply shock into a mandate for dirigisme, and voters so far seem grateful rather than alarmed.

South Korea's Lee Jae-myung is playing a subtler game. His nomination of Shin Hyun-song, a hawkish crisis-predictor, to run the central bank signals that he wants a monetary authority willing to tolerate a weaker won if it means preserving room to spend. The Bank of Korea's record profits from dollar-selling interventions — a peculiar windfall that lets the government pocket foreign-exchange gains while the currency slides — buy Mr Lee fiscal space just as he launches emergency budgets and twelve-step conservation campaigns. Simultaneously, he is accelerating the timeline for reclaiming wartime operational control from Washington and considering dropping co-sponsorship of the UN resolution on North Korean human rights. Each move individually is explicable; taken together they reveal a president using the fog of a Middle Eastern war to redraw South Korea's security posture without provoking a direct confrontation with its American ally.

Japan's manoeuvre is the most elegant of the lot. Sanae Takaichi refused Mr Trump's request for destroyers in the Strait of Hormuz, citing Article 9 of the pacifist constitution — a document that has never stopped Tokyo from doing anything it truly wished to do. The real payment came in Alaskan crude-oil contracts and the implicit promise of mine-clearing expertise after any ceasefire. Yet even as Japan demurred on Hormuz, it quietly abolished its sixty-year-old Fleet Escort Force and created a leaner, more offensive naval structure designed for island defence against China. The restructuring, dressed up as administrative tidying, is the most significant reorganisation of Japan's surface fleet in a generation. Tokyo is using constitutional scruples to avoid one theatre while retooling for another.

Taiwan and Indonesia round out the picture in complementary ways. Lai Ching-te's decision to restart nuclear plants — shattering the Democratic Progressive Party's decades-old environmental orthodoxy — is crisis realism dressed as pragmatism: an island whose semiconductor fabs are already choking global supply chains cannot afford to run short of electricity. In Jakarta, Prabowo Subianto's Rp 239 trillion downstreaming push predates the Hormuz disruption but has been supercharged by it; telling your energy minister that natural resources are "strategic assets" under state control sounds more reasonable when two Pertamina tankers are stranded in the Persian Gulf. Both leaders are converting external threat into domestic authority, sidelining opponents who might otherwise object.

The common thread is not co-ordination but convergence. Across the Indo-Pacific, elected leaders are exploiting a genuine external shock to accelerate pre-existing agendas — centralising economic control, reshaping military command, reversing long-held party doctrines, and recalibrating alliance obligations — all while pointing to the crisis as justification. The playbook is older than any of them: never let a disruption go to waste. What distinguishes this week is the sheer simultaneity of the manoeuvre, and the speed with which democratic publics are acquiescing. The risk, as always, is that powers grabbed in emergencies outlast the emergencies themselves.

## frontline-eastern-europe

**When the War Comes Home**

The week's news from central and eastern Europe points to a single, uncomfortable conclusion: the institutions these states built to anchor themselves in the West are now buckling under the combined weight of a war next door and the political opportunism it invites. Stray drones, fuel shocks and coalition fractures are not separate crises but symptoms of one. Governments that spent decades constructing liberal-democratic machinery—independent courts, central banks, stable coalitions—find that machinery tested not mainly by Moscow's aggression but by their own leaders' readiness to exploit the emergency for domestic advantage.

Poland's constitutional confrontation is the clearest case. Karol Nawrocki, the president, has refused to swear in six judges elected by parliament, turning what should be a ceremonial act into a veto over judicial appointments. Donald Tusk, the prime minister, insists the judges will take their oath regardless, while the parliamentary speaker has simply cut off contact with the Presidential Palace. The standoff is nominally about restoring the Constitutional Tribunal's legitimacy after years of politicisation under Law and Justice, yet it risks replacing one form of institutional capture with another—a government that bypasses the head of state when he proves inconvenient. Mr Nawrocki's retort was to fly to Budapest and pose with Viktor Orbán, prompting Mr Tusk to invoke Putin's satisfaction, a rhetorical escalation that folds a domestic dispute into the language of wartime loyalty. Both sides are weaponising the crisis rather than resolving it.

Ukraine's own institutions betray similar strain. Volodymyr Zelensky publicly accused the Trump administration of conditioning security guarantees on a withdrawal from Donbas; Marco Rubio, the secretary of state, called it "a lie." The exchange marks the most serious rupture between Kyiv and Washington since the full-scale invasion began, and it is hard to say which side benefits from airing it. At home, Mr Zelensky's ruling party held an emergency congress to restructure itself while members of parliament boycott votes on legislation the IMF requires. A country fighting for survival is simultaneously hollowing out the parliamentary processes that underwrite Western financial support—a contradiction its allies will not overlook indefinitely.

The physical overspill of the war now reaches countries that thought geography still offered a buffer. Ukrainian drones crashed in Latvia, Estonia and near Lithuania's border with Belarus within the same week, the first such incidents on allied soil. Estonia's Auvere power plant took a hit; Latvia's president lodged a diplomatic protest with Moscow. Yet neither country tempered its support for Ukraine—Latvia's defence minister travelled to Kyiv with more drones the same week. The real test was domestic: Estonia's hastily composed emergency alerts overloaded phone lines and sowed confusion, exposing gaps between the political will to stand firm and the operational capacity to manage consequences. Finland's finance minister admitted the country lacks the fiscal buffers of Sweden should the Middle East conflict deepen, even as Helsinki pushed to lift Cold War restrictions on NATO nuclear deterrence. Preparedness, it turns out, is easier to declare than to fund.

Coalition politics across the region reflects the same tension between security imperatives and partisan calculation. Lithuania's Social Democrats threatened to blow up their governing alliance over a military training ground, demanding loyalty on defence while their leader quietly locked up his party's internal nomination. Romania's Social Democratic Party scheduled a membership referendum on whether to stay in government at all, even as the central bank's deputy governor warned of an "unprecedented fiscal crisis." In the Czech Republic, a drone factory supplying Ukraine was torched—police suspect Russian-directed sabotage—while Andrej Babiš raged at his coalition partner for freelancing on broadcasting policy and the opposition staged petrol-station stunts. In each case, leaders treat the emergency as background noise to be selectively amplified or muted depending on what a domestic audience wants to hear.

The pattern is now plain enough to name. War, energy shocks and great-power realignment have not, on the whole, broken these countries' democratic frameworks. What they have done is reveal how thin the margin is between institutional resilience and institutional exploitation. Leaders across the region are learning the same lesson at the same time: a crisis large enough to justify extraordinary measures is also large enough to disguise ordinary power grabs. The question is no longer whether these states can withstand external pressure, but whether their politicians will let them.

## near-east-south-asia

**The Broker's Dilemma**

The Iran war is proving that middle powers gain leverage not by fighting but by making themselves useful—yet the price of usefulness keeps rising. Across the Gulf, Turkey, South Asia and beyond, governments are scrambling to convert a crisis they did not start into influence they badly want, only to find that diplomatic ambition collides with domestic fragility. The pattern of the week is not escalation itself but the widening gap between what regional actors promise abroad and what they can sustain at home.

Saudi Arabia illustrates the contradiction most starkly. Mohammed bin Salman is urging Washington to intensify strikes on Iran, calling the campaign a "historic opportunity" to remake the Middle East. Yet the war he wants enlarged is already dismantling his grandest domestic project. NEOM, the centrepiece of Vision 2030, cancelled more than $5bn in construction contracts this week, while Aramco rerouted crude shipments away from the Strait of Hormuz and its chief executive vanished from international conferences. A crown prince simultaneously lobbying for wider conflict and signing a drone-defence pact with Ukraine—whose battlefield expertise he needs precisely because Iranian missiles keep hitting his kingdom—is not a strategist in command of events. He is a man whose two ambitions, regional dominance and economic transformation, now work against each other.

Turkey has extracted the most from the crisis at the least apparent cost. Donald Trump called Recep Tayyip Erdoğan "fantastic" for staying out of the fighting, while Iran's president thanked him in Turkish for condemning Israeli strikes. Ankara has become the only capital trusted by both Washington and Tehran to relay messages, a mediator's dream. But neutrality is not free. The Central Bank sold roughly 60 tons of gold in a fortnight to defend the lira against war-induced volatility, and at home Mr Erdoğan's government continued prosecuting Ekrem İmamoğlu, Istanbul's mayor, alongside hundreds of co-defendants, while arresting opposition mayors across six provinces. The diplomatic prestige Turkey earns abroad rests on a political system that is hollowing out its own democratic legitimacy.

Pakistan's bid is the most audacious and the most precarious. Field Marshal Asim Munir telephoned Mr Trump to pitch Islamabad as a venue for peace talks, and Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif followed up with Gulf leaders to cement the offer. The gamble is that brokering a deal would transform Pakistan from a supplicant perpetually seeking IMF bailouts into an indispensable interlocutor. Yet Field Marshal Munir undermined the effort almost immediately by telling Shia clerics who "love Iran so much" to emigrate there—a remark that inflamed sectarian fury days after at least 35 people died in the suppression of Shia protests. A country that cannot manage its own sectarian cleavages is unlikely to be trusted to manage a negotiation between the powers that exploit them. Meanwhile, military operations against Afghanistan resumed after a brief Eid pause, a reminder that Pakistan's borders are no less volatile than its diplomacy is ambitious.

India, by contrast, has chosen the opposite strategy: quiet self-sufficiency dressed up as strategic autonomy. Narendra Modi, now India's longest-serving elected leader, spoke to Mr Trump about Hormuz security, shipped 38,000 tonnes of petroleum to Sri Lanka and approved $25bn in defence purchases—all without committing to any side. His foreign minister's public dismissal of Pakistan as a mere "broker nation" was less a diplomatic statement than a declaration that India considers mediation beneath it. The posture works so long as oil keeps flowing from 41 different suppliers, but it also means India accumulates no diplomatic capital from the crisis, only hardware.

What the week reveals is a structural truth about regional power in wartime: the countries trying hardest to shape the conflict are the ones most destabilised by it. Saudi Arabia wants escalation but bleeds investment; Turkey wants mediation but burns reserves and prosecutes opponents; Pakistan wants relevance but fans the sectarian fires the war ignites. Brokering influence from someone else's war is seductive precisely because it appears cheap—until the invoice arrives at home.

## the-americas

# The Subsidy Hangover

**Across the Americas this week, governments discovered that spending commitments made for political survival are easier to announce than to sustain — and that the bills, whether fiscal, diplomatic, or coalitional, arrive faster than the benefits.**

Canada's leap to NATO's 2% defence-spending target, trumpeted by Mark Carney from the deck of a warship in Halifax, looks less like strategic conviction than like a political debt paid under duress. For three decades Ottawa coasted as a free-rider; now it pledges 5% of GDP by 2035, a figure so ambitious it strains credulity. The promise buys credibility with Washington at a moment when Donald Trump's tariff threats have made alliance membership feel existential. Yet the Bank of Canada held rates at 2.25% the same week, warning that war-driven oil prices could force tightening — a reminder that the fiscal space Mr Carney is pledging away may not exist when the invoices come due. Defence surges funded during commodity booms have a poor record of surviving the next downturn.

In Chile, José Antonio Kast is learning the arithmetic of austerity in real time. His approval rating shed 13 points in a single month after he scrapped fuel subsidies, sending petrol prices up by as much as 54%. Students filled Santiago's streets; police answered with water cannon and arrested a 13-year-old. The irony is structural: a president elected on a law-and-order platform simultaneously cut 72 billion pesos from the security ministry, including 51 billion from the Carabineros who were dispersing his opponents. Mr Kast inherited record copper and lithium revenues from Codelco — 84% of its $2.4 billion profit came from a state lithium joint venture his predecessors arranged — yet chose to impose pain on consumers rather than spend the windfall. Ideology, not insolvency, is driving the backlash.

Mexico's Banco de México made the opposite bet, cutting its benchmark rate to 6.75% even as inflation touched 4.63%, its highest since 2024. Two of five board members dissented. The easing looks like a quiet subsidy to a slowing economy at the cost of price discipline — the monetary mirror of the fiscal largesse Claudia Sheinbaum is dispensing through a new pension reaching 3.5 million women. Ms Sheinbaum's political instincts are sharp: she simultaneously abolished gold-plated pensions for former officials of the state electricity company and Pemex, framing redistribution as anti-elite populism. But accommodation from the central bank, combined with peso weakness, risks making the transfers self-defeating if inflation eats them away.

Brazil illustrates where these contradictions lead once a political cycle matures. Lula da Silva's approval sits at 61%, yet nearly three-quarters of voters aged 16 to 24 disapprove of him, and polls show Flávio Bolsonaro — son of a man serving 27 years in prison — running level for 2026. Lula's centrist coalition partners, the Brazilian Democratic Movement and the Social Democratic Party, have already signalled they will not join his re-election alliance, forcing the Workers' Party back onto a narrow left-wing base. The spending that bought broad popularity has not purchased durable loyalty, least of all among the young, who entered the workforce amid inflation and fiscal strain they associate with incumbency rather than opposition.

The pattern across these four countries is not ideological but temporal: governments are mortgaging future fiscal and political capital to meet today's crises, and the repayment schedule is accelerating. Canada binds itself to defence outlays it may not afford; Chile discovers that cutting subsidies without a growth story produces revolt; Mexico eases monetary policy into rising prices; Brazil's governing coalition frays even as headline approval holds. In each case, leaders treated public money or institutional credibility as a resource to be drawn down now and replenished later. The week's evidence suggests that "later" keeps arriving sooner than expected.

## western-europe

**When the Atlantic Frays**

The week's events across Europe point to a single, uncomfortable conclusion: the transatlantic bond is no longer holding European politics together but is actively pulling it apart, forcing governments into domestic contortions they would otherwise avoid. From Berlin to London, from Stockholm to Madrid, the strain of American belligerence over Iran—and the broader erosion of trust in Washington—is not merely a foreign-policy headache. It is reshaping coalitions, budgets, defence plans and the electoral fortunes of incumbents and insurgents alike.

Germany offers the starkest proof. Frank-Walter Steinmeier's declaration that the Iran war is "illegal under international law" marks the most explicit rupture with Washington since the Federal Republic's founding—a sentence no German president could have uttered even five years ago without causing constitutional vertigo. Yet Mr Steinmeier's words follow logically from the position staked out by Friedrich Merz and Boris Pistorius: that Germany will not be drawn into a conflict it was never consulted about. The domestic corollary is a government that can afford to be bold at home precisely because it has chosen defiance abroad. A 500-billion-euro infrastructure fund, a 17% jump in federal investment, a proposed overhaul of the tax code—these are the moves of leaders who know that standing up to Mr Trump plays well with voters, as the CDU's breakthrough in Rheinland-Pfalz confirms. But the AfD's 19.5% in that same election, its best-ever western result, suggests that nationalist energy, once summoned, does not flow in only one direction.

Britain's predicament is less dignified. Mr Trump's mockery of Keir Starmer and dismissal of the Royal Navy's carriers as "toys" is cruel in part because it is not entirely wrong: the defence secretary's inability to say how many warships the navy has made the insult land harder. Yet the humiliation is paradoxically useful to Mr Starmer, whose approval ratings have risen as voters rally against American bullying. The deeper damage is economic. Oil and gas prices have surged by the most since Russia's 2022 invasion, the Bank of England is holding rates at 3.75% with hikes now expected, and Rachel Reeves has been forced to ration energy support. A war Britain refused to join is nonetheless taxing its economy as if it had.

France and Italy show that the stress fractures run through institutions, not just budgets. Marine Le Pen's Rassemblement National seized 74 town halls—its biggest local haul in modern history—while the left alliance tore itself to pieces, with Jean-Luc Mélenchon savaging his own former allies. Ms Le Pen's trip to Budapest to back Viktor Orban's veto of a €90 billion EU loan to Ukraine reveals the real strategic danger: the transatlantic rupture does not just embolden the far right at home, it gives it a foreign-policy programme. In Rome, Giorgia Meloni watched voters reject her constitutional referendum by a comfortable margin, a rebuke made sharper by coalition infighting within Forza Italia. Even her government's one unifying moment—protesting Israel's blocking of Palm Sunday Mass in Jerusalem—underscored how readily European leaders now reach for gestures of independence from American-aligned positions.

The Nordic countries, supposedly the continent's most stable corner, are feeling the same pull. Norway announced its largest defence increase in decades—115 billion kroner through 2036—only to see its coalition crack when parliament voted to cut fuel taxes against the government's wishes, blowing a 6.7-billion-kroner hole in the budget. Sweden's Liberals formally accepted the Sweden Democrats as a governing partner, completing that party's journey from pariah to power, while Ulf Kristersson picked a public fight with Hungary over leaked EU discussions with Russia. Even Spain's Pedro Sánchez, clinging to his "No to war" stance in congress, reshuffled his cabinet to elevate technocrats over politicians, a tacit admission that the political class he leads is not trusted to manage the gathering storm. The Bank of Spain warned that a prolonged Iran conflict could nearly triple inflation and cut growth by a third of a percentage point—numbers that concentrate minds faster than any parliamentary debate.

The pattern is now plain enough to name. American coercion is not producing compliance; it is accelerating Europe's centrifugal tendencies. Governments that defy Washington win popularity at home but face economic blowback they cannot control. Far-right parties exploit the disorder to present themselves as the only honest brokers of sovereignty. And defence spending, long deferred, is being ramped up in a hurry that guarantees waste and shortages—Sweden's 134 cold-weather injuries in two months of Arctic training are a small but telling emblem. Europe is not uniting against America so much as fragmenting in America's shadow, each country improvising its own balance of defiance and dependence. That is not an alliance in crisis. It is an alliance discovering it may no longer be one.
