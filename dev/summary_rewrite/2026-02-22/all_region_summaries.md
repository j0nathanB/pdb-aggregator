## asia-pacific

**The Alignment Premium**

Across the Indo-Pacific this week, governments that once hedged between Washington and Beijing are discovering that Donald Trump's return demands a price of admission—and most are willing to pay it. From Indonesia's sweeping trade concessions to Taiwan's chipmaking transplant to Japan's scrapping of arms-export limits, the pattern is less a pivot than a purchase: influence in the new Washington is being bought with tangible strategic goods, and the buyers are calculating that the cost of staying outside is higher still.

Indonesia's case is the most striking. Prabowo Subianto flew to Washington and signed away tariffs on 99% of American products, lifted restrictions on critical minerals, and pledged 8,000 peacekeepers for Gaza—a commitment ten times larger than any previous Indonesian deployment and one no other Southeast Asian country has matched. The deal was extravagant by any measure, yet it was rational. Mr Prabowo keeps his BRICS membership and his Chinese partnerships, but he has given Mr Trump something no other regional leader has: a visible win. The trouble is that Washington's favour comes cheaper than Jakarta's domestic credibility. Credit agencies have downgraded Indonesia over central-bank independence, the rupiah is weakening, and Mr Prabowo's coalition has imposed a formal ban on criticising the president. A leader who gives everything abroad while tightening everything at home eventually finds he has pleased neither audience.

Taiwan and Japan show a different version of the same calculation. TSMC is weighing $100 billion to build four more fabs in Arizona, a move that would shift much of the world's most advanced chipmaking to American soil and, not incidentally, exempt those chips from tariffs. It is a staggering concession of Taiwan's single greatest strategic asset—the silicon shield that makes the island indispensable—in exchange for deeper economic integration with its protector. Japan's bet is military rather than industrial. Sanae Takaichi, re-elected as prime minister with 354 votes, has already moved to scrap rules limiting lethal-arms exports and pledged to end fiscal austerity through "proactive public finances." Mr Trump claims credit for her landslide and has invited her to the White House. Ms Takaichi, whom *The Economist* calls the world's most powerful woman, appears content to let him.

South Korea is the awkward exception that proves the rule. Lee Jae-myung, riding a 56.5% approval rating after Yoon Suk-yeol's life sentence for the martial-law debacle, has clashed with Washington over scaling back the annual Freedom Shield military exercise—the first known dispute over joint planning since he took office. Mr Lee is courting Lula and the developing world instead. Whether this is strategic diversification or mere stubbornness, it carries risk: the won is weak, the Bank of Korea is frozen at 2.5% for a sixth straight meeting, and Seoul can ill afford to be the one ally in the region that Mr Trump decides to punish.

Australia, meanwhile, is writing cheques its politics can barely cash. The $3.9 billion payment for the AUKUS submarine yard at Osborne—part of a $30 billion programme—locks the country into decades of alliance infrastructure even as the Liberal opposition polls at 18%, behind Pauline Hanson's One Nation. The Reserve Bank has tightened rates to 3.85%, making Australia the first major economy to raise borrowing costs after the pandemic. The paradox is instructive: Australia's defence commitments are bipartisan and generational, yet its domestic politics are so fractured that no party can claim a mandate for the grocery bill, let alone a nuclear submarine.

What the week reveals is not that the Indo-Pacific is rallying to America out of conviction, but that alignment has become transactional in a way it was not before. Each government is offering something concrete—market access, troops, semiconductor fabs, arms-export liberalisation, shipyard billions—in exchange for tariff relief, diplomatic cover, or simply the absence of punishment. The price of entry is rising, and the leaders paying it are betting that the returns will justify what they have given away. That bet may prove correct. But when alignment is purchased rather than earned, it lasts only as long as the buyer feels he is getting value. The moment the terms change, so will the loyalty.

## frontline-eastern-europe

**The Hardening of the Frontier**

The week across central and eastern Europe reveals a single uncomfortable truth: the states most committed to defending the liberal order abroad are steadily straining it at home. From Warsaw to Prague, from Vilnius to Bucharest, governments and presidents are arming against Russia, rallying behind Ukraine and lecturing allies on burden-sharing — while simultaneously testing the independence of judges, intelligence services, central banks and prosecutors. The contradiction is not hypocrisy so much as a structural bind: war-footing politics concentrates power, and concentrated power erodes the institutions these countries claim to be protecting.

Poland's President Karol Nawrocki offered the week's starkest example. Having vetoed legislation that would have restored judicial independence from political interference, he proposed his own bill threatening judges with up to five years in prison for questioning state institutions or applying EU law. The justice minister warned the measure could force Poland out of the EU — an extraordinary statement about a president elected, in part, to defend Polish sovereignty within Europe. Mr Nawrocki's move is not an aberration; it is the logical terminus of a contest between Law and Justice's institutional legacy and Donald Tusk's coalition, which is itself splintering as fifteen lawmakers defected from Poland 2050 to form a new parliamentary club. Mr Tusk boasts of 3.6% growth and calls Poland an "oasis of stability," but the phrase rings hollow when the president and parliament are at open war over who controls the judiciary.

In Prague, the collision between democratic norms and governing convenience was almost as blunt. Hundreds of towns staged rallies defending President Petr Pavel's refusal to appoint a minister with a tainted record, while the same week a parliamentary committee recommended shielding Prime Minister Andrej Babiš from fraud charges and the speaker from hate-speech prosecution. The full vote falls on March 5. Czechs are simultaneously defending one constitutional prerogative in the streets and watching another gutted in committee rooms. That the same government continues to deliver millions of artillery rounds to Ukraine — 4.4 million large-calibre shells so far — only sharpens the irony: Prague fights for the rules-based order abroad while bending rules at home.

The Baltic states, often held up as the alliance's most serious members, show similar fractures beneath their hawkish surfaces. Estonia's foreign minister offered to host NATO nuclear weapons and promised to carry any war onto Russian soil, yet a former intelligence executive published an account of ministers phoning at midnight to demand she alter assessments. Lithuania's government split openly over whether to rename its Taiwanese office to placate Beijing, with the prime minister calling the original decision a case of "jumping in front of a train" — hardly the rhetoric of a country that lectures others on standing up to autocracies. Latvia's central bank warned the state is heading "straight toward a cliff edge" on debt even as it opens new training ranges and pledges 5% of GDP to defence. Finland's finance minister floated a plan to make immigrants collect branches for benefits, then conceded it was unconstitutional — a sequence that manages to be both nativist and incompetent.

Ukraine itself, the cause around which this entire region organises its foreign policy, demonstrated the same tension in its most acute form. Valerii Zaluzhnyi, the former army chief and the country's most trusted public figure, broke openly with President Volodymyr Zelensky, accusing him of sending security agents to intimidate his command and of political interference that doomed the 2023 counteroffensive. The rift is the gravest domestic challenge Mr Zelensky has faced since the full-scale invasion. It arrived in the same week that peace talks with Russia collapsed in Geneva after two hours and Mr Zelensky asked American lawmakers in Munich for Tomahawk missiles. The pattern is familiar: external peril justifies the concentration of authority, which in turn produces the internal fractures that weaken the state's bargaining position.

What connects all these cases is not mere coincidence of timing but a common logic. Governments on a war footing accumulate executive power — over courts, intelligence agencies, budgets, diplomacy — faster than their institutions can absorb. The longer the emergency persists, the harder it becomes to distinguish between measures taken to defend democracy and those that quietly hollow it out. Eastern Europe's leaders would do well to notice that the autocracies they oppose got started the same way.

## near-east-south-asia

# The Hedging Powers

The week's events across the middle powers of Asia and the Gulf reveal a common pattern: governments that once relied on a single dominant patron are now building overlapping alliances so feverishly that their hedging strategies have begun to contradict themselves. India, Turkey, Saudi Arabia and the UAE each acted simultaneously to deepen ties with the West and to defy or undercut Western partners, producing diplomatic postures that look less like strategic ambiguity than strategic incoherence.

India offered the clearest case. Narendra Modi's government used Emmanuel Macron's visit to Mumbai to sign more than 20 defence and technology agreements, elevating France to "Special Global Strategic Partner" — a title plainly designed to give New Delhi leverage against Washington's demands that it stop buying Russian oil. Yet India also courted American goodwill by hosting an AI summit at which the Adani Group pledged $100bn in renewable-energy-powered data centres, a commitment calibrated to appeal to both Silicon Valley and the White House. Mr Modi cannot indefinitely tell Marco Rubio that Indian energy choices are none of America's business while asking American capital to bankroll his digital ambitions. The contradiction is sustainable only so long as neither patron forces a choice.

Recep Tayyip Erdoğan's Turkey stretched a similar elastic further. Ankara celebrated 74 years in NATO by showing off its TB3 drones in the alliance's biggest exercise, then turned around and jailed a Deutsche Welle journalist for insulting the president, daring Germany to do something about it. Mr Erdoğan offered troops for a Gaza peacekeeping force in Washington while his courts prepared espionage proceedings against Istanbul's opposition mayor, Ekrem İmamoğlu — the man Western capitals regard as Turkey's democratic hope. The Kurdish peace process, meanwhile, is hostage to the constitutional arithmetic that could extend presidential rule beyond 2028. Every outward-facing gesture of alliance loyalty serves an inward-facing consolidation of power, and Western partners, needing Turkish drones and Turkish geography, swallow the affront.

Saudi Arabia's hedging crossed into open confrontation with a fellow hedger. Mohammed bin Salman ordered airstrikes against UAE-backed forces in Yemen and sent a formal complaint to Abu Dhabi listing grievances from Sudan to the southern coast — a remarkable escalation between two states that once presented a united front. Yet the crown prince simultaneously decorated a retired American general with the King Abdulaziz Medal, reinforcing the security guarantee that underpins his entire modernisation programme. The kingdom is also quietly paring back Vision 2030's grandiose projects in favour of manufacturing and mining, an implicit admission that spectacle without returns cannot survive a world of tighter capital. Saudi Arabia wants American protection, Chinese investment and regional dominance; the Yemen strikes suggest that juggling all three is growing harder.

The UAE, for its part, spent the week denying that its president was unwell, a sign of how much its web of global bets depends on a single decision-maker. Mohammed bin Zayed's government posted record oil revenues, expanded Bitcoin holdings through BlackRock, committed to an AI supercomputer in India and met American officials on Gaza — all while a senior adviser publicly attacked Wahhabism, Saudi Arabia's founding creed. Abu Dhabi is hedging against its own closest ally even as it hedges against the West.

The pattern is not balance-of-power diplomacy in any classical sense. It is something messier: middle powers accumulating options faster than they can reconcile them, betting that no partner will call the bluff because each needs something in return. That bet holds until a crisis — a forced choice on Russian oil, a Yemen escalation, a leadership vacuum — reveals that overlapping alliances are not the same as reliable ones. Hedging, pursued to excess, becomes its own vulnerability.

## the-americas

**North America's Expensive Autonomy**

The week's most striking pattern is not that middle powers are pushing back against the United States—that has been true for years—but that the cost of doing so is climbing fast enough to reshape domestic politics, military procurement, and fiscal strategy all at once. From Ottawa to Santiago, governments are discovering that reducing dependence on Washington demands institutional overhauls they had long postponed, while the crises that force their hand expose weaknesses they would rather conceal.

Mark Carney's $6.6 billion defence-industrial strategy is the clearest case. The plan to raise Canadian firms' share of defence contracts from 30% to 70% within a decade is less a military programme than an economic-nationalist manifesto dressed in khaki. Mr Carney is betting that the political dividend—125,000 jobs, a brokering role between the EU and the CPTPP, and a narrative of sovereign resilience—will outweigh the higher unit costs that come with shrinking the supplier pool. The bet appears to be paying off domestically: a third Conservative defection has brought the Liberals within touching distance of a majority, while Pierre Poilievre's caucus stumbles over whether resisting American tariffs counts as patriotism or hysteria. Yet the strategy's logic depends on trade tensions remaining high enough to justify the premium Canadians will pay. Should Washington offer a friendlier deal at the USMCA review, the entire edifice could look like expensive insurance against a fire that never came.

Chile's submarine-cable affair shows what happens when a smaller economy tries the same balancing act with fewer cards. Washington's decision to revoke visas of three Chilean officials, including the transport minister, over a planned fibre-optic link to China is the first time the United States has used personal sanctions to kill a Chilean infrastructure project. The episode demolished the comfortable fiction that Santiago could treat superpower competition as someone else's problem. China's embassy denounced the move as hegemonic; the incoming government of José Antonio Kast, whose inauguration Secretary of State Marco Rubio will attend, has every incentive to let the cable quietly die. Chile's tradition of commercial pragmatism survives only so long as neither great power forces a binary choice—and that condition no longer holds.

Mexico's killing of Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes, the Jalisco cartel's leader, illustrates the paradox from the security side. The operation relied on American intelligence yet was executed by Mexican special forces—a division of labour that let Claudia Sheinbaum claim sovereignty while quietly accepting dependence. The cartel's retaliation, 252 roadblocks across more than 20 states and 25 dead National Guard troops, demonstrated the state's continued fragility. Ms Sheinbaum's political position is weakening in parallel: her coalition partners refused to back electoral reform, stripping Morena of its constitutional supermajority, and internal feuds over gubernatorial nominations have erupted in 15 states. Pemex, meanwhile, completed a $1.8 billion bond issue even as auditors revealed $2.3 billion owed to contractors and unjustified payments to Carlos Slim's companies—proof that investors trust the sovereign guarantee more than the institution behind it.

Brazil's Lula faces the starkest version of the dilemma. The American capture of Nicolás Maduro prompted emergency meetings with military chiefs that exposed gaps in air defences—an uncomfortable discovery for a president who had styled himself as the region's diplomatic heavyweight. His response has been to double down on multipolarity: an AI summit with Narendra Modi, advocacy for local-currency trade, and resumed Amazon-margin drilling by Petrobras. Yet the domestic scene undermines the grand posture. The Bolsonaro family is feuding over 2026 succession from a jail cell, the Supreme Court quietly dropped proceedings against one of its own justices despite police evidence of illegality, and even a samba school's tribute to Lula at Carnival became the subject of electoral litigation. The gap between Brazil's global ambitions and its institutional housekeeping has rarely been wider.

What unites these cases is not ideology but arithmetic. Autonomy from Washington now carries a measurable price tag—in defence budgets, lost infrastructure deals, cartel blowback, and exposed military shortcomings—and every government in the hemisphere is discovering that the bill arrives before the benefits do. The pattern is one of costly sovereignty: leaders are willing to pay, but none yet knows whether their electorates will keep writing the cheques.

## western-europe

**When the State Frays, It Frays From Within**

Western Europe's democracies are not being toppled by external enemies; they are being hollowed out by the failure of their own institutions to hold the centre against domestic fragmentation. The week's events across France, Germany, Britain, Italy, Spain and Scandinavia share a common thread: governments that remain capable abroad — signing defence deals, dispatching aid, projecting force — are losing their grip on the political bargains that make governance possible at home. The gap between external ambition and internal coherence is widening, and no amount of military modernisation or diplomatic pageantry can close it.

France offers the starkest case. The beating death of Quentin Deranque, a 23-year-old nationalist, in Lyon has done what years of polarisation could not: it has made political violence a live variable in French politics. A minute of silence in the National Assembly became a shouting match. Jordan Bardella demanded a cordon around La France Insoumise; Jean-Luc Mélenchon was branded a "dishonour to the Republic." Emmanuel Macron, meanwhile, flew to India to negotiate a $40 billion Rafale deal and summoned the American ambassador for meddling — all while the July 7 verdict that could bar Marine Le Pen from the 2027 presidential race ticks closer. France's capacity to act abroad has never looked more impressive, nor its capacity to govern at home more threadbare.

Germany's contradiction is subtler but no less telling. Friedrich Merz won re-election as CDU chairman with 91% of delegate votes and authorised the most sweeping expansion of intelligence powers since reunification, granting the BND authority for cyber sabotage and armed operations abroad. Yet his principal opposition, the Alternative for Germany, is simultaneously being classified as a confirmed extremist organisation in Lower Saxony and engulfed in a nepotism scandal that mocks its anti-establishment brand. The centre holds — but only because the fringe is discrediting itself, not because the centre has built anything durable. Defence procurement, meanwhile, is tangled in debates over whether a minority stake held by Peter Thiel in a drone supplier disqualifies it from a €540 million contract. Strategic ambition keeps colliding with procedural paralysis.

Britain and Italy illustrate the same pattern through different institutions. In London, Prince Andrew's arrest on his birthday forced King Charles into an unprecedented public disavowal — the monarchy's worst moment since the abdication — while a minister was revealed to have fed journalists' names to GCHQ under the pretext of countering Russian propaganda. Keir Starmer notched his 14th policy reversal, yet the Treasury posted a record £30.4 billion surplus. The books balance; the politics do not. In Rome, President Sergio Mattarella made his first appearance at the Superior Council of Magistrates in a decade, provoked by a justice minister who likened the judiciary to the mafia. A constitutional referendum on separating prosecutors from judges looms on March 22, polls split at 38-38. Giorgia Meloni's coalition is leaking support to a breakaway party led by a former general, and a spat with Mr Macron over the Lyon killing forced the postponement of an Italy-France summit. Both countries can still project power — AUKUS shipyards proceed, Italian troop expansion is planned — but the domestic scaffolding shakes.

Even Scandinavia, the supposed redoubt of stable governance, shows cracks under the surface calm. Sweden's foreign minister publicly rebuked Washington, calling American actions "damaging to trust," while dispatching a 12.9 billion kronor arms package to Ukraine — criticising the ally it still depends on. Jimmie Åkesson of the Sweden Democrats quietly retreated from a flagship immigration demand, exposing the fragility of the coalition's confidence-and-supply arrangement. In Spain, Pedro Sánchez's minority government lost Catalan budget support after a secret meeting with Oriol Junqueras failed, and his own deputy snubbed a left-wing unity event. Norway's prime minister floated an Olympic bid with the caveat that politicians would not lead it — a small symbol of a larger instinct: executives who no longer trust their own capacity to carry public opinion.

The pattern is not decline in the dramatic sense that editorialists like to invoke. These states are rearming, investing, and engaging diplomatically with real energy. What they are losing is the internal political consensus that turns policy into action at home. Coalitions fracture, norms erode, institutional referees are called in to do what politics once managed on its own. The danger is not that Europe's democracies will collapse, but that they will become capable fortresses with nobody minding the courtyard — strong enough to deter threats from abroad, too divided to fix anything within.
