# The Price of Keeping Up Appearances

**Governments everywhere are projecting strength abroad while losing their grip at home — and the gap is becoming the defining feature of the current order.**

The most important pattern in global politics this week is not any single crisis but a structural contradiction now visible on every continent: states are spending more, arming faster, and signing grander deals than at any point since the cold war, yet the domestic political foundations on which all this ambition rests are crumbling. From Jakarta to Warsaw, from Ottawa to Paris, governments are discovering that the capacity to act externally and the capacity to govern internally have decoupled. The result is not decline in any simple sense. It is something more disorienting — a world of impressive-looking powers that cannot reliably deliver at home what they promise abroad.

Indonesia's Prabowo Subianto captured the dynamic in its crudest form. He flew to Washington and gave Donald Trump almost everything imaginable — zero tariffs on 99% of American goods, 8,000 peacekeepers for Gaza, open access to critical minerals — and in return kept his seat at the table. The deal was rational as foreign policy. But its domestic cost is already visible: credit agencies have downgraded Indonesia over central-bank meddling, the rupiah is sliding, and Mr Prabowo's coalition has imposed a formal ban on criticising the president. A leader who gives everything abroad while tightening everything at home eventually satisfies neither audience. The same logic played out, more decorously, in Tokyo and Taipei. Japan's re-elected prime minister, Sanae Takaichi, scrapped lethal-arms-export limits and embraced fiscal expansion to please Washington; TSMC weighed shifting $100 billion in chipmaking capacity to Arizona, surrendering the silicon shield that makes Taiwan strategically indispensable. Each was paying an alignment premium — offering tangible strategic goods in exchange for tariff relief or diplomatic cover — and each was betting that the returns would justify the sacrifice. The trouble with purchased loyalty is that it lasts only as long as both sides feel they are getting value.

Eastern Europe's frontline states demonstrated the contradiction in its most acute form. Poland, the continent's most energetic supporter of Ukraine, saw its new president propose legislation threatening judges with prison for applying EU law — a measure the justice minister warned could force the country out of the union it claims to be defending. In Prague, hundreds of towns rallied for constitutional norms while a parliamentary committee moved to shield the prime minister from fraud charges. The Baltic states, routinely held up as NATO's most serious members, revealed their own fractures: a former Estonian intelligence chief published an account of ministers phoning at midnight to demand altered assessments, while Lithuania's government split over whether to rename its Taiwanese office to placate Beijing. Ukraine itself, the cause around which the entire region organises its foreign policy, saw its gravest internal rupture since the full-scale invasion when Valerii Zaluzhnyi accused President Zelensky of political interference that doomed the 2023 counteroffensive. War-footing politics concentrates power; concentrated power erodes the institutions these governments say they are fighting to protect.

Western Europe's wealthier democracies are not immune. France's Emmanuel Macron flew to India to close a $40 billion Rafale deal in the same week that political violence became a live variable in French politics and a verdict that could bar Marine Le Pen from the 2027 race drew closer. Germany's Friedrich Merz won a commanding re-election as party chairman and authorised sweeping new intelligence powers, yet a €540 million drone contract stalled because a minority shareholder happened to be Peter Thiel — strategic ambition colliding with procedural paralysis. In London, a minister was revealed to have fed journalists' names to GCHQ under the pretext of countering Russian propaganda, while the Treasury posted a record surplus. The books balanced; the politics did not. These states are rearming and engaging diplomatically with real energy, but the coalitions needed to turn external commitments into domestic policy are fragmenting faster than leaders can patch them together.

The Americas showed the same arithmetic in starker terms. Mark Carney's $6.6 billion plan to raise Canadian firms' share of defence contracts to 70% is economic nationalism dressed in khaki, sustainable only if trade tensions with Washington remain high enough to justify the premium. Chile discovered that commercial pragmatism has limits when the United States revoked visas of three officials over a planned fibre-optic link to China — the first time Washington used personal sanctions to kill a Chilean infrastructure project. Mexico killed the Jalisco cartel's leader using American intelligence but claimed sovereign credit, only to face 252 roadblocks and 25 dead National Guard troops in retaliation. Brazil's Lula convened emergency meetings with military chiefs after the American capture of Nicolás Maduro exposed gaps in air defences — an awkward discovery for a president who styles himself the region's diplomatic heavyweight. In each case, the cost of autonomy from Washington arrived before the benefits did.

The pattern across all these theatres is not ideological convergence or geopolitical realignment in any familiar sense. It is a widening gap between what governments can do abroad and what they can hold together at home. External commitments — alliance dues, trade concessions, arms purchases, peacekeeping pledges — are growing more expensive precisely as domestic coalitions grow more fragile. Leaders are writing cheques their politics can barely cash. The danger is not that these states will collapse, but that they will become capable fortresses with nobody minding the courtyard: strong enough to project power outward, too fractured to govern what lies within.
