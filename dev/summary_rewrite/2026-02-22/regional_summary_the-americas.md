**North America's Expensive Autonomy**

The week's most striking pattern is not that middle powers are pushing back against the United States—that has been true for years—but that the cost of doing so is climbing fast enough to reshape domestic politics, military procurement, and fiscal strategy all at once. From Ottawa to Santiago, governments are discovering that reducing dependence on Washington demands institutional overhauls they had long postponed, while the crises that force their hand expose weaknesses they would rather conceal.

Mark Carney's $6.6 billion defence-industrial strategy is the clearest case. The plan to raise Canadian firms' share of defence contracts from 30% to 70% within a decade is less a military programme than an economic-nationalist manifesto dressed in khaki. Mr Carney is betting that the political dividend—125,000 jobs, a brokering role between the EU and the CPTPP, and a narrative of sovereign resilience—will outweigh the higher unit costs that come with shrinking the supplier pool. The bet appears to be paying off domestically: a third Conservative defection has brought the Liberals within touching distance of a majority, while Pierre Poilievre's caucus stumbles over whether resisting American tariffs counts as patriotism or hysteria. Yet the strategy's logic depends on trade tensions remaining high enough to justify the premium Canadians will pay. Should Washington offer a friendlier deal at the USMCA review, the entire edifice could look like expensive insurance against a fire that never came.

Chile's submarine-cable affair shows what happens when a smaller economy tries the same balancing act with fewer cards. Washington's decision to revoke visas of three Chilean officials, including the transport minister, over a planned fibre-optic link to China is the first time the United States has used personal sanctions to kill a Chilean infrastructure project. The episode demolished the comfortable fiction that Santiago could treat superpower competition as someone else's problem. China's embassy denounced the move as hegemonic; the incoming government of José Antonio Kast, whose inauguration Secretary of State Marco Rubio will attend, has every incentive to let the cable quietly die. Chile's tradition of commercial pragmatism survives only so long as neither great power forces a binary choice—and that condition no longer holds.

Mexico's killing of Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes, the Jalisco cartel's leader, illustrates the paradox from the security side. The operation relied on American intelligence yet was executed by Mexican special forces—a division of labour that let Claudia Sheinbaum claim sovereignty while quietly accepting dependence. The cartel's retaliation, 252 roadblocks across more than 20 states and 25 dead National Guard troops, demonstrated the state's continued fragility. Ms Sheinbaum's political position is weakening in parallel: her coalition partners refused to back electoral reform, stripping Morena of its constitutional supermajority, and internal feuds over gubernatorial nominations have erupted in 15 states. Pemex, meanwhile, completed a $1.8 billion bond issue even as auditors revealed $2.3 billion owed to contractors and unjustified payments to Carlos Slim's companies—proof that investors trust the sovereign guarantee more than the institution behind it.

Brazil's Lula faces the starkest version of the dilemma. The American capture of Nicolás Maduro prompted emergency meetings with military chiefs that exposed gaps in air defences—an uncomfortable discovery for a president who had styled himself as the region's diplomatic heavyweight. His response has been to double down on multipolarity: an AI summit with Narendra Modi, advocacy for local-currency trade, and resumed Amazon-margin drilling by Petrobras. Yet the domestic scene undermines the grand posture. The Bolsonaro family is feuding over 2026 succession from a jail cell, the Supreme Court quietly dropped proceedings against one of its own justices despite police evidence of illegality, and even a samba school's tribute to Lula at Carnival became the subject of electoral litigation. The gap between Brazil's global ambitions and its institutional housekeeping has rarely been wider.

What unites these cases is not ideology but arithmetic. Autonomy from Washington now carries a measurable price tag—in defence budgets, lost infrastructure deals, cartel blowback, and exposed military shortcomings—and every government in the hemisphere is discovering that the bill arrives before the benefits do. The pattern is one of costly sovereignty: leaders are willing to pay, but none yet knows whether their electorates will keep writing the cheques.
