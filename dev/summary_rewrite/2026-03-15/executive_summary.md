# The Age of the Hedge

The week's events across every continent point to a single, uncomfortable conclusion: the international order is not breaking apart so much as being hollowed out by the governments that still profess to uphold it. From Seoul to Ottawa, from Riyadh to Warsaw, leaders are responding to overlapping crises — American retrenchment, Iranian aggression, trade wars, institutional rot — not with decisive action but with elaborate hedges. Each hedge is locally rational. Taken together, they describe a system in which no state is willing to bear the cost of leadership and every state is borrowing against a stability none is replenishing.

The Indo-Pacific offers the starkest illustration. America's removal of missile-defence batteries from South Korea to the Middle East, over Seoul's objections, shattered the polite fiction that alliance commitments are non-negotiable. Japan responded by acting unilaterally — releasing oil reserves before consulting the G7, hosting Taiwan's premier for the first time in half a century — while Taiwan's parliament unanimously approved $9 billion in arms purchases whose very urgency betrayed the anxiety behind them. Australia sent warplanes to the Gulf; Indonesia called American strikes "irrational" and offered to mediate. Each moved in a different direction, yet all were doing the same thing: building above a security floor that Washington once provided as a ceiling. The alliance holds, but only because every member has quietly stopped relying on it.

In eastern Europe, the friction is internal rather than external, and no less damaging for that. Poland's president vetoed a €43.7 billion EU defence loan on sovereignty grounds while Russia wages war next door. Ukraine rebuked American sanctions policy and accused Hungary of state terrorism in the same week that some 40 of its own ruling-party deputies tried to resign. Finland completed its transformation from neutral to nuclear-ready NATO state, yet its coalition frays over tax policy. Lithuania's prime minister stumbled into an ethics scandal as her army commander freelanced about offering bases for American strikes on Iran. These democracies are rearming at historic speed, but their politics remain organised around peacetime incentives — vetoes wielded for partisan advantage, scandals consuming oxygen that strategy demands. Moscow does not face equivalent friction costs, and knows it.

The Middle East and South Asia reveal a darker variant: governments using Iranian aggression as cover for agendas that would draw sharper scrutiny in peacetime. Saudi Arabia shelved NEOM's fantasies in favour of data centres and locked Pakistan into a mutual-defence pact, converting fiscal strain into purposeful reform. Pakistan itself imposed school closures, 30% salary cuts and a four-day working week under emergency authority no elected government could sustain without the alibi of war. Mr Erdoğan positioned Turkey as indispensable mediator while his judiciary opened the largest corruption trial in Turkish history, aimed at eliminating the opposition. The UAE responded to an Iranian refinery strike not with escalation but with a blitz of institution-building and 25 arrests for spreading panic online. Resilience, in each case, doubled as opportunism — and the emergency powers accrued along the way show no sign of being temporary.

Across the Americas, sovereignty itself has become the instrument of hedge. Mr Carney pledged to defend Greenland "however necessary," a promise Canada lacks the means to keep but which lured a fourth opposition MP into his caucus. Chile's new president signed a minerals deal with Washington on his first day, framing alignment as agency. Mexico's Ms Sheinbaum declared sovereignty "non-negotiable" while her coalition handed her its first legislative defeat; Brazil's Mr Lula revoked a Trump adviser's visa as a gesture of dignity while his approval ratings sank and diesel subsidies quietly rose. Each leader converted external pressure into domestic capital, but the conversion rate is falling: every act of defiance must be louder than the last, while the underlying problems — fragile coalitions, commodity shocks, institutional decay — remain untouched.

Western Europe completes the picture. Britain's Mr Starmer lost a safe seat to the Greens, refused American use of British bases, then took a conciliatory call from Mr Trump about the Strait of Hormuz — three postures in a single week, none of them a policy. Spain's Mr Sánchez found that opposing the war won votes in Castilla y León but earned endorsements from Hamas. Germany's Mr Merz criticised American sanctions while his party quietly cooperated with the far right it claims to shun. France's Mr Macron called an Iranian-linked militia attack "unacceptable" and immediately ruled out retaliation. The Nordic states threaded needles between defiance and loyalty with characteristic discipline, but even Stockholm urged citizens to keep cash at home and reduce dependence on American payment networks. Left and right, large and small, every government is splitting the difference between commitments it cannot meet and bases it cannot satisfy.

The pattern deserves a plain name: systemic hedging. When every government optimises for the next quarter rather than the next decade, when crises are exploited rather than resolved, and when alliances are maintained in form while being emptied of content, the structure still stands — but only until someone leans on it. The leaders described here are not fools; most are responding shrewdly to genuine constraints. The trouble is that shrewdness, aggregated across dozens of capitals, produces an international order in which everyone is buying time and no one is buying security. That is not a strategy. It is a bet that the bill never comes due.
