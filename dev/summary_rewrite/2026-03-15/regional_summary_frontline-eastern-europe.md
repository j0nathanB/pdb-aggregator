**When Allies Bicker, Adversaries Benefit**

The most striking feature of this week's events across central and eastern Europe is not the threat from Russia or Iran but the degree to which countries nominally on the same side are spending political capital fighting each other—or themselves. From Warsaw to Bucharest, from Kyiv to Prague, governments that should be marshalling resources for defence are instead locked in institutional trench warfare that saps their credibility and slows their preparations. The pattern suggests that the democratic messiness tolerable in peacetime is becoming a strategic liability in an era of live conflict and nuclear brinkmanship.

Poland offers the starkest case. President Karol Nawrocki's veto of the €43.7 billion SAFE defence loan—the country's largest allocation under the EU's €150 billion programme—was justified on sovereignty grounds, yet its practical effect is to deprive the Polish military of modernisation funds while Russia wages war next door. Mr Nawrocki then threatened to block six new Constitutional Tribunal judges, turning an institutional dispute into a second front against the Tusk government. The central bank governor's counter-proposal to finance defence from unrealised gold profits was rightly dismissed as fantasy. Poland is not short of enemies; it should not be manufacturing domestic ones at this pace.

Ukraine, the country with the most reason to keep friends close, chose this week to antagonise two of them. President Zelensky publicly rebuked American sanctions policy over a 30-day waiver on Russian oil, while his foreign minister accused Hungary of "state terrorism" after Hungarian authorities seized millions in cash and gold from Ukrainian bank couriers. Both complaints may be justified, but the timing—amid a parliamentary revolt in which some 40 ruling-party deputies are trying to resign—suggests a government reaching for external fights to distract from internal fragility. That Ukraine simultaneously dispatched drone specialists to Gulf states shows it retains diplomatic initiative; whether it can sustain that initiative while haemorrhaging legislative cohesion is another matter.

Finland's decision to lift restrictions on nuclear weapons from its territory represents the week's most consequential policy shift, yet even here the theme of allied friction applies. The move completes Finland's transformation from Nordic neutral to frontline NATO state, but Prime Minister Orpo's coalition is fraying over tax policy, with the Finns Party blocking inheritance-tax cuts. In Lithuania, the prime minister stumbled into an ethics scandal over an €8,000 family trip to the Vatican just as her party slid to fourth in polls, while her army commander freelanced publicly about offering bases for American strikes on Iran—a suggestion she had to disavow in evident surprise. Latvia's prime minister faced criticism from her own coalition partners for poor coordination. In each case the substance of defence policy is advancing—German troops deploying, missiles arriving, NATO facilities being built—while the political superstructure wobbles.

The Czech Republic and Romania round out the picture. Prague drew sharp American criticism for budgeting only 1.7% of GDP on defence in 2026, even as Czech-American trade hit $12.6 billion and Czech troops tested new fighting vehicles alongside allies. Romania approved American use of its airbases for Middle East operations, a significant commitment, but its governing coalition is fracturing over social spending, with the Social Democrats forming tactical alliances with the far-right to block appointments. In both countries the gap between military ambition and political discipline is widening.

What the week reveals is a structural problem, not a series of coincidences. Eastern Europe's democracies are rearming at historic speed, yet their domestic politics remain organised around peacetime incentives—vetoes deployed for partisan advantage, coalition agreements treated as optional, scandals allowed to consume oxygen that should go to strategy. Adversaries in Moscow and Tehran do not face equivalent friction costs. Until the region's leaders treat political cohesion as itself a defence asset, the weapons they are buying will arrive faster than the consensus needed to use them.
