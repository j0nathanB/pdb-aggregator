## asia-pacific

**The Shield Slips**

The week's events across the Indo-Pacific reveal a single uncomfortable truth: American security guarantees, long treated as fixed architecture, are now visibly contingent on Washington's priorities elsewhere—and every capital in the region is scrambling to adjust, each in a way that exposes the contradictions of its own position.

South Korea offers the starkest proof. The removal of THAAD and Patriot batteries to the Middle East over Seoul's objections is not merely a logistical reshuffle; it is the first time America has stripped defence assets from the peninsula against its ally's wishes. Lee Jae-myung's admission that South Korea "cannot stop" the move laid bare the asymmetry that decades of alliance management had papered over. Mr Lee's response—fuel price caps, military pension pledges, an invitation to Mr Macron—amounts to a domestic anaesthetic and a diplomatic hedge, neither of which replaces a missile-defence system. His opposition, meanwhile, is too busy disowning an impeached predecessor to exploit the opening. Pyongyang, analysts note, may not be so distracted.

Japan drew the opposite lesson and acted first. Sanae Takaichi's unilateral release of oil reserves—before consulting G7 partners—and her deployment of long-range missiles covering the East China Sea signal a country that has decided waiting for alliance coordination is itself a risk. The symbolism of hosting Taiwan's premier for the first time in 52 years reinforces the point: Tokyo is building relationships that Washington has long discouraged, precisely because Washington can no longer be relied upon to hold every line. Ms Takaichi's approval rating dipped, but her boldness has a domestic constituency that her gift-catalogue scandal does not threaten.

Taiwan, the territory most exposed to any shift in American attention, responded with hardware. Parliament unanimously approved $9 billion in arms purchases, and the defence minister took pains to deny that Washington had asked Taipei to divert weapons to the Middle East—a denial whose very necessity illustrates the anxiety. TSMC's record revenues and deepening market dominance provide Taiwan with a form of deterrence no missile system can match: the world's dependence on its chips makes an interruption of its economy everybody's catastrophe. The opposition's decision to unite for the next elections suggests that even domestic rivals sense the island cannot afford internal division when external shelter is uncertain.

Australia and Indonesia, farther from the flashpoint, illustrate two poles of response to the same American rebalancing. Mr Albanese sent warplanes to the Gulf and endorsed strikes on Iran, binding Australia tighter to Washington at the cost of rising interest rates and Labour backbench dissent—a reminder that loyalty to the alliance has a price measured in mortgage payments as well as political capital. Mr Prabowo moved in the opposite direction, calling American strikes "irrational" and offering to mediate, language far sharper than Jakarta's usual non-aligned boilerplate. Yet his boldness abroad sits alongside the quiet intimidation of domestic critics and implausible claims of 300% returns from a sovereign wealth fund, suggesting that foreign-policy independence is easier to declare than democratic accountability is to practise.

The pattern is now plain enough to name. America's pivot to the Middle East has turned its Indo-Pacific alliances from a ceiling into a floor: a baseline each government must build above, using its own resources, diplomacy and, where possible, economic leverage. The allies are not breaking away, but they are no longer standing still. What holds them together is less shared conviction than shared uncertainty about what Washington will do next.

## frontline-eastern-europe

**When Allies Bicker, Adversaries Benefit**

The most striking feature of this week's events across central and eastern Europe is not the threat from Russia or Iran but the degree to which countries nominally on the same side are spending political capital fighting each other—or themselves. From Warsaw to Bucharest, from Kyiv to Prague, governments that should be marshalling resources for defence are instead locked in institutional trench warfare that saps their credibility and slows their preparations. The pattern suggests that the democratic messiness tolerable in peacetime is becoming a strategic liability in an era of live conflict and nuclear brinkmanship.

Poland offers the starkest case. President Karol Nawrocki's veto of the €43.7 billion SAFE defence loan—the country's largest allocation under the EU's €150 billion programme—was justified on sovereignty grounds, yet its practical effect is to deprive the Polish military of modernisation funds while Russia wages war next door. Mr Nawrocki then threatened to block six new Constitutional Tribunal judges, turning an institutional dispute into a second front against the Tusk government. The central bank governor's counter-proposal to finance defence from unrealised gold profits was rightly dismissed as fantasy. Poland is not short of enemies; it should not be manufacturing domestic ones at this pace.

Ukraine, the country with the most reason to keep friends close, chose this week to antagonise two of them. President Zelensky publicly rebuked American sanctions policy over a 30-day waiver on Russian oil, while his foreign minister accused Hungary of "state terrorism" after Hungarian authorities seized millions in cash and gold from Ukrainian bank couriers. Both complaints may be justified, but the timing—amid a parliamentary revolt in which some 40 ruling-party deputies are trying to resign—suggests a government reaching for external fights to distract from internal fragility. That Ukraine simultaneously dispatched drone specialists to Gulf states shows it retains diplomatic initiative; whether it can sustain that initiative while haemorrhaging legislative cohesion is another matter.

Finland's decision to lift restrictions on nuclear weapons from its territory represents the week's most consequential policy shift, yet even here the theme of allied friction applies. The move completes Finland's transformation from Nordic neutral to frontline NATO state, but Prime Minister Orpo's coalition is fraying over tax policy, with the Finns Party blocking inheritance-tax cuts. In Lithuania, the prime minister stumbled into an ethics scandal over an €8,000 family trip to the Vatican just as her party slid to fourth in polls, while her army commander freelanced publicly about offering bases for American strikes on Iran—a suggestion she had to disavow in evident surprise. Latvia's prime minister faced criticism from her own coalition partners for poor coordination. In each case the substance of defence policy is advancing—German troops deploying, missiles arriving, NATO facilities being built—while the political superstructure wobbles.

The Czech Republic and Romania round out the picture. Prague drew sharp American criticism for budgeting only 1.7% of GDP on defence in 2026, even as Czech-American trade hit $12.6 billion and Czech troops tested new fighting vehicles alongside allies. Romania approved American use of its airbases for Middle East operations, a significant commitment, but its governing coalition is fracturing over social spending, with the Social Democrats forming tactical alliances with the far-right to block appointments. In both countries the gap between military ambition and political discipline is widening.

What the week reveals is a structural problem, not a series of coincidences. Eastern Europe's democracies are rearming at historic speed, yet their domestic politics remain organised around peacetime incentives—vetoes deployed for partisan advantage, coalition agreements treated as optional, scandals allowed to consume oxygen that should go to strategy. Adversaries in Moscow and Tehran do not face equivalent friction costs. Until the region's leaders treat political cohesion as itself a defence asset, the weapons they are buying will arrive faster than the consensus needed to use them.

## near-east-south-asia

**The Price of Shelter**

The war with Iran is revealing which Middle Eastern and South Asian governments can absorb punishment and which are merely postponing its consequences. Across the region this week, states took hits to oil infrastructure, airspace and budgets, yet responded not with panic but with a flurry of deal-making, austerity and repression. The pattern looks like resilience. In fact it is something more ambiguous: a series of regimes using external crisis to accelerate pre-existing domestic agendas—centralising power, disciplining spending and silencing opposition—while hoping the international order holds together long enough for the bills to come due elsewhere.

Saudi Arabia is the clearest case of a state converting strategic vulnerability into purposeful adaptation. Aramco's 12% profit drop and its first-ever share buyback tell a story of genuine fiscal strain, yet Mohammed bin Salman has used the moment to do what peacetime politics made difficult: quietly shelving NEOM's most absurd ambitions in favour of data centres and industrial zones, while locking Pakistan into a mutual-defence pact that gives the kingdom military options beyond Washington. The $3.8 billion in fresh property investment pledged at a French fair suggests foreign capital still trusts the Saudi trajectory—or at least fears missing out on it. Mr bin Salman's domestic calendar, from arts universities to Vision 2030 anniversaries, proceeded without interruption, projecting a normality that is itself a political act.

Pakistan, by contrast, is discovering that crisis on multiple fronts leaves no room for pretence. Field Marshal Munir's government is simultaneously backing Riyadh, bombing Taliban positions in Kandahar and imposing austerity measures—school closures, salary cuts of up to 30%, a four-day working week—that no elected government could survive for long without serious unrest. The State Bank held its policy rate steady, but the decision to authorise emergency crude-import procedures for 60 days amounted to an admission that normal economic governance had been suspended. Imran Khan's party is already threatening a nationwide movement, sensing that war-driven hardship could do what courtroom battles could not. Pakistan is not adapting to crisis so much as being reshaped by it, with the military tightening its grip on both foreign and economic policy under the cover of emergency.

Turkey and India show how middle powers exploit the fog of regional war for domestic ends. In Ankara, Mr Erdoğan positioned himself as indispensable mediator—Mr Fidan shuttled between Iranian and Western interlocutors—while the judiciary opened the largest corruption trial in Turkish history, aimed squarely at eliminating the opposition's leadership. The central bank held rates at a punishing 37%, daring inflation to blink first. The message was unmistakable: orthodoxy at home, diplomacy abroad, repression where convenient. Mr Modi, for his part, turned a phone call with Iran's president into a tangible win—safe passage for LPG tankers—then weaponised the energy crisis against Congress on the campaign trail, calling opponents "puppets of anti-India forces." Emergency fuel regulations and ₹42,000 crore in infrastructure launches served the dual purpose of crisis management and electoral stagecraft ahead of state elections.

The UAE's week was the starkest illustration of the thesis. Iran struck the Ruwais refinery complex—922,000 barrels a day, the backbone of Abu Dhabi's downstream economy—and the Emirates responded not with escalation but with a blitz of institution-building: an Australian military deployment secured within days, diplomatic calls to a dozen capitals, a sovereign-wealth-fund real-estate venture with a French partner, and an AI-driven financial-surveillance award, all while the attorney-general arrested 25 people for spreading panic online. The government even folded Emirati Children's Day into its crisis messaging, telling families their children were safe. Every gesture carried a second meaning: reassurance doubled as control.

What the week reveals is not simply that war tests governments but that it offers them cover. Each of these states used Iranian aggression to justify measures—austerity, repression, military centralisation, diplomatic realignment—that would have drawn sharper scrutiny in peacetime. The danger is that the crisis becomes self-sustaining: regimes grow comfortable with emergency powers, populations grow accustomed to diminished expectations, and the question of whether the adaptation was wise gets deferred until the shooting stops. Resilience, in this region and this moment, is often just another word for opportunism.

## the-americas

**The Sovereignty Reflex**

The most revealing thing about this week in the Americas is not that governments clashed with Washington — that is old news — but that the clashes are now shaping domestic politics more than foreign policy. From Ottawa to Mexico City to Brasília, leaders are discovering that standing up to the United States wins votes, consolidates coalitions and justifies spending that might otherwise face scrutiny. Sovereignty has become less a principle than a political instrument, and the leaders wielding it most boldly are the ones with the most to gain at home.

Mark Carney's pledge to defend Greenland "however necessary" against American encroachment is the sharpest example. The promise is striking not for its geopolitical content — Canada lacks the military means to do much about Greenland on its own — but for what it does for Mr Carney domestically. His government sits two seats short of a majority, and his muscular posture on sovereignty has already lured a fourth opposition MP into the Liberal caucus, this time from the northern riding of Nunavut, where Arctic defence spending lands directly. The $35 billion announced for northern military bases sounds formidable until one notices that $32 billion was already earmarked under the previous government. The repackaging is the point: in a country rattled by tariffs and presidential insults, the appearance of defiance matters more than the substance of new expenditure.

Chile's new president, José Antonio Kast, demonstrates that the sovereignty reflex can work in the opposite direction. Rather than distancing himself from Washington, Mr Kast signed a minerals agreement with the United States on his first full day in office, framing alignment with American interests as an assertion of Chilean agency after years of left-wing governance. The deal on rare earths and supply chains is thin on detail, but its political purpose was unmistakable: it told both his base and his opponents that Chile's rightward shift would be fast and transactional. His approval ratings, above 50%, give him room to move. Yet the same week brought reminders of the institutional rot he inherits — a police officer shot dead responding to a public-drinking complaint, and plainclothes officers killing a man who mistook them for robbers — suggesting that governing will be harder than signalling.

Claudia Sheinbaum in Mexico and Lula da Silva in Brazil both face the less comfortable version of this dynamic: sovereignty rhetoric that buys time but cannot paper over domestic fractures. Ms Sheinbaum's retort to Donald Trump — that Mexico's sovereignty is "non-negotiable" — played well publicly, yet the same week her own coalition allies handed her a first legislative defeat by blocking electoral reform, the clearest crack in the ruling alliance since 2018. In Brazil, Mr Lula revoked the visa of a Trump adviser who tried to visit Jair Bolsonaro in prison, casting the move as a defence of national dignity. But polls show him tied at 41% with Flávio Bolsonaro in a hypothetical run-off, his approval rating sinking, and 19 of his 38 ministers about to leave the cabinet to run for office. The visa spat distracted nicely from a 38-centavo diesel price rise that required immediate subsidies to contain.

The pattern across the hemisphere is consistent enough to name plainly. External pressure from Washington — whether through tariffs, military posturing or diplomatic meddling — is being converted by incumbents and newcomers alike into domestic political capital. The conversion is often efficient: it rallies bases, justifies spending, and forces opponents into uncomfortable positions. But it is also depreciating. Each act of defiance must be louder than the last to register, while the underlying problems — fragile coalitions, polarised electorates, institutional decay, commodity-price shocks — remain stubbornly unresolved. Sovereignty is a renewable rhetorical resource; the political credit it buys is not.

## western-europe

# The Fraying Centre

**When democratic governments lose arguments at home and abroad simultaneously, they do not collapse—they hedge, and the hedging accelerates the very fragmentation they hope to contain.**

The clearest evidence comes from Britain, where Keir Starmer's government is being squeezed from every direction at once. The Green Party's capture of Gorton and Denton—a northern English seat Labour had held for generations—is not a protest vote but a realignment: the Greens took 41% while Labour slumped to third. Reports that several Labour MPs are in talks about defecting confirm the scale of the fracture. Yet Mr Starmer cannot pivot to shore up his base because the Iran crisis demands his attention abroad, where his refusal to let America use British bases for strikes provoked public rebuke from Mr Trump, only to be followed by a conciliatory phone call about reopening the Strait of Hormuz. The Bank of England, meanwhile, has shelved rate cuts as oil prices climb past $119. A prime minister who cannot hold his own seats, his closest ally, or his central bank's trajectory is not governing so much as arbitrating between crises.

Spain illustrates what happens when a leader chooses confrontation abroad to win peace at home—and finds it works, briefly. Pedro Sánchez's "No to War" stance earned praise from Hamas and the Houthis, an endorsement no NATO government would normally welcome, yet it also helped his Socialists beat expectations in the Castilla y León election. Mr Sánchez has discovered that defying Washington is domestically profitable. The trouble is that this posture cannot be sustained without cost: his own foreign minister skipped the king's trip to Chile over internal disagreements about how far to push the confrontation, and the government remains hostage to Catalan separatists who are blocking the regional budget over tax-collection powers. A foreign policy designed to rally the base does not fix the structural weakness beneath it.

Germany's Friedrich Merz faces the inverse problem: he wants to challenge Washington selectively while pretending his party has not quietly breached its own principles. His public criticism of America's decision to ease Russian oil sanctions—the sharpest yet from a German chancellor—positioned him as a European leader willing to dissent. But leaked European Parliament chats show his Christian Democrats cooperating with the Alternative for Germany on migration legislation, thanking AfD colleagues for "excellent cooperation" even as the CDU insists its firewall against the far right remains intact. In Baden-Württemberg, the AfD nearly doubled its vote despite corruption scandals, with 29% of its own voters acknowledging the graft and voting for the party regardless. Mr Merz's hedging—confronting America when convenient, accommodating the far right when useful—captures a broader pattern in which the centre borrows from the extremes it claims to oppose.

France and Italy demonstrate how external shocks expose coalitions that were already brittle. Emmanuel Macron lost a soldier in Iraq to an Iran-linked militia, called the attack "unacceptable," and immediately ruled out retaliation—a sequence that projects restraint to some and impotence to others. His left flank is disintegrating as Socialists threaten to break with Jean-Luc Mélenchon over antisemitism, while Jordan Bardella's National Rally uses municipal election victories to court centre-right alliances it was once denied. In Rome, Giorgia Meloni faces a referendum on her judicial reform that polls suggest she will lose, even as her two most senior coalition partners publicly disagree over whether to follow Mr Trump on Russian oil sanctions. Neither leader can resolve their domestic contradictions because the Iran crisis demands constant attention, and neither can resolve the Iran crisis because their domestic coalitions will not permit decisive action.

The Nordic countries offer the most disciplined version of this hedging. Norway's Jonas Gahr Støre hosted a summit that rejected American calls for military involvement in Iran while affirming NATO membership—threading a needle between defiance and loyalty. Sweden's Liberals quietly ended their refusal to govern alongside the Sweden Democrats, completing the far right's normalisation as a coalition partner. Even Sweden's central bank urged citizens to keep cash at home and reduce dependence on American payment networks. These are not revolutionary acts but careful recalibrations, each one shifting the boundary of what is acceptable just enough to manage the next crisis.

The pattern across western Europe this week is not ideological convergence but strategic convergence on evasion. Governments of left and right, in large countries and small, are discovering that they can neither satisfy their domestic bases nor meet their international commitments, and so they split the difference—hedging on alliances, hedging on the far right, hedging on economic pain. The result is not stability but the slow erosion of the commitments that once made hedging unnecessary. When every government is buying time, no government is setting direction.
