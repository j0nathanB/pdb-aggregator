**The Shield Slips**

The week's events across the Indo-Pacific reveal a single uncomfortable truth: American security guarantees, long treated as fixed architecture, are now visibly contingent on Washington's priorities elsewhere—and every capital in the region is scrambling to adjust, each in a way that exposes the contradictions of its own position.

South Korea offers the starkest proof. The removal of THAAD and Patriot batteries to the Middle East over Seoul's objections is not merely a logistical reshuffle; it is the first time America has stripped defence assets from the peninsula against its ally's wishes. Lee Jae-myung's admission that South Korea "cannot stop" the move laid bare the asymmetry that decades of alliance management had papered over. Mr Lee's response—fuel price caps, military pension pledges, an invitation to Mr Macron—amounts to a domestic anaesthetic and a diplomatic hedge, neither of which replaces a missile-defence system. His opposition, meanwhile, is too busy disowning an impeached predecessor to exploit the opening. Pyongyang, analysts note, may not be so distracted.

Japan drew the opposite lesson and acted first. Sanae Takaichi's unilateral release of oil reserves—before consulting G7 partners—and her deployment of long-range missiles covering the East China Sea signal a country that has decided waiting for alliance coordination is itself a risk. The symbolism of hosting Taiwan's premier for the first time in 52 years reinforces the point: Tokyo is building relationships that Washington has long discouraged, precisely because Washington can no longer be relied upon to hold every line. Ms Takaichi's approval rating dipped, but her boldness has a domestic constituency that her gift-catalogue scandal does not threaten.

Taiwan, the territory most exposed to any shift in American attention, responded with hardware. Parliament unanimously approved $9 billion in arms purchases, and the defence minister took pains to deny that Washington had asked Taipei to divert weapons to the Middle East—a denial whose very necessity illustrates the anxiety. TSMC's record revenues and deepening market dominance provide Taiwan with a form of deterrence no missile system can match: the world's dependence on its chips makes an interruption of its economy everybody's catastrophe. The opposition's decision to unite for the next elections suggests that even domestic rivals sense the island cannot afford internal division when external shelter is uncertain.

Australia and Indonesia, farther from the flashpoint, illustrate two poles of response to the same American rebalancing. Mr Albanese sent warplanes to the Gulf and endorsed strikes on Iran, binding Australia tighter to Washington at the cost of rising interest rates and Labour backbench dissent—a reminder that loyalty to the alliance has a price measured in mortgage payments as well as political capital. Mr Prabowo moved in the opposite direction, calling American strikes "irrational" and offering to mediate, language far sharper than Jakarta's usual non-aligned boilerplate. Yet his boldness abroad sits alongside the quiet intimidation of domestic critics and implausible claims of 300% returns from a sovereign wealth fund, suggesting that foreign-policy independence is easier to declare than democratic accountability is to practise.

The pattern is now plain enough to name. America's pivot to the Middle East has turned its Indo-Pacific alliances from a ceiling into a floor: a baseline each government must build above, using its own resources, diplomacy and, where possible, economic leverage. The allies are not breaking away, but they are no longer standing still. What holds them together is less shared conviction than shared uncertainty about what Washington will do next.
