**When the Missiles Fly, Autocrats Consolidate**

The week's events across the broader Middle East and South Asia share an uncomfortable truth: external military crises are proving more useful to incumbent strongmen than to their opponents. From Riyadh to Ankara to Islamabad, governments facing Iranian projectiles, domestic dissent, or American suspicion have seized on the emergency to centralise power, silence critics, or avoid hard choices — often all three at once.

Saudi Arabia's diplomatic rupture with Iran looks decisive, but it is also conveniently clarifying for Mohammed bin Salman. The crown prince expelled Iranian diplomats, invoked the spectre of military action, and reportedly urged Donald Trump to "keep hitting Iran hard" — all while his Public Investment Fund quietly closed a $6 billion gaming deal with ByteDance's Shanghai studio. The kingdom's air defences intercepted hundreds of drones and missiles with minimal damage, a record that bolsters the crown prince's claim to military competence. Yet the real dividend is political: a wartime footing leaves little oxygen for questions about the pace of Vision 2030 reforms or the kingdom's human-rights record. Prince Faisal's warning that Saudi patience "is not unlimited" is directed at Tehran, but it also serves notice to anyone at home tempted to second-guess the throne.

The UAE tells the same story in starker terms. Iran fired more than 1,900 projectiles in 18 days, slashing ADNOC output by half and shutting Fujairah port. Yet Sheikh Mohamed's government responded not with panic but with institution-building: a trillion-dirham central bank backstop, a 136-country coalition at the Security Council, and a dismantled Iranian intelligence network presented as proof of vigilance. That the Zayed National Museum opened on schedule was less a show of cultural ambition than a political statement — the ruling family can absorb punishment and still function. Abu Dhabi's deal-making continued uninterrupted, with ADNOC's $60 billion chemicals merger and a fresh $500 million commitment from the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority. Wars, in short, have not slowed the state's commercial machine; they have given it a patriotic gloss.

Turkey under Recep Tayyip Erdoğan demonstrates a subtler version of the pattern. The mass trial of Ekrem İmamoğlu and 401 co-defendants grinds on in Silivri, yet the government permits thousands to rally in the jailed mayor's support — a calibrated tolerance that lets Mr Erdoğan claim democratic legitimacy while keeping his most dangerous rival locked up. The opposition's counter-attack has shifted from the streets to the balance sheets: Özgür Özel's accusation that the justice minister accumulated 452 million lira in unexplained property wealth targets the judiciary itself. Mr Erdoğan, meanwhile, positions Turkey as a regional mediator, criticising Israel and courting both sides of the Gulf conflict. The crisis abroad provides cover for repression at home, and the protests he permits are the ones he has already defanged.

Pakistan offers the most extreme case. Having bombed a facility in Kabul that killed more than 400 people — an unprecedented escalation — Islamabad simultaneously fended off Saudi pressure to invoke a defence pact against Iran and absorbed Washington's new classification of it as a top-tier nuclear threat alongside China and Russia. Shehbaz Sharif's government used the Afghanistan conflict as its excuse to avoid the Gulf war, while Field Marshal Asim Munir inflamed the country's Shia minority by telling critics who "love Iran so much" to go there. Mr Sharif then froze fuel prices before Eid, absorbing 45 billion rupees in costs to buy public calm even as global oil surged past $158 a barrel. Each gesture — the bombing, the diplomacy, the populist price freeze — serves to justify a security state that keeps Imran Khan in prison and the army paramount.

The pattern is plain. When external threats are real and missiles are intercepted on camera, governments gain latitude to spend, repress, and restructure without the scrutiny that peacetime invites. The regimes in question are not manufacturing crises — Iran's attacks are genuine, Pakistan's border war is bloody, Turkey's judicial overreach is documented — but each has learned that an emergency is a terrible thing to waste. The consolidation now under way across the region will outlast the missiles.
