## asia-pacific

**The Price of Protection**

The week's events across the Indo-Pacific reveal a single, uncomfortable truth: American security guarantees are no longer free goods, and every government in the region is scrambling to decide what currency to pay in. From Tokyo's record investment pledge to Canberra's calls to wind down the Iran campaign, each capital is repricing the alliance in real time—not because the threat environment has eased, but because Washington under Donald Trump has made the transactional nature of protection impossible to ignore.

Japan's $550 billion investment commitment is the clearest receipt yet for an alliance that used to rest on shared values and quiet burden-sharing. Sanae Takaichi, the prime minister, travelled to Washington not with a military offer but with a chequebook, pledging semiconductor plants, small modular reactors and gas facilities across American swing states. The logic is frank: Japan's constitution and 2015 security legislation make combat contributions to a Hormuz operation legally treacherous, so Tokyo will pay its way through capital flows instead. Yet even as it bought goodwill, Japan quietly deployed upgraded Type-12 missiles capable of reaching China's coast—hedging against the possibility that no amount of investment will keep American carriers in the western Pacific forever.

Taiwan and South Korea are repricing from positions of greater vulnerability. Lai Ching-te's $38 billion "Shield of Taiwan" defence budget, his nuclear-restart trial balloon and his premier's baseball-diplomacy trip to Tokyo all point to an island trying to build independent deterrence while courting a secondary patron in Japan—moves that infuriate Beijing precisely because they imply doubt about Washington's reliability. In Seoul, Lee Jae-myung's 67% approval rating has given him the confidence to overhaul capital markets, restructure the prosecution and float troop deployments to Hormuz, the last of which his opposition backs explicitly as leverage for American nuclear submarines and trade concessions. Both governments treat security contributions as bargaining chips rather than obligations, a posture that would have been unthinkable a decade ago.

Australia shows what happens when the invoice arrives and the domestic constituency refuses to pay. Anthony Albanese is calling on America to end the Iran campaign, citing achieved objectives, but the real pressure is internal: a confrontation at a Sydney mosque that forced him to flee Eid prayers, a Reserve Bank rate rise blamed on Middle Eastern fuel costs and a One Nation surge in South Australia that signals voter exhaustion with foreign entanglements. Even an Iranian missile strike near an Australian air base in the UAE failed to rally public support for deeper involvement. Canberra is learning that the political cost of alliance loyalty now competes dollar-for-dollar with the economic cost of staying out.

Indonesia's Prabowo Subianto, meanwhile, demonstrates that a country outside the formal alliance network can exploit the repricing without bearing its burdens. His denunciation of the Iran campaign as "irrational," his refusal to pay into the Gaza reconstruction board and his populist Eid photo-ops in flood-hit Aceh all serve the same purpose: positioning Indonesia as a voice of the global south while conceding nothing material. That four military intelligence officers were arrested for attacking a human-rights activist the same week suggests Mr Prabowo understands he must offer just enough accountability to keep Western donors and domestic reformers quiet—another form of transactional politics, albeit pointed inward.

The pattern is not ideological divergence from America but contractual renegotiation with it. Each government—ally or not—is calculating what protection costs, what autonomy is worth and how little it can offer before the arrangement collapses. Alliances built on shared threat perception are giving way to alliances built on invoices, and nobody in the region is sure the arithmetic will hold.

## frontline-eastern-europe

**When the Front Line Is Everywhere**

The week across central and eastern Europe suggests that the continent's security perimeter is expanding faster than its politics can manage. Countries that a few years ago thought of defence as a NATO obligation confined to their own borders are now projecting force into the Middle East, deploying expertise to the Gulf and hosting American tankers for strikes on Iran — all while their domestic institutions fray under the weight of corruption, coalition feuds and populist subversion. The pattern is not merely one of rising threat; it is that external ambition and internal dysfunction are advancing in lockstep, and the contradiction may soon become unsustainable.

Romania illustrates the problem most starkly. Its Supreme Council of National Defence approved American use of airbases for operations against Iran, a commitment that goes well beyond hosting NATO's defensive architecture at Deveselu. KC-135 refuelling aircraft are now arriving at Mihail Kogălniceanu; Tehran has issued warnings. Yet at the very moment Romania takes on global military risk, its government is tearing itself apart. Marcel Ciolacu, the former prime minister, has launched personal attacks on his successor, Ilie Bolojan, calling him "an executioner" and "a satrap with a scythe." His Social Democrats plan to collapse the coalition through mass ministerial resignations. The country's main intelligence service has lacked a permanent civilian head for nearly two years. A state willing to host American bombers cannot find a spymaster.

Poland presents the same split in more dramatic form. Karol Nawrocki, the president, chose this week to visit Budapest in support of Viktor Orbán, aligning himself with Marine Le Pen and Matteo Salvini just as the Washington Post reported that Hungary's foreign minister regularly briefs his Russian counterpart on EU Council discussions. Mr Nawrocki's veto still blocks €132bn in EU defence funding — the bloc's largest allocation to any single country — and his refusal to swear in new constitutional court judges prolongs an institutional standoff that makes governance nearly impossible. Football fans across the country booed him; Donald Tusk, the prime minister, called the Budapest trip a "fatal mistake." Poland is simultaneously one of Europe's most hawkish states on Ukraine and one of the most politically paralysed.

Estonia and Finland show that even the more functional northern governments are stretching themselves thin. Tallinn offered to send troops to the Strait of Hormuz if Washington asked — a remarkable leap for a country of 1.3m people whose primary concern is a 294-kilometre border with Russia, underscored this week by yet another Russian Su-30 incursion into its airspace. Finland's prime minister, Petteri Orpo, pushed ahead with legislation enabling NATO nuclear planning on Finnish soil, overriding opposition calls for delay, while his coalition fractured over inheritance tax and an €800 bottle of champagne accepted by the finance minister, Riikka Purra. Both countries are building deeper ties with American defence contractors — Lockheed Martin is opening maintenance facilities across the Baltics — but their domestic coalitions are fragile vessels for such commitments.

Ukraine, the country all these efforts are meant to support, embodies the contradiction most painfully. It deployed 201 military experts to Gulf states to help counter Iranian drones, an audacious bid to make itself indispensable to partners beyond Europe. Yet Donald Trump dismissed the offer, calling Volodymyr Zelensky "the last person we need help from," even as Ukrainian anti-corruption prosecutors charged 41 sitting MPs and arrested senior security-service officials taking six-figure bribes. A state fighting for survival on the battlefield and seeking relevance on the global stage is simultaneously hollowed out by the venality of its own officials. Lithuania's governing Social Democrats, meanwhile, are in revolt against their own prime minister over her use of military aircraft for personal trips, leading a former foreign minister to lament that allies now see the country as "a state without a message."

The common thread is not merely that war in Ukraine and conflict in the Middle East have raised the stakes. It is that the political systems asked to bear these stakes — coalition governments, divided executives, weak oversight institutions — are buckling under the load. Defence budgets rise, American contractors arrive, airbases open, troops are offered to distant theatres; yet at home, presidents sabotage prime ministers, ethics commissions investigate champagne gifts, and intelligence agencies go unleaded. Europe's eastern flank is arming for a world of permanent confrontation while governing as though peace were still the default. That gap is the real vulnerability.

## near-east-south-asia

**When the Missiles Fly, Autocrats Consolidate**

The week's events across the broader Middle East and South Asia share an uncomfortable truth: external military crises are proving more useful to incumbent strongmen than to their opponents. From Riyadh to Ankara to Islamabad, governments facing Iranian projectiles, domestic dissent, or American suspicion have seized on the emergency to centralise power, silence critics, or avoid hard choices — often all three at once.

Saudi Arabia's diplomatic rupture with Iran looks decisive, but it is also conveniently clarifying for Mohammed bin Salman. The crown prince expelled Iranian diplomats, invoked the spectre of military action, and reportedly urged Donald Trump to "keep hitting Iran hard" — all while his Public Investment Fund quietly closed a $6 billion gaming deal with ByteDance's Shanghai studio. The kingdom's air defences intercepted hundreds of drones and missiles with minimal damage, a record that bolsters the crown prince's claim to military competence. Yet the real dividend is political: a wartime footing leaves little oxygen for questions about the pace of Vision 2030 reforms or the kingdom's human-rights record. Prince Faisal's warning that Saudi patience "is not unlimited" is directed at Tehran, but it also serves notice to anyone at home tempted to second-guess the throne.

The UAE tells the same story in starker terms. Iran fired more than 1,900 projectiles in 18 days, slashing ADNOC output by half and shutting Fujairah port. Yet Sheikh Mohamed's government responded not with panic but with institution-building: a trillion-dirham central bank backstop, a 136-country coalition at the Security Council, and a dismantled Iranian intelligence network presented as proof of vigilance. That the Zayed National Museum opened on schedule was less a show of cultural ambition than a political statement — the ruling family can absorb punishment and still function. Abu Dhabi's deal-making continued uninterrupted, with ADNOC's $60 billion chemicals merger and a fresh $500 million commitment from the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority. Wars, in short, have not slowed the state's commercial machine; they have given it a patriotic gloss.

Turkey under Recep Tayyip Erdoğan demonstrates a subtler version of the pattern. The mass trial of Ekrem İmamoğlu and 401 co-defendants grinds on in Silivri, yet the government permits thousands to rally in the jailed mayor's support — a calibrated tolerance that lets Mr Erdoğan claim democratic legitimacy while keeping his most dangerous rival locked up. The opposition's counter-attack has shifted from the streets to the balance sheets: Özgür Özel's accusation that the justice minister accumulated 452 million lira in unexplained property wealth targets the judiciary itself. Mr Erdoğan, meanwhile, positions Turkey as a regional mediator, criticising Israel and courting both sides of the Gulf conflict. The crisis abroad provides cover for repression at home, and the protests he permits are the ones he has already defanged.

Pakistan offers the most extreme case. Having bombed a facility in Kabul that killed more than 400 people — an unprecedented escalation — Islamabad simultaneously fended off Saudi pressure to invoke a defence pact against Iran and absorbed Washington's new classification of it as a top-tier nuclear threat alongside China and Russia. Shehbaz Sharif's government used the Afghanistan conflict as its excuse to avoid the Gulf war, while Field Marshal Asim Munir inflamed the country's Shia minority by telling critics who "love Iran so much" to go there. Mr Sharif then froze fuel prices before Eid, absorbing 45 billion rupees in costs to buy public calm even as global oil surged past $158 a barrel. Each gesture — the bombing, the diplomacy, the populist price freeze — serves to justify a security state that keeps Imran Khan in prison and the army paramount.

The pattern is plain. When external threats are real and missiles are intercepted on camera, governments gain latitude to spend, repress, and restructure without the scrutiny that peacetime invites. The regimes in question are not manufacturing crises — Iran's attacks are genuine, Pakistan's border war is bloody, Turkey's judicial overreach is documented — but each has learned that an emergency is a terrible thing to waste. The consolidation now under way across the region will outlast the missiles.

## the-americas

**The Strongman's Paradox**

The most revealing thing about the Americas this week is not that the right is advancing or the left retreating, but that governments of every stripe are discovering the same uncomfortable truth: bold executive action abroad is far easier than durable change at home. From Santiago to Brasília to Ottawa, leaders are projecting decisiveness outward while confronting the stubborn limits of their domestic authority. The pattern suggests that in an era of geopolitical flux, foreign-policy theatrics have become a substitute for the harder work of governing.

José Antonio Kast's five-metre border trenches are the week's most vivid case. Chile's new president has moved at startling speed—2,905 arrests in three days, corporate tax cuts from 27% to 23%, the scrapping of 43 environmental decrees—yet the very breadth of the offensive betrays its fragility. Thousands marched in 16 cities against the environmental rollbacks, and his approval ratings, though high at 57%, carry the highest disapproval of any new president since 2010. Mr Kast can build walls and appoint retired admirals, but his polarisation arithmetic depends on a divided opposition that may not stay divided. Washington's eagerness to negotiate access to Chile's rhenium supply, where the country controls roughly half the world's stock, gives him a foreign-policy prize that costs nothing domestically. The border barrier is a prop; the mineral deal is the real leverage, and it was handed to him by American need, not Chilean strength.

Mark Carney's 47 trips in 100 days make the same point from the opposite ideological direction. Canada's prime minister has turned diplomacy into a full-time job because the alternative—dependence on a hostile Washington—is untenable. That he and Pierre Poilievre, his Conservative rival, now text each other to coordinate abroad is less a sign of national unity than of shared desperation. At home, Mr Carney is accumulating power not through persuasion but through defections and favourable byelection maths, inching toward a majority he never won at the ballot box. His budget cuts $60 billion from science, tourism and foreign aid to fund defence—a reallocation that makes strategic sense but concedes that Canada's room for manoeuvre at home has narrowed even as its ambitions abroad have expanded.

Claudia Sheinbaum achieved the week's tidiest diplomatic result when King Felipe VI acknowledged Spanish colonial abuses, ending a freeze that began under her predecessor in 2019. The gesture cost Spain little and gave Mexico a symbolic triumph it can brandish alongside a pending EU trade deal and World Cup preparations. Yet Pemex, the state oil company, tells the domestic story more honestly: safety risks at its facilities have risen 117% across recent administrations, with nearly four-fifths of known problems past their repair deadlines. Mexico can stage a reconciliation with a European monarch and rehearse bomb-detection drills for a football tournament, but it cannot fix corroding pipelines.

Brazil's contest is the starkest illustration of the gap between political spectacle and governance. Lula flew to a CELAC summit in Bogotá attended by just five heads of state to deliver speeches on Latin American sovereignty, then announced the re-nationalisation of a refinery Mr Bolsonaro had privatised—gestures aimed at his base, not at economic logic. His real problem is closer to home: Flávio Bolsonaro's presidential campaign is gaining ground even in the northeast, traditionally hostile terrain for the Bolsonaro family, while the Workers' Party has been forced to narrow its fire onto a single opponent. That Brazil's two political camps have reached rough parity—one led by a president wielding Petrobras investments as campaign tools, the other by the convicted son of a prisoner in intensive care—says more about the exhaustion of both sides' domestic agendas than about either's strength.

The common thread is not ideology but evasion. Left or right, these leaders find it simpler to build fences, sign communiqués and rack up air miles than to repair refineries, balance budgets or hold together fractious coalitions. Foreign policy is where executives enjoy the widest discretion and the fewest legislative vetoes; it is also where results can be announced before they are delivered. When governing at home grows hard, the airport lounge beckons.

## western-europe

**The Hormuz Reflex**

America's war with Iran has become a loyalty test that Europe's governments are choosing, one after another, to fail — and the pattern reveals less about transatlantic solidarity than about a continent discovering that saying no to Washington is now cheaper than saying yes. Germany, Norway, Italy, Spain and Britain all declined or hedged on Donald Trump's demand for naval support in the Strait of Hormuz this week. What is striking is not the refusal itself but how little anguish accompanied it. From Berlin to Rome, leaders spoke not of agonising alliance obligations but of mandates they lacked, goals they found unclear, and sovereignty they meant to protect. The cumulative effect is a Europe that has quietly crossed a threshold: collective defiance of American military requests is no longer a crisis but a policy preference.

Friedrich Merz, Germany's chancellor, offered the most articulate version of this new posture. He rejected Mr Trump's call for naval help, complained that America "did not consult us before this war," and warned against "an eternal war with unclear goals." Yet the refusal was not isolationist; it was competitive. Mr Merz told the Bundestag that Europe's 450 million people should stop selling themselves "below value" and dispatched Boris Pistorius, his defence minister, to Tokyo, Singapore and Australia to cultivate military ties with middle powers — a diplomatic itinerary that reads as a blueprint for a world in which Washington is one partner among several rather than the indispensable one.

Britain illustrates what happens when a government lacks even that degree of strategic composure. Mr Trump called Keir Starmer "no Winston Churchill," shared comedy sketches mocking him, and questioned whether Britain remains "the Rolls-Royce of allies." Borrowing costs hit their highest since 2008, energy prices spiked, and Rachel Reeves, the chancellor, made the most explicit case yet for economic reintegration with Europe, conceding that Brexit caused "deep damage." Where Mr Merz's Germany rejected American demands from a position of deliberate self-assertion, Mr Starmer's Britain stumbled into estrangement with no evident plan for what comes next — scrambling between a Nigerian state visit, a wobbling monarch and a justice secretary proposing to abolish jury trials.

Italy and Spain showed that even governments ideologically sympathetic to Washington can resist its pull when the domestic cost is plain. Giorgia Meloni called Hormuz intervention "a step toward involvement" Italy wished to avoid and kept her navy confined to the Red Sea. At home she lost a constitutional referendum on judicial reform by seven points, proof that Italian voters will tolerate her nationalism but not her institutional overreach. In Madrid, Pedro Sánchez maintained his opposition to the Iran campaign while hosting Volodymyr Zelensky to demonstrate that European solidarity need not mean American obedience. His own coalition nearly fractured when Sumar ministers boycotted a cabinet meeting over his war-response spending package, relenting only after last-minute concessions on housing — a reminder that in Spain the real threat to governing is not Washington but the junior partner at the next desk.

Across Scandinavia the same reflex took distinct forms. Norway's rejection of the Hormuz request was serious enough to draw Mark Rutte, NATO's secretary-general, to Oslo on a damage-limitation visit; American officials were irked not merely by the refusal but by Oslo's habit of building Nordic-Canadian defence channels that bypass Washington. Sweden's Riksbank held rates steady, its governor citing "major uncertainty" from the Middle East, even as the Liberal party sealed a historic cooperation pact with the Sweden Democrats — a domestic realignment that may matter more for European politics than any single act of alliance defiance. In both countries the message was identical: the Iran war is America's problem, and Europe's task is to insulate itself from the fallout.

The pattern is now unmistakable. Europe's governments are not breaking with the United States out of principle or even conviction; they are doing so because the incentive structure has changed. When American wars no longer come with consultation, shared objectives or economic upside, alliance loyalty becomes a cost with no offsetting benefit. What emerged this week is not a coordinated European front but something potentially more durable: a series of independent calculations, made in Berlin, London, Rome, Madrid, Oslo and Stockholm, all arriving at the same answer. The transatlantic bargain, in which Europe deferred to American military leadership in exchange for security guarantees and diplomatic weight, has not been formally repudiated. It has simply stopped paying.
