**When the Front Line Is Everywhere**

The week across central and eastern Europe suggests that the continent's security perimeter is expanding faster than its politics can manage. Countries that a few years ago thought of defence as a NATO obligation confined to their own borders are now projecting force into the Middle East, deploying expertise to the Gulf and hosting American tankers for strikes on Iran — all while their domestic institutions fray under the weight of corruption, coalition feuds and populist subversion. The pattern is not merely one of rising threat; it is that external ambition and internal dysfunction are advancing in lockstep, and the contradiction may soon become unsustainable.

Romania illustrates the problem most starkly. Its Supreme Council of National Defence approved American use of airbases for operations against Iran, a commitment that goes well beyond hosting NATO's defensive architecture at Deveselu. KC-135 refuelling aircraft are now arriving at Mihail Kogălniceanu; Tehran has issued warnings. Yet at the very moment Romania takes on global military risk, its government is tearing itself apart. Marcel Ciolacu, the former prime minister, has launched personal attacks on his successor, Ilie Bolojan, calling him "an executioner" and "a satrap with a scythe." His Social Democrats plan to collapse the coalition through mass ministerial resignations. The country's main intelligence service has lacked a permanent civilian head for nearly two years. A state willing to host American bombers cannot find a spymaster.

Poland presents the same split in more dramatic form. Karol Nawrocki, the president, chose this week to visit Budapest in support of Viktor Orbán, aligning himself with Marine Le Pen and Matteo Salvini just as the Washington Post reported that Hungary's foreign minister regularly briefs his Russian counterpart on EU Council discussions. Mr Nawrocki's veto still blocks €132bn in EU defence funding — the bloc's largest allocation to any single country — and his refusal to swear in new constitutional court judges prolongs an institutional standoff that makes governance nearly impossible. Football fans across the country booed him; Donald Tusk, the prime minister, called the Budapest trip a "fatal mistake." Poland is simultaneously one of Europe's most hawkish states on Ukraine and one of the most politically paralysed.

Estonia and Finland show that even the more functional northern governments are stretching themselves thin. Tallinn offered to send troops to the Strait of Hormuz if Washington asked — a remarkable leap for a country of 1.3m people whose primary concern is a 294-kilometre border with Russia, underscored this week by yet another Russian Su-30 incursion into its airspace. Finland's prime minister, Petteri Orpo, pushed ahead with legislation enabling NATO nuclear planning on Finnish soil, overriding opposition calls for delay, while his coalition fractured over inheritance tax and an €800 bottle of champagne accepted by the finance minister, Riikka Purra. Both countries are building deeper ties with American defence contractors — Lockheed Martin is opening maintenance facilities across the Baltics — but their domestic coalitions are fragile vessels for such commitments.

Ukraine, the country all these efforts are meant to support, embodies the contradiction most painfully. It deployed 201 military experts to Gulf states to help counter Iranian drones, an audacious bid to make itself indispensable to partners beyond Europe. Yet Donald Trump dismissed the offer, calling Volodymyr Zelensky "the last person we need help from," even as Ukrainian anti-corruption prosecutors charged 41 sitting MPs and arrested senior security-service officials taking six-figure bribes. A state fighting for survival on the battlefield and seeking relevance on the global stage is simultaneously hollowed out by the venality of its own officials. Lithuania's governing Social Democrats, meanwhile, are in revolt against their own prime minister over her use of military aircraft for personal trips, leading a former foreign minister to lament that allies now see the country as "a state without a message."

The common thread is not merely that war in Ukraine and conflict in the Middle East have raised the stakes. It is that the political systems asked to bear these stakes — coalition governments, divided executives, weak oversight institutions — are buckling under the load. Defence budgets rise, American contractors arrive, airbases open, troops are offered to distant theatres; yet at home, presidents sabotage prime ministers, ethics commissions investigate champagne gifts, and intelligence agencies go unleaded. Europe's eastern flank is arming for a world of permanent confrontation while governing as though peace were still the default. That gap is the real vulnerability.
