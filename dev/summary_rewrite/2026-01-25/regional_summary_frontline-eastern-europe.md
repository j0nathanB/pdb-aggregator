**When Allies Become the Problem**

The week's events across eastern Europe and the Nordic states reveal a single uncomfortable truth: America's European allies now spend as much diplomatic energy managing Washington as they do confronting Moscow. From Davos to Abu Dhabi to Brussels, governments that have staked their security on the transatlantic bond found themselves scrambling not against Russian aggression but against American unpredictability — and discovering that their own domestic fractures make the task harder still.

Ukraine's recalibration is the starkest case. President Zelensky's blistering speech at Davos, calling Europe "fragmented" and "lost," was not mere frustration but a strategic pivot: having concluded that Brussels cannot deliver, he is betting on American-mediated talks with Russia, dispatching his intelligence chief and defence minister to Abu Dhabi for trilateral negotiations. Yet even as Kyiv courts Mr Trump's favour, it is hedging ruthlessly. The appointment of Kyrylo Budanov — the spymaster trusted by more Ukrainians than Mr Zelensky himself — to run the President's Office looks less like a routine reshuffle than the opening move in a succession plan. A wartime leader who simultaneously escalates offensives, negotiates through his former adversary's patron, and installs a potential heir is a leader who knows the ground beneath him is shifting.

Poland illustrates how cohabitation turns allied management into farce. President Nawrocki secured an invitation to Mr Trump's Peace Council and reassurance that American troops would stay; days later Mr Trump dishonoured 44 Polish soldiers killed in Afghanistan. The ensuing public brawl between Mr Nawrocki and Prime Minister Tusk — each accusing the other of either kneeling to or alienating Washington — did more damage to Poland's credibility than Mr Trump's original slight. That the country's central bank is meanwhile hoarding gold at a record pace, aiming for 700 tonnes, hints at a quiet institutional bet that neither alliance nor currency union can be wholly relied upon. Polling suggests the coalition's junior partners may not survive the next election, meaning the very government trying to straddle Washington and Brussels could soon be replaced by one less inclined to straddle at all.

The smaller allies faced the sharpest version of the dilemma. When Mr Trump threatened tariffs over Greenland, Estonia — which spends 5% of GDP on defence precisely because it depends on American protection — sided publicly with Denmark, confirmed participation in Arctic military drills Mr Trump had warned against, and yet simultaneously rejected any European defence structure that might dilute NATO. The message was principled but precarious: defy America on sovereignty, defer to it on security architecture, and hope the contradiction holds. Finland performed a similar balancing act, with Prime Minister Orpo extending a meeting with Xi Jinping in Beijing while coordinating EU countermeasures against American tariffs, and President Stubb joining the eight-nation statement defending Greenland — all while Brussels placed Helsinki under formal deficit procedure partly because its F-35 purchases blew through fiscal limits. The cost of being a good ally, it turns out, can put you in breach of being a good European.

Domestic politics compound every external challenge. In the Czech Republic, the speaker of parliament — a far-right figure holding the country's third-highest office — manipulated headlines to inflame anti-Ukrainian sentiment and called a Ukrainian flag on Charles Bridge a "provocation," prompting the Senate to pass an unprecedented resolution of rebuke. In Romania, the far-right AUR leader cut a Greenland-shaped cake at a Washington reception, triggering outrage that only confirmed his party's 35% polling lead. Latvia's coalition fractured again over compensation legislation, its 17th prime-ministerial crisis in 33 years of independence, even as it tried to accelerate Rail Baltica and deepen military ties with Poland. Lithuania's president hedged on joining Mr Trump's Peace Council while fending off a European Parliament resolution condemning his government's moves against the public broadcaster. In each case, leaders committed to Western integration found their room for manoeuvre narrowed not by enemies but by coalition partners, constitutional rivals, or populist challengers at home.

The pattern is now plain. Eastern Europe's security-first states built their post-Cold War order on the assumption that American power and European institutions would pull in the same direction. That assumption has broken down. Governments must now play Washington against Brussels, hedge military commitments with gold reserves and arms-industry IPOs, and manage domestic populists who see in American disruption not a threat but an opportunity. The alliance still holds, but its members are learning that dependence on a protector who demands loyalty without offering predictability is not partnership — it is exposure.
