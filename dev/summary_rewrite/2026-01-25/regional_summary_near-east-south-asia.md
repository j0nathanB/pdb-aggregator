# The Alignment Bazaar

**The week's most telling pattern across the Middle East and South Asia is not that governments are choosing sides but that they are collecting partnerships like currencies, hedging against a world in which no single alliance is worth trusting on its own.** From Ankara to Riyadh to Abu Dhabi to New Delhi, leaders struck deals that would have seemed contradictory a decade ago — and did so simultaneously, in plain sight, with no apparent embarrassment. The common thread is not multi-alignment as ideology but multi-alignment as insurance: every government is quietly pricing in the possibility that any one patron may prove unreliable.

Turkey illustrates the logic most vividly. President Erdoğan accepted a seat on Mr Trump's Gaza Peace Council, the warmest American embrace Ankara has received in years, while his foreign minister warned of military operations in Syria if Kurdish negotiations falter. At home, Mr Bahçeli revealed that Abdullah Öcalan, the imprisoned leader of the Kurdistan Workers' Party, had sent him a handwoven carpet — an almost surreal token of back-channel depth between lifelong enemies. Yet this same peace process nearly derailed when a Turkish flag was torn down at the Nusaybin border crossing, provoking an emergency coalition meeting. Mr Erdoğan is running an elaborate arbitrage: deepening American cooperation to bolster regional leverage, dangling Kurdish peace to unlock constitutional changes that would extend his rule, and eliminating domestic rivals — Istanbul's mayor, Ekrem İmamoğlu, was constitutionally barred from the presidency by a court ruling on his diploma — to ensure no alternative centre of power survives. The strategy's weakness is that every moving part depends on every other.

Saudi Arabia's calculation is blunter. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman secured Major Non-NATO Ally status from Washington, a legal designation that only 20 countries hold, while simultaneously pursuing a trilateral defence pact with Turkey and Pakistan that some have labelled a "Muslim NATO." The kingdom signed the founding charter for Mr Trump's Gaza board at Davos even as it scaled back the Neom megaproject, conceding fiscal reality after years of grandiose ambition. The juxtaposition is instructive: Riyadh is willing to pay more for American security guarantees precisely because it can no longer afford to assume that oil wealth alone will buy strategic autonomy. Aramco's chief dismissed forecasts of oversupply as exaggerated, but the decision to shrink The Line speaks louder than any Davos panel.

The UAE, characteristically, pursued the most elaborate hedging of all. President Mohammed bin Zayed visited New Delhi to sign a defence partnership and a $3 billion LNG deal, then hosted the first trilateral Russia-Ukraine-American peace talks in Abu Dhabi, then joined the same Trump-backed Gaza board as its Saudi rival. G42, the Emirati AI champion, unveiled a "digital sovereignty" framework plainly designed to reassure Western partners still nervous about earlier Chinese entanglements — while targeting a 5-gigawatt AI campus that would make Abu Dhabi indispensable to the American tech stack. Each move reinforced the others: mediation credentials burnish the investment pitch, which funds the defence relationships, which underwrite the mediation. The circle works only so long as no patron demands exclusivity.

India, for its part, showed that the strategy is not confined to the Gulf. External Affairs Minister Jaishankar publicly rebuked Poland for engaging Pakistan and criticised selective American tariffs, while welcoming Mr bin Zayed with a pledge to double bilateral trade to $200 billion. More quietly, the Reserve Bank of India proposed linking BRICS digital currencies for cross-border payments — an institutional step toward reducing dollar dependence that Washington would once have treated as provocation. Mr Adani's conglomerate lost $12.5 billion in market capitalisation after the SEC sought to serve him legal papers by email, yet pressed ahead with a Brazilian aircraft-assembly deal and a full takeover of the IANS news agency, as though American legal risk were simply another cost of doing business in multiple jurisdictions at once.

What unites these manoeuvres is a shared, if unspoken, premise: that the post-American order has not yet arrived, but the exclusively American one is already gone. Each government is therefore building redundancy into its alliances — collecting security guarantees, trade corridors and mediating roles the way a prudent investor diversifies a portfolio. The danger is the same one that faces any over-leveraged portfolio: when a crisis forces a binary choice, optionality evaporates. The pattern this week reveals is not strategic genius but strategic anxiety — a region busily insuring itself against a future it suspects no single partnership can secure.
