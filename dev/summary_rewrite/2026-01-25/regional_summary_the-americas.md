# The Sovereignty Shuffle

**When middle powers assert independence from Washington, they end up proving how little room they have to manoeuvre.**

Mark Carney's declaration at Davos that "the old order is dead" was the week's most quotable line, but the Canadian prime minister's subsequent retreat told a truer story. Within days of announcing trade deals with China — including a quota for 49,000 Chinese electric vehicles at a reduced tariff — Mr Carney was forced to clarify, under threat of 100% American tariffs, that no comprehensive free-trade agreement was planned. The episode distilled a pattern visible across the Americas: leaders are loudly staking out sovereign positions against the United States, only to find that the constraints binding them to Washington are structural, not rhetorical. Even Mr Carney's domestic reward was ambiguous. Pierre Poilievre, the Conservative leader, praised the Davos speech and offered bipartisan cooperation — a rare gift — yet Quebec sovereigntists seized on Mr Carney's clumsy historical gloss on the Plains of Abraham to mount the fiercest federal-provincial backlash in years. External defiance, it turns out, does not buy internal cohesion; it can sharpen old fractures instead.

Brazil's President Lula da Silva tested the same thesis with more theatrical flair and no less contradiction. His New York Times op-ed denouncing the American capture of Venezuela's Nicolás Maduro as the first direct military attack on South America in two centuries was followed, on the same day, by a 45-minute call with Xi Jinping to coordinate what Brasília calls Global South resistance. Visa exemptions for Chinese citizens added a material flourish. Yet Mr Lula cannot escape Washington's gravitational pull either. Mr Trump's invitation to join a Gaza peace council was rightly read in Brasília as a trap: accept and become a subordinate, decline and confirm misalignment. Mr Lula chose grandiloquence — "Brazil will not submit to hegemonic projects" — while keeping dialogue open with Washington, a formula whose shelf life depends entirely on American patience. At home, the 2026 election is already sharpening. Mr Lula signalled he will run again with his vice-president, but polling shows Senator Flávio Bolsonaro beating him in a hypothetical run-off, and a rally of 18,000 for the imprisoned former president demonstrated that Bolsonarismo retains real mobilisation power. Sovereignty talk abroad may yet prove a weak substitute for economic results at home.

Mexico offers the clearest illustration of how performative sovereignty coexists with operational dependence. President Claudia Sheinbaum banned American military aircraft from landing at civilian airports, a move calibrated to look firm on television. Days later her security secretary met the new FBI director and promptly handed over 37 criminals to American custody, bringing the total transferred to 92 — with the careful caveat that none would face the death penalty. Ms Sheinbaum's approval rating sits at nearly 70%, proof that most Mexicans accept the balancing act. But her governing coalition is creaking: allied parties have stalled electoral reform because they were not consulted, and Morena lacks the votes for constitutional change on its own. Sovereignty gestures are easier than coalition management.

Chile's incoming president, José Antonio Kast, is attempting a different kind of independence — from his own coalition. His cabinet of 24 ministers includes 16 independents, the most technocratic line-up in Chilean democratic memory. Mr Kast sold this as "emergency governance," free of party quotas. Coalition partners, given almost no input, were not charmed. The gamble only makes sense if Mr Kast can bypass Congress through sheer executive competence, yet he enters office with a weak legislative position, a predecessor still facing a reopened embezzlement case, and deadly forest fires testing state capacity in the south. His early foreign-policy moves — studying the Dominican Republic's border-fence model, deploying a naval vessel to Antarctica — signal a presidency built on security and sovereignty claims. Whether technocracy without a legislative majority amounts to strength or isolation remains to be seen.

What unites these cases is not merely posture but paradox. Each leader is leveraging sovereignty rhetoric to consolidate domestic standing in an era when American unpredictability makes such rhetoric both more appealing and more dangerous. Mr Carney, Mr Lula, Ms Sheinbaum and Mr Kast all discovered the same thing this week: the louder you proclaim independence, the more visible become the constraints — economic, institutional, coalitional — that make true independence impossible. Middle powers across the Americas are performing sovereignty rather than practising it, and the audience they most need to convince is at home.
