## asia-pacific

**When Washington Steps Back, Asia's Leaders Leap — but Not Always in the Right Direction**

The week's events across the Asia-Pacific share a single, uncomfortable truth: America's allies are discovering that strategic self-reliance is easier to demand than to execute well. From Tokyo to Canberra, governments are scrambling to fill the vacuum left by a Pentagon that now explicitly asks partners to shoulder more of their own defence and economic burdens. Yet in each case the scramble is exposing domestic fractures, institutional risks, and political contradictions that American predominance once papered over.

Japan's Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has grasped this logic most clearly — and most ruthlessly. By dissolving parliament for snap elections while her approval ratings are high, she is betting that voters will reward a leader who matches American retrenchment with Japanese assertiveness: ¥39 billion for rare-earth independence from China, a ¥200 trillion public-private investment target by 2040, and continued interest-rate normalisation even as fiscal spending balloons. The gamble is coherent but precarious. Ms Takaichi is simultaneously loosening the purse strings and tightening monetary policy, counting on wage growth to square the circle. Her opposition, a hastily assembled Centrist Reform Alliance of 172 lower-house members, looks more like a marriage of desperation than conviction — which is itself a sign of how thoroughly one leader has seized the initiative.

Taiwan's semiconductor leverage has purchased it something no other American partner obtained this week: a concrete bargain. The 15% tariff rate it secured — matching Japan, South Korea and the EU — cost up to $500 billion in investment commitments, including an accelerated expansion of TSMC's Arizona plants. President Lai Ching-te can plausibly claim that Taiwan traded from strength; less than 15% of TSMC's advanced processes are expected to migrate to America by 2029, and the TAIEX hit a record high on the news. Yet the deal's domestic politics are poisonous. The opposition blocked the island's $39.55 billion special defence budget for an eighth consecutive time, even as Mr Lai promises European visitors that military spending will reach 5% of GDP by 2030. Taiwan has found the price of American partnership but cannot yet persuade its own legislature to pay it.

Indonesia and South Korea illustrate the hazards of self-reliance pursued without institutional discipline. President Prabowo Subianto returned from Davos boasting of "Prabowonomics" and 5% growth, then promptly nominated his nephew for a deputy governorship at Bank Indonesia — a move that helped push the rupiah to record lows and sent foreign investors fleeing from blue-chip bank stocks. In Seoul, President Lee Jae-myung's Kospi breached 5,000 even as GDP contracted 0.3% in the fourth quarter, a split-screen image of chaebol exuberance and household fragility. The Pentagon's new strategy tells South Korea to take "primary responsibility" for deterring the North; Seoul's defence ministry immediately objected, revealing a gap between American expectations and Korean readiness that neither side has a plan to close. Meanwhile, a former prime minister was sentenced to 23 years for his role in December 2024's martial-law debacle — a reminder that South Korea's democratic resilience is real but hard-won.

Australia proves that even a country far from the Chinese mainland is not immune to the centrifugal forces the new American posture accelerates. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese's net approval has cratered from plus 6 to minus 22 after a muddled response to the Bondi Beach massacre, and the conservative opposition has fractured so badly that its entire Nationals frontbench threatened to resign over hate-speech legislation. Mr Albanese's cautious hedge on Mr Trump's proposed Gaza "Board of Peace" — he would "give it further consideration" — captures in miniature the broader regional mood: leaders who once reflexively followed Washington now hesitate, not from strength but from the sheer complexity of deciding what to do on their own.

The pattern is plain. American retrenchment has not produced a disciplined, self-organising Asia but rather a continent of improvisation — bold in ambition, brittle in execution, and increasingly reliant on individual leaders whose domestic coalitions are thinner than they look.

## frontline-eastern-europe

**When Allies Become the Problem**

The week's events across eastern Europe and the Nordic states reveal a single uncomfortable truth: America's European allies now spend as much diplomatic energy managing Washington as they do confronting Moscow. From Davos to Abu Dhabi to Brussels, governments that have staked their security on the transatlantic bond found themselves scrambling not against Russian aggression but against American unpredictability — and discovering that their own domestic fractures make the task harder still.

Ukraine's recalibration is the starkest case. President Zelensky's blistering speech at Davos, calling Europe "fragmented" and "lost," was not mere frustration but a strategic pivot: having concluded that Brussels cannot deliver, he is betting on American-mediated talks with Russia, dispatching his intelligence chief and defence minister to Abu Dhabi for trilateral negotiations. Yet even as Kyiv courts Mr Trump's favour, it is hedging ruthlessly. The appointment of Kyrylo Budanov — the spymaster trusted by more Ukrainians than Mr Zelensky himself — to run the President's Office looks less like a routine reshuffle than the opening move in a succession plan. A wartime leader who simultaneously escalates offensives, negotiates through his former adversary's patron, and installs a potential heir is a leader who knows the ground beneath him is shifting.

Poland illustrates how cohabitation turns allied management into farce. President Nawrocki secured an invitation to Mr Trump's Peace Council and reassurance that American troops would stay; days later Mr Trump dishonoured 44 Polish soldiers killed in Afghanistan. The ensuing public brawl between Mr Nawrocki and Prime Minister Tusk — each accusing the other of either kneeling to or alienating Washington — did more damage to Poland's credibility than Mr Trump's original slight. That the country's central bank is meanwhile hoarding gold at a record pace, aiming for 700 tonnes, hints at a quiet institutional bet that neither alliance nor currency union can be wholly relied upon. Polling suggests the coalition's junior partners may not survive the next election, meaning the very government trying to straddle Washington and Brussels could soon be replaced by one less inclined to straddle at all.

The smaller allies faced the sharpest version of the dilemma. When Mr Trump threatened tariffs over Greenland, Estonia — which spends 5% of GDP on defence precisely because it depends on American protection — sided publicly with Denmark, confirmed participation in Arctic military drills Mr Trump had warned against, and yet simultaneously rejected any European defence structure that might dilute NATO. The message was principled but precarious: defy America on sovereignty, defer to it on security architecture, and hope the contradiction holds. Finland performed a similar balancing act, with Prime Minister Orpo extending a meeting with Xi Jinping in Beijing while coordinating EU countermeasures against American tariffs, and President Stubb joining the eight-nation statement defending Greenland — all while Brussels placed Helsinki under formal deficit procedure partly because its F-35 purchases blew through fiscal limits. The cost of being a good ally, it turns out, can put you in breach of being a good European.

Domestic politics compound every external challenge. In the Czech Republic, the speaker of parliament — a far-right figure holding the country's third-highest office — manipulated headlines to inflame anti-Ukrainian sentiment and called a Ukrainian flag on Charles Bridge a "provocation," prompting the Senate to pass an unprecedented resolution of rebuke. In Romania, the far-right AUR leader cut a Greenland-shaped cake at a Washington reception, triggering outrage that only confirmed his party's 35% polling lead. Latvia's coalition fractured again over compensation legislation, its 17th prime-ministerial crisis in 33 years of independence, even as it tried to accelerate Rail Baltica and deepen military ties with Poland. Lithuania's president hedged on joining Mr Trump's Peace Council while fending off a European Parliament resolution condemning his government's moves against the public broadcaster. In each case, leaders committed to Western integration found their room for manoeuvre narrowed not by enemies but by coalition partners, constitutional rivals, or populist challengers at home.

The pattern is now plain. Eastern Europe's security-first states built their post-Cold War order on the assumption that American power and European institutions would pull in the same direction. That assumption has broken down. Governments must now play Washington against Brussels, hedge military commitments with gold reserves and arms-industry IPOs, and manage domestic populists who see in American disruption not a threat but an opportunity. The alliance still holds, but its members are learning that dependence on a protector who demands loyalty without offering predictability is not partnership — it is exposure.

## near-east-south-asia

# The Alignment Bazaar

**The week's most telling pattern across the Middle East and South Asia is not that governments are choosing sides but that they are collecting partnerships like currencies, hedging against a world in which no single alliance is worth trusting on its own.** From Ankara to Riyadh to Abu Dhabi to New Delhi, leaders struck deals that would have seemed contradictory a decade ago — and did so simultaneously, in plain sight, with no apparent embarrassment. The common thread is not multi-alignment as ideology but multi-alignment as insurance: every government is quietly pricing in the possibility that any one patron may prove unreliable.

Turkey illustrates the logic most vividly. President Erdoğan accepted a seat on Mr Trump's Gaza Peace Council, the warmest American embrace Ankara has received in years, while his foreign minister warned of military operations in Syria if Kurdish negotiations falter. At home, Mr Bahçeli revealed that Abdullah Öcalan, the imprisoned leader of the Kurdistan Workers' Party, had sent him a handwoven carpet — an almost surreal token of back-channel depth between lifelong enemies. Yet this same peace process nearly derailed when a Turkish flag was torn down at the Nusaybin border crossing, provoking an emergency coalition meeting. Mr Erdoğan is running an elaborate arbitrage: deepening American cooperation to bolster regional leverage, dangling Kurdish peace to unlock constitutional changes that would extend his rule, and eliminating domestic rivals — Istanbul's mayor, Ekrem İmamoğlu, was constitutionally barred from the presidency by a court ruling on his diploma — to ensure no alternative centre of power survives. The strategy's weakness is that every moving part depends on every other.

Saudi Arabia's calculation is blunter. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman secured Major Non-NATO Ally status from Washington, a legal designation that only 20 countries hold, while simultaneously pursuing a trilateral defence pact with Turkey and Pakistan that some have labelled a "Muslim NATO." The kingdom signed the founding charter for Mr Trump's Gaza board at Davos even as it scaled back the Neom megaproject, conceding fiscal reality after years of grandiose ambition. The juxtaposition is instructive: Riyadh is willing to pay more for American security guarantees precisely because it can no longer afford to assume that oil wealth alone will buy strategic autonomy. Aramco's chief dismissed forecasts of oversupply as exaggerated, but the decision to shrink The Line speaks louder than any Davos panel.

The UAE, characteristically, pursued the most elaborate hedging of all. President Mohammed bin Zayed visited New Delhi to sign a defence partnership and a $3 billion LNG deal, then hosted the first trilateral Russia-Ukraine-American peace talks in Abu Dhabi, then joined the same Trump-backed Gaza board as its Saudi rival. G42, the Emirati AI champion, unveiled a "digital sovereignty" framework plainly designed to reassure Western partners still nervous about earlier Chinese entanglements — while targeting a 5-gigawatt AI campus that would make Abu Dhabi indispensable to the American tech stack. Each move reinforced the others: mediation credentials burnish the investment pitch, which funds the defence relationships, which underwrite the mediation. The circle works only so long as no patron demands exclusivity.

India, for its part, showed that the strategy is not confined to the Gulf. External Affairs Minister Jaishankar publicly rebuked Poland for engaging Pakistan and criticised selective American tariffs, while welcoming Mr bin Zayed with a pledge to double bilateral trade to $200 billion. More quietly, the Reserve Bank of India proposed linking BRICS digital currencies for cross-border payments — an institutional step toward reducing dollar dependence that Washington would once have treated as provocation. Mr Adani's conglomerate lost $12.5 billion in market capitalisation after the SEC sought to serve him legal papers by email, yet pressed ahead with a Brazilian aircraft-assembly deal and a full takeover of the IANS news agency, as though American legal risk were simply another cost of doing business in multiple jurisdictions at once.

What unites these manoeuvres is a shared, if unspoken, premise: that the post-American order has not yet arrived, but the exclusively American one is already gone. Each government is therefore building redundancy into its alliances — collecting security guarantees, trade corridors and mediating roles the way a prudent investor diversifies a portfolio. The danger is the same one that faces any over-leveraged portfolio: when a crisis forces a binary choice, optionality evaporates. The pattern this week reveals is not strategic genius but strategic anxiety — a region busily insuring itself against a future it suspects no single partnership can secure.

## the-americas

# The Sovereignty Shuffle

**When middle powers assert independence from Washington, they end up proving how little room they have to manoeuvre.**

Mark Carney's declaration at Davos that "the old order is dead" was the week's most quotable line, but the Canadian prime minister's subsequent retreat told a truer story. Within days of announcing trade deals with China — including a quota for 49,000 Chinese electric vehicles at a reduced tariff — Mr Carney was forced to clarify, under threat of 100% American tariffs, that no comprehensive free-trade agreement was planned. The episode distilled a pattern visible across the Americas: leaders are loudly staking out sovereign positions against the United States, only to find that the constraints binding them to Washington are structural, not rhetorical. Even Mr Carney's domestic reward was ambiguous. Pierre Poilievre, the Conservative leader, praised the Davos speech and offered bipartisan cooperation — a rare gift — yet Quebec sovereigntists seized on Mr Carney's clumsy historical gloss on the Plains of Abraham to mount the fiercest federal-provincial backlash in years. External defiance, it turns out, does not buy internal cohesion; it can sharpen old fractures instead.

Brazil's President Lula da Silva tested the same thesis with more theatrical flair and no less contradiction. His New York Times op-ed denouncing the American capture of Venezuela's Nicolás Maduro as the first direct military attack on South America in two centuries was followed, on the same day, by a 45-minute call with Xi Jinping to coordinate what Brasília calls Global South resistance. Visa exemptions for Chinese citizens added a material flourish. Yet Mr Lula cannot escape Washington's gravitational pull either. Mr Trump's invitation to join a Gaza peace council was rightly read in Brasília as a trap: accept and become a subordinate, decline and confirm misalignment. Mr Lula chose grandiloquence — "Brazil will not submit to hegemonic projects" — while keeping dialogue open with Washington, a formula whose shelf life depends entirely on American patience. At home, the 2026 election is already sharpening. Mr Lula signalled he will run again with his vice-president, but polling shows Senator Flávio Bolsonaro beating him in a hypothetical run-off, and a rally of 18,000 for the imprisoned former president demonstrated that Bolsonarismo retains real mobilisation power. Sovereignty talk abroad may yet prove a weak substitute for economic results at home.

Mexico offers the clearest illustration of how performative sovereignty coexists with operational dependence. President Claudia Sheinbaum banned American military aircraft from landing at civilian airports, a move calibrated to look firm on television. Days later her security secretary met the new FBI director and promptly handed over 37 criminals to American custody, bringing the total transferred to 92 — with the careful caveat that none would face the death penalty. Ms Sheinbaum's approval rating sits at nearly 70%, proof that most Mexicans accept the balancing act. But her governing coalition is creaking: allied parties have stalled electoral reform because they were not consulted, and Morena lacks the votes for constitutional change on its own. Sovereignty gestures are easier than coalition management.

Chile's incoming president, José Antonio Kast, is attempting a different kind of independence — from his own coalition. His cabinet of 24 ministers includes 16 independents, the most technocratic line-up in Chilean democratic memory. Mr Kast sold this as "emergency governance," free of party quotas. Coalition partners, given almost no input, were not charmed. The gamble only makes sense if Mr Kast can bypass Congress through sheer executive competence, yet he enters office with a weak legislative position, a predecessor still facing a reopened embezzlement case, and deadly forest fires testing state capacity in the south. His early foreign-policy moves — studying the Dominican Republic's border-fence model, deploying a naval vessel to Antarctica — signal a presidency built on security and sovereignty claims. Whether technocracy without a legislative majority amounts to strength or isolation remains to be seen.

What unites these cases is not merely posture but paradox. Each leader is leveraging sovereignty rhetoric to consolidate domestic standing in an era when American unpredictability makes such rhetoric both more appealing and more dangerous. Mr Carney, Mr Lula, Ms Sheinbaum and Mr Kast all discovered the same thing this week: the louder you proclaim independence, the more visible become the constraints — economic, institutional, coalitional — that make true independence impossible. Middle powers across the Americas are performing sovereignty rather than practising it, and the audience they most need to convince is at home.

## western-europe

**The Transatlantic Crack-Up**

The week's events across Europe point to a single, uncomfortable conclusion: the American alliance, long treated as the fixed point around which European security and diplomacy revolve, is now the main source of instability that European governments must manage. From Paris to Rome to Oslo, leaders who had calibrated their foreign policies to Washington's preferences found themselves scrambling not against a rival power but against their principal ally — and discovering, in the process, that their domestic political arrangements are too fragile to absorb the shock.

France's response was the most deliberately theatrical. Mr Macron used Davos to denounce American attempts to "subordinate Europe," while his Foreign Ministry launched a meme-laden social media account to parry disinformation from both Moscow and Washington — a pairing that would have been unthinkable two years ago. Yet the most revealing detail was quieter: a French magistrate reported that American State Department officials had approached her to cast Marine Le Pen's conviction as political persecution. If true, this represents not diplomatic friction but interference in a sovereign judiciary, and it landed in a country where Ms Le Pen's appeal trial — and with it her 2027 presidential eligibility — already consumes the political oxygen. France talks tough abroad while governing at home through constitutional workarounds; Prime Minister Lecornu broke his own promise and rammed the budget through parliament under Article 49.3, surviving censure motions from left and right alike. Confronting America is easier when it distracts from the absence of a functioning majority.

Italy's case is more telling precisely because Ms Meloni had invested so heavily in her personal rapport with Mr Trump. She called his Greenland tariff threats "an error," telephoned him to say so, then issued a sharper rebuke after he disparaged NATO allies' service in Afghanistan — a remark that touched the memory of 53 dead Italian soldiers. The pivot was strategic as well as emotional: within days Ms Meloni was in Berlin signing defence and competitiveness agreements with Chancellor Merz, positioning a "Merzoni" axis as a rival engine to the old Franco-German partnership. Yet her coalition remains fractured. Deputy Prime Minister Salvini received Tommy Robinson, the British far-right agitator, at his ministry, forcing Mr Tajani to distance himself publicly and reminding observers that Italy's diplomatic recalibration rests on a government whose members cannot agree on which foreigners to be seen with.

Britain and the Nordic states illustrate the pattern from different angles. Mr Starmer called Mr Trump's Afghanistan slur "insulting and frankly appalling" and demanded an apology — language no British prime minister has used toward a sitting American president in decades. King Charles reportedly raised concerns through private channels, an intervention that hints at how far normal diplomatic guardrails have eroded. In Norway, Mr Trump sent Prime Minister Støre a message linking Greenland to the Nobel Peace Prize, prompting the foreign minister to break a longstanding taboo by floating EU membership. Sweden chose European solidarity over automatic American alignment, its prime minister declaring the country would not bow and scrape; meanwhile Jimmie Åkesson of the Sweden Democrats overtook the Social Democrat leader as the most trusted politician, a milestone that underscores how domestic realignments complicate any government's room for manoeuvre abroad. Even Spain, consumed by a deadly train crash and territorial financing disputes, found King Felipe defending European unity before the European Parliament in terms plainly aimed at Washington.

Germany, the continent's largest economy, split the difference. Mr Merz spoke coolly at Davos about strategic autonomy; his finance minister called Mr Trump's tariffs "blackmail." Yet Berlin quietly waited for NATO's secretary-general to broker a face-saving Arctic framework, after which the threats evaporated. Germany's Bundeswehr posted its best recruitment figures in 12 years — but experts publicly questioned whether 1,236 tonnes of German gold sitting in New York Federal Reserve vaults remained safe under an erratic American president. That a serious country entertains such a question tells you how deep the mistrust runs.

What the week reveals is not a temporary spat but a structural shift. European governments are being forced to treat the United States simultaneously as an indispensable partner and an unpredictable threat — and none of them possesses the domestic political stability to sustain that balancing act for long. The alliance is not breaking; it is becoming the problem it was designed to solve.
