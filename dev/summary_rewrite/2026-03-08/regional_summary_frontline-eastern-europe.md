**Nuclear Umbrellas, Paper Coalitions**

The week's events across central and northern Europe suggest a single, uncomfortable truth: the countries rushing fastest to militarise are the ones least able to keep their domestic politics together, and the contradiction is not incidental—it is structural. Governments that stake everything on defence credibility find they have little political capital left for anything else, while oppositions discover that a wartime atmosphere does not, as the textbooks promise, rally citizens behind their leaders.

Finland offers the starkest case. Helsinki announced the most consequential shift in its defence posture since joining NATO in 2023: a bill to scrap the decades-old ban on nuclear weapons transiting Finnish soil, a public recruitment drive for Russian- and Chinese-speaking spies, and a 25,000-troop Arctic exercise. Yet the government making these moves is haemorrhaging support. Petteri Orpo, the prime minister, was heckled by 20,000 demonstrators at a Women's Day march, and his National Coalition Party has sunk to 17.9%, its lowest in five years. The opposition Social Democrats lead by seven points. A government projecting strength abroad is projecting weakness at home, and voters appear unmoved by the security argument that is supposed to justify austerity and alliance obligations.

Poland's version of the same tension is fiscal rather than electoral. Karol Nawrocki, the president, unveiled a plan to finance defence spending from the central bank's gold reserves instead of EU loans—a manoeuvre that keeps Warsaw's military upgrade on track while snubbing Brussels. The scheme is politically ingenious: it lets the right claim sovereignty and defence seriousness simultaneously. But it also reveals how deeply the question of who pays for rearmament divides Polish politics. Jarosław Kaczyński's decision to name the hardliner Przemysław Czarnek as his candidate for prime minister in 2027 confirms that the conservative flank is fragmenting rightward, not consolidating. Defence consensus exists in the abstract; the bill for it does not.

In the Czech Republic, the cost is institutional rather than financial. Parliament voted to shield both Andrej Babiš and Tomio Okamura from prosecution, freezing fraud and hate-speech charges until at least 2029. The coalition's unity on self-protection contrasted grotesquely with its disunity on foreign policy: Mr Babiš backed American strikes on Iran while Mr Okamura called them destabilising, and the prime minister shrugged that each man "speaks to his voters." When Václav Moravec, the country's most prominent political interviewer, quit Czech Television saying he could no longer guarantee editorial independence, the signal was plain. A government that cannot agree on its own foreign posture can still agree to insulate itself from the law, and the institutions meant to hold it accountable are buckling.

Romania rounds out the pattern. The governing coalition—already an awkward marriage of Social Democrats, Liberals, and the Save Romania Union—is edging toward collapse over the 2026 budget, even as the country takes delivery of more than a thousand Turkish armoured vehicles and its president proposes expanding the Bucharest Nine to include Nordic allies. Klaus Iohannis insists Romania needs no nuclear weapons of its own because NATO's umbrella suffices, yet the coalition that would have to sustain defence spending cannot agree on how much to allocate to pensions. Estonia, whose defence minister was caught holidaying in Dubai during the Iran strikes, polls at a combined 14.9% for its two governing parties—against nearly 75% for the opposition—yet secured a €100m South Korean ammunition factory in the same week.

The pattern is now clear enough to name. Across NATO's eastern and northern rim, democratic governments are achieving real military integration at a pace that would have seemed fanciful five years ago: nuclear hosting, Arctic exercises, arms factories, drone-sharing with Gulf states. But each step demands political capital that these same governments do not possess, and voters are punishing them not for the defence agenda itself but for everything they neglect while pursuing it. Rearmament is winning the argument and losing the electorate. That gap is not a paradox to be admired; it is a vulnerability an adversary could exploit.
