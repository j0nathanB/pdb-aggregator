## asia-pacific

**The Week the Indo-Pacific Discovered It Was Already at War**

The Middle East conflict that erupted between America, Israel and Iran this week did not stay in the Middle East. Across the Indo-Pacific, governments that had spent years hedging between great powers found themselves forced into operational choices — on military posture, monetary policy, energy security and diplomatic alignment — that revealed how thin the line between peacetime partnership and wartime commitment had become.

Australia's predicament is the starkest. Three Royal Australian Navy submariners were aboard the American vessel that sank an Iranian frigate and killed 87 crew. Anthony Albanese, the prime minister, insisted they took no offensive action, a distinction that matters legally but not strategically: Iranian drones then struck Al Minhad Air Base near Dubai, where Australian forces are stationed, marking the first time Tehran has directly targeted Australians. Penny Wong, the foreign minister, floated expanding military support to Gulf states while ruling out ground combat — a calibration that assumes adversaries will respect fine print. Meanwhile, Michele Bullock, the Reserve Bank governor, said a rate rise was now possible because oil-price spikes from the war were feeding into inflation. AUKUS, sold to Australians as a submarine programme for deterring China, has delivered them into a shooting conflict with Iran.

Japan's response has been more deliberate but no less consequential. Sanae Takaichi refused to offer a legal assessment of American-Israeli strikes, citing insufficient information — diplomatic code for wanting to say nothing before meeting Donald Trump on March 19. Yet her government moved fast on the structural side, submitting proposals to abolish the five export-control categories that have constrained Japan's defence industry, and activating evacuation preparations for nationals across four Gulf states. Kazuo Ueda, the Bank of Japan governor, warned that Middle East turmoil could delay further rate rises. Japan is not choosing sides; it is choosing capability, trusting that an expanded arms industry and a deepened alliance network — including a new comprehensive strategic partnership with Canada — will keep options open regardless of which crisis comes next.

South Korea's exposure is more economic than military, but no less sharp. The KOSPI suffered its worst single-day crash in history, plunging over 12% as oil fears hammered Samsung and SK Hynix. The won breached 1,500 per dollar before the Bank of Korea intervened. The swift partial recovery suggests the sell-off reflected energy anxiety rather than structural rot, yet it laid bare how dependent Asia's fourth-largest economy remains on stable Gulf oil flows. Lee Jae-myung, riding a 65% approval rating after state visits to Singapore and the Philippines, has the political capital to absorb the shock — for now. His opponents in the People Power Party chose this moment to rally against his judicial reforms rather than rally around the flag, a sign that South Korea's domestic polarisation may prove harder to manage than any external threat.

Taiwan and Indonesia, meanwhile, reveal how a distant war reshapes even those countries not on the front line. Cho Jung-tai's visit to Tokyo — the first by a Taiwanese leader since 1972 — was planned before the Iran crisis, but it landed in a world where Japan is loosening arms controls and deepening security ties: context that made a baseball game look like a strategic signal. At home, Mr Cho's refusal to countersign opposition legislation and the stalemate over defence spending show an island whose institutional paralysis persists even as the security environment darkens. In Jakarta, Prabowo Subianto seized the moment to offer Indonesia as a mediator, consulting two former presidents before making the bid. The ambition is grand; the vulnerability is concrete. Two Pertamina tankers remained stuck in the Strait of Hormuz, a reminder that Indonesia's energy lifeline runs through the war zone it aspires to pacify.

The pattern is now plain. Every government in the Indo-Pacific treated its American alliance, its trade dependencies and its diplomatic hedging as separate policies, manageable in isolation. This week proved they are not. A war in the Persian Gulf instantly became a monetary-policy problem in Sydney, a stock-market crisis in Seoul, a constitutional argument in Taipei and a stranded-tanker headache in Jakarta. The region's leaders are not choosing whether to be involved; they are discovering they already are.

## frontline-eastern-europe

**Nuclear Umbrellas, Paper Coalitions**

The week's events across central and northern Europe suggest a single, uncomfortable truth: the countries rushing fastest to militarise are the ones least able to keep their domestic politics together, and the contradiction is not incidental—it is structural. Governments that stake everything on defence credibility find they have little political capital left for anything else, while oppositions discover that a wartime atmosphere does not, as the textbooks promise, rally citizens behind their leaders.

Finland offers the starkest case. Helsinki announced the most consequential shift in its defence posture since joining NATO in 2023: a bill to scrap the decades-old ban on nuclear weapons transiting Finnish soil, a public recruitment drive for Russian- and Chinese-speaking spies, and a 25,000-troop Arctic exercise. Yet the government making these moves is haemorrhaging support. Petteri Orpo, the prime minister, was heckled by 20,000 demonstrators at a Women's Day march, and his National Coalition Party has sunk to 17.9%, its lowest in five years. The opposition Social Democrats lead by seven points. A government projecting strength abroad is projecting weakness at home, and voters appear unmoved by the security argument that is supposed to justify austerity and alliance obligations.

Poland's version of the same tension is fiscal rather than electoral. Karol Nawrocki, the president, unveiled a plan to finance defence spending from the central bank's gold reserves instead of EU loans—a manoeuvre that keeps Warsaw's military upgrade on track while snubbing Brussels. The scheme is politically ingenious: it lets the right claim sovereignty and defence seriousness simultaneously. But it also reveals how deeply the question of who pays for rearmament divides Polish politics. Jarosław Kaczyński's decision to name the hardliner Przemysław Czarnek as his candidate for prime minister in 2027 confirms that the conservative flank is fragmenting rightward, not consolidating. Defence consensus exists in the abstract; the bill for it does not.

In the Czech Republic, the cost is institutional rather than financial. Parliament voted to shield both Andrej Babiš and Tomio Okamura from prosecution, freezing fraud and hate-speech charges until at least 2029. The coalition's unity on self-protection contrasted grotesquely with its disunity on foreign policy: Mr Babiš backed American strikes on Iran while Mr Okamura called them destabilising, and the prime minister shrugged that each man "speaks to his voters." When Václav Moravec, the country's most prominent political interviewer, quit Czech Television saying he could no longer guarantee editorial independence, the signal was plain. A government that cannot agree on its own foreign posture can still agree to insulate itself from the law, and the institutions meant to hold it accountable are buckling.

Romania rounds out the pattern. The governing coalition—already an awkward marriage of Social Democrats, Liberals, and the Save Romania Union—is edging toward collapse over the 2026 budget, even as the country takes delivery of more than a thousand Turkish armoured vehicles and its president proposes expanding the Bucharest Nine to include Nordic allies. Klaus Iohannis insists Romania needs no nuclear weapons of its own because NATO's umbrella suffices, yet the coalition that would have to sustain defence spending cannot agree on how much to allocate to pensions. Estonia, whose defence minister was caught holidaying in Dubai during the Iran strikes, polls at a combined 14.9% for its two governing parties—against nearly 75% for the opposition—yet secured a €100m South Korean ammunition factory in the same week.

The pattern is now clear enough to name. Across NATO's eastern and northern rim, democratic governments are achieving real military integration at a pace that would have seemed fanciful five years ago: nuclear hosting, Arctic exercises, arms factories, drone-sharing with Gulf states. But each step demands political capital that these same governments do not possess, and voters are punishing them not for the defence agenda itself but for everything they neglect while pursuing it. Rearmament is winning the argument and losing the electorate. That gap is not a paradox to be admired; it is a vulnerability an adversary could exploit.

## near-east-south-asia

**The Gulf Under Fire Reveals a Region Where Crises Accelerate Ambitions Rather Than Check Them**

The week's Iranian drone and missile barrage across the Gulf tested the proposition that the region's autocratic modernisers have built states sturdy enough to absorb a serious blow and keep building. On the evidence so far, they have—but the price of resilience is a tighter embrace of the very foreign patrons and military pacts that these leaders spent years trying to diversify away from.

Saudi Arabia's response to the shutdown of Ras Tanura, its largest oil refinery, was a textbook display of crisis as opportunity. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman worked the phones with a half-dozen capitals, accepted Volodymyr Zelensky's offer of Ukrainian drone-warfare expertise, and dispatched his defence minister to invoke the kingdom's nuclear-ambiguous defence pact with Pakistan—all while Aramco quietly inaugurated the $100 billion Jafurah gas development on schedule. The message was that Vision 2030 does not pause for Iranian Shaheds. Yet the very breadth of Mr bin Salman's diplomacy betrayed the limits of Saudi self-sufficiency: the kingdom needed partners from Islamabad to Kyiv to plug the holes in its air defences. And NEOM's quiet pivot from a fantastical linear city to AI data centres suggests the leadership is learning that resilience demands commercial realism, not architectural grandeur.

The UAE delivered the starkest evidence that war concentrates rather than scatters an ambitious state's energies. Mohammed bin Zayed's declaration that the country was at war—unprecedented in its 54-year history—came alongside a 93% interception rate against more than 1,400 Iranian projectiles, a fresh $16 billion investment by Mubadala in Waymo, and another prisoner exchange between Russia and Ukraine brokered by Abu Dhabi. Mr bin Zayed's warning that the UAE was "no easy prey" broke with the federation's habitual diplomatic restraint, yet the real signal was domestic: the public solidarity between Abu Dhabi and Dubai's rulers showed a federation that closes ranks under threat rather than fragmenting. The danger is that success in a short bombardment breeds overconfidence about a longer one.

India's predicament illustrated what happens when strategic ambiguity meets a geographically inconvenient war. The sinking of an Iranian frigate by an American submarine near Sri Lanka, in waters New Delhi considers its own preserve, forced Prime Minister Narendra Modi into the sharpest foreign-policy choice of his tenure—one he had spent a decade avoiding. Unlike the Ukraine conflict, which could be managed with abstentions in New York, this crisis arrived on India's doorstep. The evacuation of 52,000 citizens, the emergency security-cabinet meeting, and Mr Jaishankar's promise to brief parliament all pointed to a government scrambling to preserve its everyone's-friend posture while the neighbourhood caught fire. The simultaneous reset with Canada over trade and critical minerals showed Mr Modi still juggling, but the balls are heavier now.

Turkey offered the counter-example of a government that exploits external turmoil to settle domestic scores. Mr Erdoğan used the same week to put Istanbul's mayor on trial alongside 401 co-defendants—a prosecution that could remove his likeliest electoral challenger—while positioning Ankara as the indispensable mediator between Washington, Tehran and Brussels. The central bank burned through an estimated $7-8 billion in reserves to defend the lira, yet the finance minister trumpeted Turkey's new high-income status. The juxtaposition was brazen but coherent: Mr Erdoğan treats crisis as cover, knowing that foreign leaders calling for his help in the Gulf are unlikely to press him on judicial independence in Istanbul.

The pattern across all four countries is the same. Authoritarian and semi-authoritarian governments are using the shock of Iranian aggression not to retrench but to accelerate pre-existing plans—military, economic and political—while leveraging the emergency to deepen dependence on allies they had hoped to keep at arm's length. Resilience, it turns out, is not the same as independence. Each leader emerged from the week looking stronger at home and more entangled abroad, a combination that works brilliantly until the next crisis demands a choice between the two.

## the-americas

**The Sovereignty Shuffle**

The most revealing pattern in the Americas this week is not any single crisis but a shared contradiction: governments across the hemisphere are loudly asserting economic independence from traditional patrons while quietly discovering that independence multiplies the number of masters they must serve. Mark Carney, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, Claudia Sheinbaum and José Antonio Kast all cast themselves as champions of sovereign agency, yet each found that the pursuit of autonomy generates fresh dependencies, internal fractures and awkward debts that no trade deal can settle.

Mr Carney's Asian tour illustrates the point most cleanly. His $5.5 billion in agreements—uranium with India, free trade on the horizon, closer ties with Japan and Australia—are meant to loosen Canada's reliance on the United States. The ambition is sensible; the execution is already tangled. Even as he signed with New Delhi, Canadian intelligence confirmed that Indian agents are still meddling in Canadian politics, a fact the government had been eager to declare resolved. Worse, Mr Carney's "regretful" endorsement of American-Israeli strikes on Iran provoked a caucus revolt that forced his foreign minister into a Friday damage-control session. A leader polling at 61 per cent approval and 49 per cent party support can absorb such blows, but the episode showed that diversifying away from Washington does not free a prime minister from Washington's wars—it merely adds new audiences to displease.

In Brazil, the corrosion is financial rather than diplomatic but the logic is the same. Lula spent two years positioning himself as a developing-world statesman independent of American orthodoxy, yet this week the threat came from inside his own household. His son Lulinha's R$19.5 billion in suspect transactions, and the revelation that the owner of the troubled Banco Master held private meetings with the president, dragged Lula into the kind of scandal he once promised to leave behind. A Datafolha poll showing him effectively tied with Flávio Bolsonaro in a hypothetical 2026 run-off suggests voters are already recalculating. Sovereignty rhetoric rings hollow when the sovereign's family cannot explain its bank statements.

Chile's transition crisis is the week's sharpest case study in the costs of trying to please everyone. Mr Kast suspended all meetings with outgoing President Boric after a 22-minute row over a Chinese submarine-cable project—the first such breakdown since democracy returned in 1990. The quarrel is notionally about transparency, but its roots lie in an impossible balancing act: Washington wants Chile to block Chinese infrastructure; Beijing offers investment Chile's copper-dependent economy needs; and Mr Kast's own Republican Party sent a delegation to discuss technology deals with Chinese state firms while their leader was denouncing the cable. The contradiction is not hypocrisy so much as structural. A middle-income country caught between great powers cannot choose sovereignty; it can only choose which dependency to advertise and which to hide.

Mexico managed the juggling act most deftly, but only by keeping every ball low. Record foreign investment of $40.9 billion and an 11-year low in Pemex's debt suggest that energy sovereignty and investor confidence can coexist—for now. Yet Mexico evacuated citizens from the Middle East with American help, hunted cartel leaders under American pressure, and criticised American foreign policy at the United Nations, all in the same week. Its ruling coalition, meanwhile, began cracking over electoral reform, with the Labour Party accusing Morena of becoming the very party it replaced. Even the most nimble performer eventually runs out of hands.

What the week reveals across these four countries is a single analytical claim: the pursuit of strategic autonomy in the current international order does not reduce political risk but redistributes it inward. Every new trade partner, every fresh alignment, every declaration of independence from an old patron creates a new constituency to manage at home—a caucus to placate, a family scandal to contain, a coalition ally to mollify, a transition to salvage. Sovereignty, in practice, is not freedom from dependence but the multiplication of it.

## western-europe

**Europe's Sovereigntists Still Need a State That Works**

The week's events across western Europe point to a single, uncomfortable conclusion: governments that rush to assert strategic autonomy abroad are finding they lack the domestic cohesion to sustain it. From Paris to Berlin to Madrid, leaders declared independence from Washington with one breath and exposed their own institutional fragility with the next. The pattern is not hypocrisy so much as a structural mismatch—Europe's political classes have the ambition of great powers and the governing capacity of fractious coalitions.

France offers the purest case. Emmanuel Macron's announcement from Île Longue—extending nuclear deterrence to eight allies, ending three decades of stockpile transparency, dispatching the carrier Charles de Gaulle to the eastern Mediterranean—was the boldest repositioning of French defence policy in a generation. Yet the president who would be Europe's nuclear guarantor presides over a republic whose left is busy tearing itself apart over antisemitism. Jean-Luc Mélenchon's mockery of Jewish-sounding names prompted the Socialists to rule out any alliance with his party, handing Marine Le Pen's National Rally, already polling at 36% in the first round, an uncontested path toward 2027. Mr Macron can project force into the Mediterranean; he cannot project a governing majority into the Assemblée Nationale.

Germany's dysfunction is, if anything, more consequential. Friedrich Merz flew to Washington, broke with European partners by endorsing military action against Iran, and returned to discover his own coalition had disavowed him before his plane landed. Lars Klingbeil, the Social Democrats' co-leader, flatly declared "this is not our war." A chancellor contradicted by his defence minister on the central foreign-policy question of the moment does not lead a government; he chairs a debating society. The humiliation in Baden-Württemberg, where the Greens unseated his party, and the Bundeswehr's 20% recruit dropout rate only deepen the impression that Berlin's ambitions outrun its means. The Bundesbank's €8.6 billion loss—six consecutive years without a profit transfer—is a quieter but no less telling sign of institutional strain.

Spain and Britain illustrate the same gap from opposite directions. Pedro Sánchez denied America use of Rota and Morón, weathered Donald Trump's threat to cut all trade, and found that 53% of Spaniards backed him—a rare case of defiance translating into domestic support. But his majority depends on Catalan separatists who are now threatening to block the regional budget unless Madrid transfers income-tax collection within 16 days. Sovereignty abroad means nothing if the budget collapses at home. In Britain, Keir Starmer first refused to let American bombers use British bases, then reversed course after Iran hit British installations in Cyprus—a zigzag that satisfied nobody and coincided with Labour falling behind the Greens in national polling for the first time. A prime minister at 16% approval and 12% government satisfaction is in no position to redefine the Atlantic relationship.

Even the Nordic states, often held up as models of institutional competence, show cracks under the new pressure. Norway's foreign minister calls the security environment the worst since 1945 and opens nuclear talks with France, yet the government refused to send aircraft to evacuate its own citizens from the Gulf. Sweden deploys crisis teams to Riyadh and jams a Russian drone off Malmö, but trust in NATO has dropped ten points in a year, and the governing coalition had to pause its own deportation policy after internal revolt. Italy's defence minister was caught in Dubai when Iran struck, offering explanations his own press called "lacking, implausible and contradictory," while false bomb threats hit government buildings in Rome.

The pattern is now explicit. Europe's leaders are making commitments—nuclear umbrellas, base denials, carrier deployments, new defence doctrines—that demand sustained political will, fiscal headroom and institutional competence. In every country examined this week, at least one of those three is visibly absent. Strategic autonomy is not an announcement; it is a capacity. And capacity requires the boring, unglamorous work of holding coalitions together, keeping budgets solvent and maintaining public trust. Until Europe's sovereigntists build states sturdy enough to match their rhetoric, the doctrine will remain an aspiration dressed up as a policy.
