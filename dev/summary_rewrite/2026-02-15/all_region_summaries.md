## asia-pacific

**The Price of Protection**

The week's news across the Indo-Pacific reveals a single uncomfortable truth: the region's democracies are trading institutional integrity for security, and the bill is coming due. From Taiwan's sweeping trade concessions to Japan's constitutional ambitions, from Australia's fractured opposition to Indonesia's defiant nationalism, governments are concentrating power and accepting dependency in ways that weaken the domestic checks meant to keep them honest. The bargain may look rational today. It is storing up fragility for tomorrow.

Taiwan illustrates the dynamic most starkly. President Lai Ching-te has signed away tariff protections on almost all American goods and committed a quarter of a trillion dollars in purchases—energy, aircraft, power equipment—over five years. This is not trade liberalisation; it is tribute, paid to keep American carriers notionally between Taiwan and the mainland. Mr Lai can afford the concession only because TSMC's dominance in advanced chips gives the island leverage no tariff schedule could match. Yet even that "silicon shield" is eroding: the company's new 3nm facility in Japan shifts production closer to the ally and further from the protectorate. Meanwhile, Taiwan's parliament descends into fistfights so routine that prosecutors have indicted ten legislators, and the defence budget Mr Lai needs most remains stalled by the very institution meant to approve it. Security bought abroad cannot compensate for legitimacy squandered at home.

Japan's trajectory looks like a mirror image—strength abroad matched by democratic hollowing within. Sanae Takaichi's Liberal Democratic Party has won 316 lower-house seats, the largest single-party haul since 1945, and 93% of the winners back revising the pacifist constitution. Markets cheered: the Nikkei crossed 57,000 for the first time. But a supermajority is not the same as a mandate for any particular amendment, and the opposition has splintered so thoroughly—the Constitutional Democratic Party and Komeito cannot even form a joint upper-house caucus—that parliamentary scrutiny is now vestigial. Ms Takaichi plans an intelligence strategy, anti-espionage laws and a two-year suspension of food consumption tax, all at once. When a leader faces no credible check on her power, the risk is not tyranny but hubris: policies enacted too fast, with too little debate, tend to be policies reversed too late.

Australia offers the clearest case of a country whose strategic commitments have outrun its political capacity to sustain them. Anthony Albanese has committed $30 billion to a nuclear-submarine yard in Adelaide—17 Eiffel towers' worth of steel, 10,000 construction jobs—locking the country into decades of dependence on American and British technology. Bipartisan support for such a project ought to be a given. Instead, the Liberal Party has just deposed its first female leader after nine months, its primary vote has sunk to 18%—below One Nation's 28%—and the new leader, Angus Taylor, calls it a "change or die" moment. Social cohesion is fraying in parallel: 41% of Australians now call society "extremely" or "very" divided. A foreign minister who refuses to condemn calls to "globalise the intifada" and a prime minister dismissed as a "manager, not a leader" suggest a government that has wagered everything on hardware while neglecting the software of domestic consent.

Indonesia rounds out the pattern with a twist: rather than paying for American protection, Prabowo Subianto is leveraging it. He fired financial regulators after a market plunge, defended economic nationalism at an investor forum, and then volunteered up to 8,000 troops for Donald Trump's Gaza stabilisation force—the first country to do so. His approval rating sits near 80%, coalition parties are already lining up behind a second term in 2029, and the sovereign wealth fund Danantara is chasing credit ratings even as governance doubts triggered the selloff that made those ratings necessary. Mr Prabowo's confidence is rational only if markets and allies remain patient. Both have short memories and shorter fuses.

The pattern across these four countries is not merely that security anxieties are rising; it is that the pursuit of external safety is corroding the internal institutions—parliaments, oppositions, regulators, social trust—on which durable security ultimately depends. Democracies that hollow themselves out to buy protection may find they have purchased the one thing arms cannot defend against: their own fragility.

## frontline-eastern-europe

**The Fortress That Cannot Agree on Its Own Front Door**

The week's events across central and eastern Europe reveal a single uncomfortable truth: the states most exposed to Russian aggression are arming faster than they are governing well, and the gap between military seriousness and political coherence is becoming a strategic liability in its own right.

Ukraine illustrates the paradox most starkly. Mr Zelensky's government is simultaneously preparing territorial concessions in Donetsk and pledging to kill 50,000 Russian soldiers a month — negotiating the terms of peace while prosecuting an ever more industrialised war. Corps reform, expanded drone battalions and 74% air-defence effectiveness all suggest a military machine that has learned hard lessons. Yet the diplomatic picture is one of improvisation: courting Beijing with trade statistics, haggling with Washington over Tomahawks, and quietly discussing withdrawal lines that no politician dares announce publicly. The bind is real. A country cannot credibly negotiate from strength if its patron is shouting "get moving" from the sidelines, nor can it sustain mobilisation when draft evasion is so widespread that ruling-party politicians openly admit the system is failing.

Poland shows what happens when deterrence outpaces governance. A Polish four-star general will command NATO's Joint Forces Command Brunssum; the Sejm approved a €200 billion EU defence programme; the armed forces are set to grow to half a million. Yet the prime minister walked out of a national-security meeting because he refused to sit in a room run by the president's allies, and the intelligence services are investigating whether the head of the National Security Bureau holds valid clearances. Mr Tusk brands his opponents "enemies of Polish independence," but the real enemy of Polish readiness is a civil-military coordination process that collapses whenever the two camps share a table. The right-wing opposition is hardly healthier: Mr Kaczyński threatens to purge dissent from PiS while Confederation's Mr Mentzen offers coalition — on condition that Mr Kaczyński goes.

The Baltic states, smallest and most vulnerable, are drawing the sharpest conclusions about American reliability — and the most divergent ones about what to do next. Lithuania's prime minister speaks openly of planning for NATO's collapse and proposes renaming the Taiwanese office in Vilnius to mend fences with Beijing, a reversal that amounts to admitting a diplomatic miscalculation. Latvia's Ms Siliņa declares that "business as usual" with Washington is over, then joins an Arctic NATO mission prompted by the very American president she is rebuking. Estonia, meanwhile, recalls its ambassador from Georgia to punish democratic backsliding, yet faces embarrassment when its foreign minister leaves a tablet on a commercial flight. Each Baltic capital is serious about defence — Latvia spends 4.9% of GDP on its military, Lithuania has sent more than a billion dollars to Ukraine — but each is also improvising its foreign policy week by week, reacting to Washington's moods rather than shaping them.

Further south and west, the pattern holds in noisier form. The Czech Republic saw its largest demonstrations since 1989, not over war or economy but over text messages a foreign minister sent to the president — a domestic spat that nonetheless forced the two to travel separately to Munich and divided a country whose ammunition initiative for Ukraine is already billions short. Romania's Mr Ciolacu attacks his own statistics agency and threatens to collapse the coalition, even as Hanwha Aerospace breaks ground on a howitzer factory and NATO runs live-fire exercises across multiple Romanian sites. Defence investment rolls forward on institutional autopilot; political leadership lurches from crisis to crisis.

The pattern is now too consistent to ignore. From Kyiv to Bucharest, the states on NATO's eastern flank are converting threat perception into hardware and doctrine with impressive speed — but their political systems are consuming energy in factional warfare, institutional mistrust and diplomatic improvisation that steadily erodes the credibility their militaries are working to build. Armies do not deter on their own; alliances do, and alliances rest on political cohesion. Eastern Europe is building the wall while quarrelling over who holds the keys.

## near-east-south-asia

**The Autocrat's Toolkit Gets an Upgrade**

The most revealing thing about this week's events across the wider Middle East and South Asia is not that strongmen are consolidating power — that is old news — but that they are doing so with growing institutional sophistication, building structures designed to outlast any single crisis. From Ankara's judiciary to Abu Dhabi's identity politics to New Delhi's parliamentary manoeuvres, governments are constructing durable machinery of control while maintaining just enough diplomatic flexibility to keep foreign partners from objecting too loudly.

Turkey offers the starkest case. Recep Tayyip Erdoğan's decision to make Akın Gürlek — the prosecutor who sought a sentence of more than 2,000 years for Ekrem İmamoğlu, the jailed opposition leader — his justice minister is not merely provocative; it is architectural. Mr Gürlek's appointment puts the architect of judicial persecution in charge of the entire justice system, formalising what was previously an informal arrangement between the presidency and pliant courts. The fist fights it provoked in parliament were themselves a symptom: when institutions are captured, physical confrontation becomes the only outlet left. Mr İmamoğlu may work 18-hour days from his cell and call for snap elections, but opposition mayors are already defecting to Mr Erdoğan's party, citing "harassment and threats." The machinery is working as designed.

India's governing apparatus is subtler but no less purposeful. The contradiction between Marco Rubio's claim that New Delhi agreed to curb Russian oil purchases and S. Jaishankar's insistence on "strategic autonomy" suggests that ambiguity itself has become a tool of Indian statecraft — agree to enough to secure a trade deal, then reinterpret the terms at home. Domestically, the attempt to disqualify Rahul Gandhi from parliament, combined with a no-confidence motion against the speaker, signals that the Bharatiya Janata Party and the opposition alike now treat legislative procedure as a weapon rather than a framework. Narendra Modi's relocation of his office to a complex called "Seva Teerth" — "freedom from slavery," in his telling — wraps institutional transformation in nationalist symbolism, making it harder to oppose without appearing unpatriotic.

The Gulf monarchies are playing a longer game with deeper pockets. Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund has pivoted its 2030 strategy from vanity megaprojects toward logistics, mining and clean energy — a pragmatic admission that spectacle alone does not build an economy, though $16 billion in memorandums of understanding at a single forum shows the kingdom can still command capital's attention. The UAE, meanwhile, is using record ADNOC profits to buy American infrastructure — Mubadala's $6.2 billion acquisition of Clear Channel gives Abu Dhabi control of the largest outdoor advertising network in the United States — while simultaneously sending diplomats to Tehran and joining an eight-country condemnation of Israel's West Bank expansion. Most telling was the creation of a National Identity Committee in a country where nearly nine in ten residents are foreign nationals. When a regime that derives legitimacy from tribal authority starts institutionalising identity, it is acknowledging a demographic vulnerability it would rather not name.

What connects Ankara's captured judiciary, New Delhi's rewritten parliamentary norms, Riyadh's strategic pivot and Abu Dhabi's identity anxieties is a shared recognition: raw power is fragile unless it is embedded in institutions that reproduce themselves. The pattern this week is not authoritarianism as crisis management but authoritarianism as infrastructure — purpose-built, professionally staffed and designed to function long after the current headline fades.

## the-americas

**When the State Is the Product**

The week's news across the Americas reveals a single awkward truth: governments that once let markets lead are now betting that the state itself can be packaged, sold and rated like a corporate bond — and the bet is working just well enough to be dangerous. From Ottawa's defence-industrial plan to Pemex's return to debt markets to Codelco's lithium partnerships, the pattern is the same: the public sector is borrowing the grammar of private enterprise — "partners," credit ratings, oversubscribed offerings — while keeping political control firmly in hand. The risk is not that these ventures will fail spectacularly, but that early successes will mask the governance problems that state-led models always accumulate.

Mark Carney's $6.6 billion defence plan is the clearest case. It promises 125,000 jobs and reduced dependence on American equipment, but its architecture — private-sector "partners" chosen by government, a new Defence, Security and Resilience Bank endorsed by all six big Canadian lenders, and a bidding war among three cities to host it — reads less like industrial strategy than like a public offering of sovereignty. Mr Carney is simultaneously trying to stitch the Trans-Pacific Partnership to the European Union, a trading bloc of 1.5 billion people assembled not by comparative advantage but by shared alarm at Donald Trump. The political logic is compelling; whether the institutions can bear the weight is another matter. A mass shooting in British Columbia briefly united all parties and showed the system can coordinate, but the underlying dynamic — Liberals leading in polls, Conservatives rallying a populist base, both cutting quiet deals to keep a minority government alive — suggests that Canada's new statism rests on an electoral truce, not a consensus.

Mexico's state energy companies offer a test of what happens when the truce holds longer. Pemex's first local debt placement since 2019 drew more demand than supply; the Federal Electricity Commission's international bonds were seven times oversubscribed. The market is, for now, willing to price state enterprises as though they were run like private ones, even though the ruling Morena party's internal feuds — a senator defying anti-nepotism rules, factional stress threatening President Claudia Sheinbaum — show that political discipline is fraying precisely as fiscal discipline improves. The government's ability to pass a constitutional cut in the working week from 48 to 40 hours while simultaneously sending its security secretary to Washington for quiet bilateral meetings demonstrates a familiar Latin American talent: performing sovereignty for the domestic audience while cooperating pragmatically behind the curtain.

Brazil shows what happens when the state-as-product model collides with its own contradictions. Petrobras posted record production of 3.08 million barrels a day, pre-salt fields humming, exports at an all-time high. Yet the Supreme Court — the institution meant to referee the state's expanding reach — faces criminal evidence against one of its own justices, Dias Toffoli, over links to a banking fraud. The court's credibility is crumbling in an election year when Bolsonarismo, embodied by Flávio Bolsonaro's polling lead over President Lula in hypothetical run-offs, is proving it can outlive its founder's imprisonment. A cyberattack compromising more than half a million Workers' Party member records only underscores that the digital infrastructure of governance is as leaky as its analogue equivalent.

Chile supplies the sharpest warning. Codelco, the world's largest copper producer and the jewel of Chilean state capitalism, fired three senior executives for hiding safety data from regulators — a cover-up that preceded an accident killing six miners. The scandal lands just as President-elect José Antonio Kast inherits a structural fiscal deficit of 3.6%, triple the target, and just as Codelco secures a $900 million lithium deal with Rio Tinto and authorises $3.9 billion in new investment for 2026. The outgoing Boric administration's state-led lithium strategy will continue under a right-wing successor not because he endorses it ideologically, but because the money is already committed. That is the nature of state capitalism: it creates facts on the ground that outlast the governments which created them, and it buries the risks in the same place.

The pattern is plain. Across the hemisphere, states are not retreating from markets but dressing up in market clothing — issuing bonds, courting credit ratings, recruiting private partners — while retaining the political prerogatives that make genuine accountability difficult. Early returns look encouraging: oversubscribed offerings, record production, new defence banks. But the week also produced a covered-up mine disaster, a compromised supreme court justice, a fiscal target missed for the third year running, and a defence plan built atop a fragile minority government. States can sell themselves as products; the question is who audits the prospectus.

## western-europe

**Europe's Fortress of Words**

The week's events across western Europe expose a single uncomfortable truth: governments are growing bolder in what they say while growing weaker in what they can do. From Berlin to Stockholm, leaders issued declarations of strategic autonomy, moral clarity and institutional resolve—yet each pronouncement was undercut by coalition fractures, recruitment shortfalls, scandal or political paralysis at home. The gap between rhetoric and capacity is not a temporary embarrassment; it is becoming the defining feature of European governance.

Germany offers the starkest case. Friedrich Merz told the Munich Security Conference that American leadership in Europe may already be "lost" and called for the continent to become a self-sustaining pillar of NATO. His foreign minister, Johann Wadephul, accused Russia of murdering Alexei Navalny with a rare nerve agent and promised to take the evidence to international bodies. Yet the Bundeswehr, the instrument that would underwrite any such autonomy, cannot fill its own ranks. The Lithuania brigade—Germany's first permanent foreign deployment and the emblem of its new seriousness—has attracted volunteers for fewer than half its positions, with combat units managing a pitiful 10%. Mr Merz's coalition approval has sunk to 22%. A chancellor whose voters do not back him and whose army cannot recruit is a poor candidate to replace American hegemony.

Britain's predicament is different in form but identical in structure. Keir Starmer dispatched the carrier HMS Prince of Wales to the Arctic and had his chancellor declare stronger European ties "the biggest prize" available. Abroad, British foreign policy appeared purposeful. At home, Mr Starmer lost his chief of staff and communications director in a single week over the Mandelson-Epstein affair, faced a demand from Scottish Labour's leader that he resign, and watched the monarchy drawn into the scandal as King Charles was heckled in Lancashire. A further revelation—that a think tank close to Mr Starmer had paid to investigate journalists' personal backgrounds, including their religion—suggested an operation more paranoid than principled. The government that would project power into the High North cannot manage its own internal communications.

France and Italy illustrate a subtler version of the same contradiction. Emmanuel Macron launched Orion 26, France's largest military exercise since the Cold War, deploying 12,500 troops and 25 ships across the Atlantic coast—yet France's real political energy is consumed by the question of whether Marine Le Pen will be allowed to run for president in 2027, a verdict the Paris appeals court has deferred until July next year. Ms Le Pen remains the favourite if eligible, and Mr Macron's response has been to place allies in key posts while he still can, a strategy the opposition bluntly calls "verrouillage"—locking the doors on the way out. In Rome, Giorgia Meloni skipped Munich for a second year running, preferring her Africa summit, and announced possible observer status on Donald Trump's Board of Peace. But her coalition fractured when General Roberto Vannacci quit the Lega to form his own party, taking MPs with him. Ms Meloni tightened her grip through naval-blockade laws and denunciations of protesters as "enemies of Italy," compensating in coercive authority for what she was losing in coalition cohesion.

The Nordic countries, often held up as models of institutional health, proved no exception. Sweden's governing Tidö Alliance split publicly over converting permanent residence permits to temporary ones, with the Sweden Democrats and their coalition partners pulling in opposite directions—even as Swedish Gripen fighters joined NATO's new Arctic Sentry patrols. Norway's former prime minister, Thorbjørn Jagland, was charged with gross corruption linked to Jeffrey Epstein, and the sitting foreign minister admitted breaking conflict-of-interest rules. Spain's Popular Party formally accepted the necessity of governing with the far-right Vox, even as parliamentary debate descended into personal invective so bitter that the prime minister called the opposition leader "repugnant." In each case, the state's outward posture—NATO deployment, diplomatic firmness, democratic resolve—ran ahead of the domestic politics meant to sustain it.

The pattern is now too widespread to dismiss as coincidence. Europe's democracies are not failing to act; they are acting abroad while fraying at home. Military exercises grow larger, diplomatic language grows tougher, and institutional commitments multiply—all while coalitions crack, scandals metastasise and public trust erodes. This is not hypocrisy so much as overextension: governments reaching for a geopolitical role their domestic foundations can no longer reliably support. The fortress looks imposing from the outside. The question is whether anyone is minding the walls from within.
