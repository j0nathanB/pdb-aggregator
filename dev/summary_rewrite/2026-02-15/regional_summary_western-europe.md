**Europe's Fortress of Words**

The week's events across western Europe expose a single uncomfortable truth: governments are growing bolder in what they say while growing weaker in what they can do. From Berlin to Stockholm, leaders issued declarations of strategic autonomy, moral clarity and institutional resolve—yet each pronouncement was undercut by coalition fractures, recruitment shortfalls, scandal or political paralysis at home. The gap between rhetoric and capacity is not a temporary embarrassment; it is becoming the defining feature of European governance.

Germany offers the starkest case. Friedrich Merz told the Munich Security Conference that American leadership in Europe may already be "lost" and called for the continent to become a self-sustaining pillar of NATO. His foreign minister, Johann Wadephul, accused Russia of murdering Alexei Navalny with a rare nerve agent and promised to take the evidence to international bodies. Yet the Bundeswehr, the instrument that would underwrite any such autonomy, cannot fill its own ranks. The Lithuania brigade—Germany's first permanent foreign deployment and the emblem of its new seriousness—has attracted volunteers for fewer than half its positions, with combat units managing a pitiful 10%. Mr Merz's coalition approval has sunk to 22%. A chancellor whose voters do not back him and whose army cannot recruit is a poor candidate to replace American hegemony.

Britain's predicament is different in form but identical in structure. Keir Starmer dispatched the carrier HMS Prince of Wales to the Arctic and had his chancellor declare stronger European ties "the biggest prize" available. Abroad, British foreign policy appeared purposeful. At home, Mr Starmer lost his chief of staff and communications director in a single week over the Mandelson-Epstein affair, faced a demand from Scottish Labour's leader that he resign, and watched the monarchy drawn into the scandal as King Charles was heckled in Lancashire. A further revelation—that a think tank close to Mr Starmer had paid to investigate journalists' personal backgrounds, including their religion—suggested an operation more paranoid than principled. The government that would project power into the High North cannot manage its own internal communications.

France and Italy illustrate a subtler version of the same contradiction. Emmanuel Macron launched Orion 26, France's largest military exercise since the Cold War, deploying 12,500 troops and 25 ships across the Atlantic coast—yet France's real political energy is consumed by the question of whether Marine Le Pen will be allowed to run for president in 2027, a verdict the Paris appeals court has deferred until July next year. Ms Le Pen remains the favourite if eligible, and Mr Macron's response has been to place allies in key posts while he still can, a strategy the opposition bluntly calls "verrouillage"—locking the doors on the way out. In Rome, Giorgia Meloni skipped Munich for a second year running, preferring her Africa summit, and announced possible observer status on Donald Trump's Board of Peace. But her coalition fractured when General Roberto Vannacci quit the Lega to form his own party, taking MPs with him. Ms Meloni tightened her grip through naval-blockade laws and denunciations of protesters as "enemies of Italy," compensating in coercive authority for what she was losing in coalition cohesion.

The Nordic countries, often held up as models of institutional health, proved no exception. Sweden's governing Tidö Alliance split publicly over converting permanent residence permits to temporary ones, with the Sweden Democrats and their coalition partners pulling in opposite directions—even as Swedish Gripen fighters joined NATO's new Arctic Sentry patrols. Norway's former prime minister, Thorbjørn Jagland, was charged with gross corruption linked to Jeffrey Epstein, and the sitting foreign minister admitted breaking conflict-of-interest rules. Spain's Popular Party formally accepted the necessity of governing with the far-right Vox, even as parliamentary debate descended into personal invective so bitter that the prime minister called the opposition leader "repugnant." In each case, the state's outward posture—NATO deployment, diplomatic firmness, democratic resolve—ran ahead of the domestic politics meant to sustain it.

The pattern is now too widespread to dismiss as coincidence. Europe's democracies are not failing to act; they are acting abroad while fraying at home. Military exercises grow larger, diplomatic language grows tougher, and institutional commitments multiply—all while coalitions crack, scandals metastasise and public trust erodes. This is not hypocrisy so much as overextension: governments reaching for a geopolitical role their domestic foundations can no longer reliably support. The fortress looks imposing from the outside. The question is whether anyone is minding the walls from within.
