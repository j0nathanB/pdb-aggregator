**The Fortress That Cannot Agree on Its Own Front Door**

The week's events across central and eastern Europe reveal a single uncomfortable truth: the states most exposed to Russian aggression are arming faster than they are governing well, and the gap between military seriousness and political coherence is becoming a strategic liability in its own right.

Ukraine illustrates the paradox most starkly. Mr Zelensky's government is simultaneously preparing territorial concessions in Donetsk and pledging to kill 50,000 Russian soldiers a month — negotiating the terms of peace while prosecuting an ever more industrialised war. Corps reform, expanded drone battalions and 74% air-defence effectiveness all suggest a military machine that has learned hard lessons. Yet the diplomatic picture is one of improvisation: courting Beijing with trade statistics, haggling with Washington over Tomahawks, and quietly discussing withdrawal lines that no politician dares announce publicly. The bind is real. A country cannot credibly negotiate from strength if its patron is shouting "get moving" from the sidelines, nor can it sustain mobilisation when draft evasion is so widespread that ruling-party politicians openly admit the system is failing.

Poland shows what happens when deterrence outpaces governance. A Polish four-star general will command NATO's Joint Forces Command Brunssum; the Sejm approved a €200 billion EU defence programme; the armed forces are set to grow to half a million. Yet the prime minister walked out of a national-security meeting because he refused to sit in a room run by the president's allies, and the intelligence services are investigating whether the head of the National Security Bureau holds valid clearances. Mr Tusk brands his opponents "enemies of Polish independence," but the real enemy of Polish readiness is a civil-military coordination process that collapses whenever the two camps share a table. The right-wing opposition is hardly healthier: Mr Kaczyński threatens to purge dissent from PiS while Confederation's Mr Mentzen offers coalition — on condition that Mr Kaczyński goes.

The Baltic states, smallest and most vulnerable, are drawing the sharpest conclusions about American reliability — and the most divergent ones about what to do next. Lithuania's prime minister speaks openly of planning for NATO's collapse and proposes renaming the Taiwanese office in Vilnius to mend fences with Beijing, a reversal that amounts to admitting a diplomatic miscalculation. Latvia's Ms Siliņa declares that "business as usual" with Washington is over, then joins an Arctic NATO mission prompted by the very American president she is rebuking. Estonia, meanwhile, recalls its ambassador from Georgia to punish democratic backsliding, yet faces embarrassment when its foreign minister leaves a tablet on a commercial flight. Each Baltic capital is serious about defence — Latvia spends 4.9% of GDP on its military, Lithuania has sent more than a billion dollars to Ukraine — but each is also improvising its foreign policy week by week, reacting to Washington's moods rather than shaping them.

Further south and west, the pattern holds in noisier form. The Czech Republic saw its largest demonstrations since 1989, not over war or economy but over text messages a foreign minister sent to the president — a domestic spat that nonetheless forced the two to travel separately to Munich and divided a country whose ammunition initiative for Ukraine is already billions short. Romania's Mr Ciolacu attacks his own statistics agency and threatens to collapse the coalition, even as Hanwha Aerospace breaks ground on a howitzer factory and NATO runs live-fire exercises across multiple Romanian sites. Defence investment rolls forward on institutional autopilot; political leadership lurches from crisis to crisis.

The pattern is now too consistent to ignore. From Kyiv to Bucharest, the states on NATO's eastern flank are converting threat perception into hardware and doctrine with impressive speed — but their political systems are consuming energy in factional warfare, institutional mistrust and diplomatic improvisation that steadily erodes the credibility their militaries are working to build. Armies do not deter on their own; alliances do, and alliances rest on political cohesion. Eastern Europe is building the wall while quarrelling over who holds the keys.
