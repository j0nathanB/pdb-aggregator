**The Competence Illusion**

The week's news across western Europe reveals a shared paradox: governments are projecting strength abroad with greater conviction than they can muster at home, and the gap between external ambition and domestic dysfunction is widening faster than any of them care to admit. Bold diplomacy and landmark deals grab headlines, but they rest on political foundations that are cracking under the weight of fractured coalitions, blocked legislatures, and institutional norms quietly being abandoned.

France offers the starkest case. A government that cannot pass a budget without resorting to constitutional strong-arming—Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu invoked Article 49.3 for the third time, surviving six censure motions—simultaneously opened a consulate in Greenland to counter American territorial ambitions and launched an irreverent social-media operation to parry Russian and American disinformation. The "French Response" account gained 145,000 followers; the budget gained no parliamentary majority at all. Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot insisted Europeans "can and must take charge of their security," yet the government he serves cannot take charge of its own legislature. The assertiveness is real, but it floats on a void of domestic legitimacy that Marine Le Pen, calibrating her appeal-trial strategy for a presidential run, and Jean-Luc Mélenchon, cheerfully borrowing the far right's vocabulary, are each racing to fill.

Germany's version of the paradox is more polished but no less real. Chancellor Friedrich Merz urged Europe to speak "the language of power politics," and his finance minister, Lars Klingbeil, unveiled the EU-India free trade agreement—19 years in the making—that slashes tariffs on German cars from 110% to 10%. A new E6 format with France, Poland, Spain, Italy, and the Netherlands promises to deepen economic coordination. Yet the Alternative for Germany has published a 156-page governing programme for a state where it polls at 40%, complete with "remigration" plans and proposals to gut democratic oversight bodies. The Bundeswehr, meanwhile, remains 9% below its recruitment target. Germany is building a European architecture of resilience while its own political foundations shift beneath it.

Britain illustrates how the strain can be simultaneously external and internal. Keir Starmer secured real prizes in Beijing—visa-free travel, expanded trade, £10.9 billion in AstraZeneca investment—only for Donald Trump to call the deals "very dangerous," exposing the cost of hedging between great powers. At home, Labour's National Executive Committee blocked Andy Burnham from a by-election with Mr Starmer personally voting against a potential rival, while the Conservatives under Kemi Badenoch told centrists to "get out of the way" as more MPs defected to Reform UK. Even the monarchy broke form: King Charles released an environmental advocacy documentary on Amazon Prime, stepping into political territory that the constitutional settlement is supposed to keep closed. When the governing party suppresses internal competition, the opposition purges its own centre, and the head of state enters advocacy, diplomatic gains abroad cannot paper over institutional erosion at home.

Italy and Spain confirm the pattern from different angles. Giorgia Meloni's coalition fractured as Roberto Vannacci formally launched his own party, threatening the parliamentary arithmetic that keeps the government alive, even as Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani pushed to designate Iran's Revolutionary Guards a terrorist organisation at EU level—a move hawkish enough to provoke Tehran into summoning Italy's ambassador. In Spain, the Adamuz train disaster that killed 45 people became a crisis of governmental credibility when Pedro Sánchez broke two days of silence to attend a UN event rather than the victims' funeral, while his opponents internationalised the catastrophe at a European People's Party summit. Both governments project decisiveness beyond their borders while struggling to hold together the coalitions and public trust needed to govern at home.

Europe is a continent whose leaders speak with growing confidence about strategic autonomy, power politics, and diplomatic innovation, while their domestic political orders—hollowed out by coalition fragility, populist insurgency, and institutional norm-breaking—provide ever less ballast for the ambitions they advertise. External projection and internal erosion are not merely coexisting; they are feeding off each other, as leaders reach outward precisely because the ground at home has become too unstable to stand on.
