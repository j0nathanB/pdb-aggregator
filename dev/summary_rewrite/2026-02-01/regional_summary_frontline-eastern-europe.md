**The Militarisation of Normality**

The week's news from central and eastern Europe points to a single, uncomfortable conclusion: the region's democracies are being reshaped less by any dramatic rupture than by the quiet, steady absorption of wartime logic into peacetime institutions. From gold vaults to volunteer drone networks, from Baltic border protocols to Romanian motorway budgets, the machinery of the state is being retooled for a conflict that has not yet arrived on most of these countries' soil — and the political costs of that transformation are only beginning to show.

Ukraine illustrates the pattern in its purest form. The appointment of Kyrylo Budanov, the former intelligence chief, to run the Presidential Office is not merely a personnel shuffle; it marks the formal displacement of political operatives by security professionals at the apex of civilian governance. Pair this with the defence ministry's decision to give official advisory roles to two Telegram influencers — one with 156,000 followers, the other responsible for delivering more than 256,000 drones — and what emerges is a state that has stopped pretending the line between civil society and the war effort still exists. That these moves coincide with the most substantive diplomatic opening yet, including bilateral talks with Russia described as free of "pseudo-historical lectures" and a security-guarantees document said to be ready for signature, only sharpens the paradox: Ukraine is simultaneously preparing to negotiate peace and institutionalising permanent war readiness.

Poland's version of the same tension is fiscal rather than institutional, but no less revealing. The National Bank's purchase of 102 tons of gold in 2025, making it the world's largest buyer for the second consecutive year, is not the behaviour of a country at ease with the international monetary order. Governor Adam Glapiński frames the policy as building "credibility and security," yet the gold now exceeds the European Central Bank's own holdings. Meanwhile, the political system that must fund both butter and guns is seizing up: the governing coalition and the presidency have stopped speaking to each other, with opposition parties refusing even to attend meetings called by President Karol Nawrocki. When the prime minister must publicly rebuke the president for his response to American remarks about NATO, the dual-executive model designed to project allied unity is instead advertising its dysfunction.

Finland and the Baltic states reveal how the securitisation of policy extends well beyond defence budgets. Finland's State Audit Office found that the government's claimed €10 billion in savings amounts to barely €3.5 billion — a gap that matters enormously when the country is simultaneously trying to meet EU deficit rules and push military spending toward 3% of GDP. Estonia, spending 5% of GDP on defence while producing the world's highest density of unicorn startups, proposed an EU-wide entry ban on nearly a million Russian veterans, a policy that would have seemed fantastical three years ago. Lithuania formalised a "military Schengen" with its Baltic neighbours, eliminating peacetime bureaucratic barriers to troop movements. Each step is individually rational. Taken together, they describe societies reorganising around a threat assessment that leaves little room for the priorities — welfare, infrastructure, fiscal prudence — that defined European normality before 2022.

The cracks in that project are visible in the states where the consensus is thinnest. In the Czech Republic, 90,000 people took to the streets not over foreign policy but over a foreign minister accused of threatening the president's adviser — yet the government simultaneously confirmed it would resist NATO pressure to move beyond 2% defence spending, a stance that sits uneasily with its aspirations to be taken seriously in a three-pole world. Romania, the EU's second-largest beneficiary of a new €16.6 billion defence fund, is ramping toward 5% of GDP in military spending by 2035 while running a 9.3% budget deficit, the worst in the union. Latvia's security services now assess that Russia views the Baltics much as it viewed Ukraine before the invasion — a sentence that, once written into an official report, changes the calculus of every subsequent budget line.

The pattern, then, is not militarisation as ideology but militarisation as bureaucratic habit: gold reserves swelling, drone operators drawing government salaries, border controls redesigned for armoured columns, and audit offices struggling to reconcile the books. These democracies are not becoming garrison states. But they are discovering that preparing for the worst, year after year, reshapes institutions in ways that no peace deal can easily reverse.
