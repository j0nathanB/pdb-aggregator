## asia-pacific

**The Hedgers' Gambit**

The week's news across the Indo-Pacific reveals a single, testable proposition: governments that are actively hedging between great powers are being rewarded by their electorates, even when that hedging imposes visible costs. The old assumption—that voters punish leaders for diplomatic provocation or economic turbulence—is breaking down, replaced by a public appetite for leaders who appear to be playing all sides at once. The pattern holds whether the hedger sits in Tokyo, Seoul, or Jakarta, and it is reshaping the region's politics faster than its alliances.

Japan's Sanae Takaichi offers the sharpest case. Her declaration that the US-Japan alliance would "collapse" if Tokyo stood idle during a Taiwan evacuation provoked Beijing into cancelling 46 airline routes and issuing travel advisories that have already thinned a tourist flow once worth a fifth of all arrivals. That is a real economic penalty, not a diplomatic slap. Yet Ms Takaichi's Liberal Democratic Party is polling to win outright control of the lower house in a snap election barely three months into her premiership. Voters appear to have priced in the Chinese retaliation and decided they can live with it—a judgment that would have been unthinkable a decade ago, when Japanese leaders trimmed their sails at the first hint of Beijing's displeasure. Ms Takaichi is simultaneously deepening ties with Britain on fighter jets, critical minerals, and quantum technology, behaving less like a junior ally of Washington than like a middle power assembling its own coalition.

South Korea's Lee Jae-myung is running the same playbook with even more audacity. Faced with Donald Trump's threat to raise tariffs on Korean cars and pharmaceuticals to 25%, Mr Lee did not genuflect; he visited China and Japan on back-to-back trips, the first presidential call on Beijing since 2019, and framed both as "pragmatic diplomacy." Samsung's record quarterly profit—operating earnings more than tripled on the back of AI-driven memory demand—gives him the economic ballast to sustain the balancing act. His approval rating sits at 53%, buoyed even by the continuing spectacle of his predecessors' disgrace: the wife of impeached President Yoon Suk-yeol was sentenced to 20 months for accepting Chanel bags from the Unification Church, while former prime minister Han Duck-soo received 23 years for abetting martial law. Mr Lee is leveraging institutional accountability as proof that democratic hedging, unlike autocratic caprice, comes with guardrails.

Indonesia exposes the limits of the model. Prabowo Subianto has tried to hedge too—joining Mr Trump's Board of Peace charter in Davos, maintaining "multi-alignment," and courting both American and Chinese capital—but the market does not believe his institutions can sustain it. An $80 billion equity rout, the worst since 1998, followed MSCI's warning of a possible downgrade to frontier status and parliament's installation of Mr Prabowo's nephew as deputy governor of the central bank. The state sovereign fund, Danantara, responded by seizing a million hectares of mining concessions, ordering asset managers to keep buying stocks, and announcing plans to own up to 30% of the Jakarta exchange after demutualisation. Where Ms Takaichi and Mr Lee hedge between external powers while reinforcing domestic institutions, Mr Prabowo hedges externally while hollowing them out. Investors noticed the difference in about 48 hours.

Taiwan and Australia, each in its own way, illustrate what happens when domestic politics cannot keep pace with the hedging imperative. Taipei opened a Joint Firepower Coordination Centre staffed partly by American advisers to manage its new asymmetric arsenal, yet the opposition legislature blocked the bulk of a NT$1.25 trillion defence budget, stripping out drones, air-defence systems, and tactical software. The Kuomintang simultaneously dispatched a 40-person delegation to Beijing for talks on tourism and industrial cooperation—hedging by a different faction, at cross-purposes with the government's own hedging. In Australia, the Reserve Bank is about to become the first major central bank to raise rates since the pandemic, an own goal that compounds a Liberal opposition in open factional warfare and a Greens party surging to 45% support among young voters. Neither Canberra nor Taipei is punished for hedging per se; both are punished for internal incoherence that makes hedging look accidental rather than strategic.

The pattern, then, is not merely that Indo-Pacific leaders are balancing between Washington and Beijing—that is old news. It is that electorates have begun to treat hedging as a competence signal: voters reward leaders who absorb diplomatic and economic blows without flinching, and punish those whose institutions crack under the strain. Credible hedging now requires credible governance. Where the two diverge, markets and voters deliver their verdict with equal speed.

## frontline-eastern-europe

**The Militarisation of Normality**

The week's news from central and eastern Europe points to a single, uncomfortable conclusion: the region's democracies are being reshaped less by any dramatic rupture than by the quiet, steady absorption of wartime logic into peacetime institutions. From gold vaults to volunteer drone networks, from Baltic border protocols to Romanian motorway budgets, the machinery of the state is being retooled for a conflict that has not yet arrived on most of these countries' soil — and the political costs of that transformation are only beginning to show.

Ukraine illustrates the pattern in its purest form. The appointment of Kyrylo Budanov, the former intelligence chief, to run the Presidential Office is not merely a personnel shuffle; it marks the formal displacement of political operatives by security professionals at the apex of civilian governance. Pair this with the defence ministry's decision to give official advisory roles to two Telegram influencers — one with 156,000 followers, the other responsible for delivering more than 256,000 drones — and what emerges is a state that has stopped pretending the line between civil society and the war effort still exists. That these moves coincide with the most substantive diplomatic opening yet, including bilateral talks with Russia described as free of "pseudo-historical lectures" and a security-guarantees document said to be ready for signature, only sharpens the paradox: Ukraine is simultaneously preparing to negotiate peace and institutionalising permanent war readiness.

Poland's version of the same tension is fiscal rather than institutional, but no less revealing. The National Bank's purchase of 102 tons of gold in 2025, making it the world's largest buyer for the second consecutive year, is not the behaviour of a country at ease with the international monetary order. Governor Adam Glapiński frames the policy as building "credibility and security," yet the gold now exceeds the European Central Bank's own holdings. Meanwhile, the political system that must fund both butter and guns is seizing up: the governing coalition and the presidency have stopped speaking to each other, with opposition parties refusing even to attend meetings called by President Karol Nawrocki. When the prime minister must publicly rebuke the president for his response to American remarks about NATO, the dual-executive model designed to project allied unity is instead advertising its dysfunction.

Finland and the Baltic states reveal how the securitisation of policy extends well beyond defence budgets. Finland's State Audit Office found that the government's claimed €10 billion in savings amounts to barely €3.5 billion — a gap that matters enormously when the country is simultaneously trying to meet EU deficit rules and push military spending toward 3% of GDP. Estonia, spending 5% of GDP on defence while producing the world's highest density of unicorn startups, proposed an EU-wide entry ban on nearly a million Russian veterans, a policy that would have seemed fantastical three years ago. Lithuania formalised a "military Schengen" with its Baltic neighbours, eliminating peacetime bureaucratic barriers to troop movements. Each step is individually rational. Taken together, they describe societies reorganising around a threat assessment that leaves little room for the priorities — welfare, infrastructure, fiscal prudence — that defined European normality before 2022.

The cracks in that project are visible in the states where the consensus is thinnest. In the Czech Republic, 90,000 people took to the streets not over foreign policy but over a foreign minister accused of threatening the president's adviser — yet the government simultaneously confirmed it would resist NATO pressure to move beyond 2% defence spending, a stance that sits uneasily with its aspirations to be taken seriously in a three-pole world. Romania, the EU's second-largest beneficiary of a new €16.6 billion defence fund, is ramping toward 5% of GDP in military spending by 2035 while running a 9.3% budget deficit, the worst in the union. Latvia's security services now assess that Russia views the Baltics much as it viewed Ukraine before the invasion — a sentence that, once written into an official report, changes the calculus of every subsequent budget line.

The pattern, then, is not militarisation as ideology but militarisation as bureaucratic habit: gold reserves swelling, drone operators drawing government salaries, border controls redesigned for armoured columns, and audit offices struggling to reconcile the books. These democracies are not becoming garrison states. But they are discovering that preparing for the worst, year after year, reshapes institutions in ways that no peace deal can easily reverse.

## near-east-south-asia

**The Retreat Into Resilience**

The most revealing thing about this week across the Middle East and South Asia is not any single crisis but a shared pattern: powers that recently projected ambition outward are now pulling back behind walls of economic consolidation and diplomatic hedging, even as they insist they are still ascending. From the Gulf to the subcontinent, governments are trading grand offensive strategies for defensive depth — restructuring sovereign wealth, stockpiling reserves, courting every side of every dispute — and calling this retrenchment progress.

Saudi Arabia's NEOM debacle is the most honest admission of the trend. The Line, that 170-kilometre monument to architectural hubris, has been cut to 1.5 miles — a reduction so severe it amounts to cancellation by another name. The Public Investment Fund took an $8 billion write-down, and new NEOM contracts have collapsed from $16.2 billion in 2023 to under $900 million in early 2025. Yet the PIF itself swelled to $930 billion, and Riyadh secured State Department approval for $9 billion in Patriot missiles. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman is not abandoning transformation; he is quietly conceding that transformation looks less like futuristic cities and more like balance-sheet fortification. The kingdom's Iran diplomacy tells the same story: privately encouraging American hawkishness while publicly pledging neutrality to Tehran, a posture that maximises optionality and minimises exposure but cannot survive an actual war.

The UAE's week reads as a mirror image. Abu Dhabi declared it would not permit its territory to be used for strikes on Iran, completed its withdrawal from Yemen after a decade of costly engagement, and consolidated $263 billion in sovereign assets under a single holding company tied to the crown prince's succession. Each move narrows the country's commitments while deepening its domestic financial power. The simultaneous launch of a "fully sovereign" AI reasoning system, developed with American-authorised chips, captures the contradiction neatly: the UAE is retreating from military exposure abroad precisely so it can entrench technological and economic leverage at home. That its once-tight relationship with Riyadh has deteriorated into open media warfare only reinforces the logic of pulling inward; when your principal ally becomes a rival, hedging is the only safe play.

India's bargain is different in scale but identical in logic. The free trade agreement with the European Union — eliminating tariffs on nearly 97% of goods — is less an act of liberalisation than one of strategic insurance. Mr Modi framed it explicitly as a hedge against "turmoil" caused by other powers, which is diplomatic shorthand for American unpredictability and Chinese coercion. The record defence budget, up 15% and linked by officials to lessons from Operation Sindoor, and the Reserve Bank's gold-buying spree that pushed reserves to record highs, confirm the pattern: New Delhi is spending to make itself harder to hurt rather than to project power further. Even the Adani Group's willingness to accept SEC legal service while simultaneously signing aircraft-manufacturing deals with Embraer reflects a corporate version of the same instinct — absorb the blow, diversify the exposure, keep moving.

Turkey alone seems to buck the trend, thrusting itself forward as mediator between Washington and Tehran with a confidence bordering on presumption. Yet even Mr Erdogan's diplomatic promiscuity is a form of defensive positioning. Ankara's value to both sides depends on its refusal to commit to either; the moment it picks a camp, it loses its leverage. Meanwhile, record central-bank reserves and rising exports provide the cushion that makes such acrobatics possible, while the persecution of Istanbul's mayor — now triggering an international accreditation investigation — reveals how domestic political warfare can quietly erode the institutional credibility that underpins the whole edifice.

The pattern, then, is not multipolarity triumphant but multipolarity anxious. These are governments that have concluded the world is becoming too dangerous for bold bets, and are converting ambition into resilience — hoarding reserves, diversifying alliances, scaling back commitments, and hoping that financial mass alone will see them through whatever comes next. It is a rational response to genuine uncertainty. But resilience without direction is just expensive stasis, and no sovereign wealth fund, however large, substitutes for a strategy.

## the-americas

# Middle Powers Learn to Bow Without Kneeling

The week's sharpest lesson from the Americas is that middle powers confronting Washington have discovered compliance can be dressed up as defiance—and that the disguise works well enough to keep domestic audiences satisfied. From Ottawa to Mexico City to Brasília, leaders faced American pressure and responded not with capitulation or resistance but with a third manoeuvre: yielding on substance while loudly proclaiming sovereignty. The pattern reveals less about any single leader's courage than about a structural shift in hemispheric politics, where the appearance of autonomy now matters more than its exercise.

Mark Carney offered the most theatrical version. He refused to recant his Davos speech calling for middle-power coalitions against American dominance, telling Mr Trump flatly "I meant what I said." Yet the bravado sits alongside a frantic trade-diversification campaign—deals with South Korea, uranium talks with India—that tacitly concedes the very dependence his rhetoric denounces. Mr Carney's defiance is strategic stagecraft: it strengthens him at home, where even his Conservative rival Pierre Poilievre won a leadership review with 87% support partly by embracing a hawkish posture toward Washington. The audience for Canadian sovereignty talk is Canadian voters, not the White House.

Claudia Sheinbaum's Mexico performed the inverse trick with greater candour. Pemex quietly cancelled oil shipments to Cuba after Mr Trump threatened tariffs, redirecting a tanker carrying 700,000 barrels to Denmark. Ms Sheinbaum called the decision "sovereign"; her foreign minister insisted there was "no subordination." Mr Trump then praised her as "wonderful and very intelligent," and the peso rallied. The episode was a textbook case of operational accommodation wrapped in nationalist language, and it succeeded precisely because both sides had an interest in pretending Mexico had chosen freely. The coalition-unity pact Morena signed days later, papering over strains on electoral reform, suggests Ms Sheinbaum knows the real cost of American pressure falls on domestic cohesion, not diplomacy.

Brazil's Lula played a subtler game during a 50-minute call with Mr Trump, proposing a Palestinian seat on Washington's Peace Council while limiting its scope to Gaza—a move designed to preserve independent positioning at negligible cost. Yet the more revealing development was Finance Minister Fernando Haddad's announced departure to run for the São Paulo governorship, draining the cabinet of its most market-credible figure just as Brazil's current-account deficit hits an 11-year high of $68.8 billion. Lula's strategic autonomy abroad sits uneasily beside structural vulnerability at home: foreign direct investment rose to $77.6 billion, but commodity dependence remains the load-bearing wall. Meanwhile the Bolsonaro movement demonstrated its mobilisation strength at a Brasília rally—where lightning injured 89 supporters—proving that polarisation, not sovereignty, will dominate the 2026 contest.

Chile, preparing for José Antonio Kast's presidency, showed what compliance looks like when it is not forced. Codelco's oversubscribed $1.25 billion bond sale and the Supreme Court's definitive rejection of Tianqi Lithium's challenge to the Codelco-SQM merger confirmed regulatory sovereignty over strategic minerals without a whisper of anti-American posturing. Mr Kast's cabinet is 67% independent technocrats, a composition that signals pragmatism to markets and congress alike. His diplomatic courtship of restrictive-immigration models from El Salvador and the Dominican Republic aligns Chile with Washington's preferences not under duress but by ideological affinity—the one form of alignment that requires no face-saving rhetoric.

The common thread is not defiance of the United States, nor submission to it, but the professionalisation of the gap between the two. Each government yielded where it had to, held firm where it could afford to, and ensured the domestic narrative emphasised the holding, not the yielding. This is the emerging hemispheric grammar: sovereignty as performance, accommodation as policy. It works for now, because Washington appears content to collect substance and let its neighbours keep the symbolism. The danger is that leaders who rehearse independence too convincingly may one day be expected to deliver it.

## western-europe

**The Competence Illusion**

The week's news across western Europe reveals a shared paradox: governments are projecting strength abroad with greater conviction than they can muster at home, and the gap between external ambition and domestic dysfunction is widening faster than any of them care to admit. Bold diplomacy and landmark deals grab headlines, but they rest on political foundations that are cracking under the weight of fractured coalitions, blocked legislatures, and institutional norms quietly being abandoned.

France offers the starkest case. A government that cannot pass a budget without resorting to constitutional strong-arming—Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu invoked Article 49.3 for the third time, surviving six censure motions—simultaneously opened a consulate in Greenland to counter American territorial ambitions and launched an irreverent social-media operation to parry Russian and American disinformation. The "French Response" account gained 145,000 followers; the budget gained no parliamentary majority at all. Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot insisted Europeans "can and must take charge of their security," yet the government he serves cannot take charge of its own legislature. The assertiveness is real, but it floats on a void of domestic legitimacy that Marine Le Pen, calibrating her appeal-trial strategy for a presidential run, and Jean-Luc Mélenchon, cheerfully borrowing the far right's vocabulary, are each racing to fill.

Germany's version of the paradox is more polished but no less real. Chancellor Friedrich Merz urged Europe to speak "the language of power politics," and his finance minister, Lars Klingbeil, unveiled the EU-India free trade agreement—19 years in the making—that slashes tariffs on German cars from 110% to 10%. A new E6 format with France, Poland, Spain, Italy, and the Netherlands promises to deepen economic coordination. Yet the Alternative for Germany has published a 156-page governing programme for a state where it polls at 40%, complete with "remigration" plans and proposals to gut democratic oversight bodies. The Bundeswehr, meanwhile, remains 9% below its recruitment target. Germany is building a European architecture of resilience while its own political foundations shift beneath it.

Britain illustrates how the strain can be simultaneously external and internal. Keir Starmer secured real prizes in Beijing—visa-free travel, expanded trade, £10.9 billion in AstraZeneca investment—only for Donald Trump to call the deals "very dangerous," exposing the cost of hedging between great powers. At home, Labour's National Executive Committee blocked Andy Burnham from a by-election with Mr Starmer personally voting against a potential rival, while the Conservatives under Kemi Badenoch told centrists to "get out of the way" as more MPs defected to Reform UK. Even the monarchy broke form: King Charles released an environmental advocacy documentary on Amazon Prime, stepping into political territory that the constitutional settlement is supposed to keep closed. When the governing party suppresses internal competition, the opposition purges its own centre, and the head of state enters advocacy, diplomatic gains abroad cannot paper over institutional erosion at home.

Italy and Spain confirm the pattern from different angles. Giorgia Meloni's coalition fractured as Roberto Vannacci formally launched his own party, threatening the parliamentary arithmetic that keeps the government alive, even as Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani pushed to designate Iran's Revolutionary Guards a terrorist organisation at EU level—a move hawkish enough to provoke Tehran into summoning Italy's ambassador. In Spain, the Adamuz train disaster that killed 45 people became a crisis of governmental credibility when Pedro Sánchez broke two days of silence to attend a UN event rather than the victims' funeral, while his opponents internationalised the catastrophe at a European People's Party summit. Both governments project decisiveness beyond their borders while struggling to hold together the coalitions and public trust needed to govern at home.

The pattern is now European in scope: a continent whose leaders speak with growing confidence about strategic autonomy, power politics, and diplomatic innovation, while their domestic political orders—hollowed out by coalition fragility, populist insurgency, and institutional norm-breaking—provide ever less ballast for the ambitions they advertise. External projection and internal erosion are not merely coexisting; they are feeding off each other, as leaders reach outward precisely because the ground at home has become too unstable to stand on.
