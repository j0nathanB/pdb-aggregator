## asia-pacific

**A Week of Convenient Boldness**

The most striking feature of this week across the Indo-Pacific is not that governments acted boldly, but that they chose boldness only where it cost them least—reserving caution, or outright retreat, for audiences closer to home. From Jakarta to Canberra, leaders projected strength abroad while trimming their ambitions at domestic pressure points, revealing a region where foreign policy is increasingly a performance staged for external partners rather than a coherent expression of national strategy.

Indonesia's Prabowo Subianto is the clearest case. In a single week he attended a peace summit in Washington, offered himself as a mediator between America, Israel and Iran, signed a semiconductor deal in London, and confirmed troops would deploy to Gaza—a dizzying itinerary for a president who also quietly scrapped a 105,000-truck order from Indian manufacturers after domestic industry lobbied against it. The truck reversal was small beer in dollar terms, yet it exposed where Mr Prabowo's real constraints lie: not in the chancelleries he visited but in the political marketplace at home, where even an ambitious nationalist president cannot afford to be seen importing vehicles his own industrialists want to build. His mediation offer, by contrast, costs nothing until someone accepts it.

Taiwan's Lai Ching-te demonstrated a subtler version of the same calculus. His sudden adoption of "mainland China"—language Taipei's independence-leaning camp has long avoided—was not a change of conviction but a tactical concession timed to smooth a Trump-Xi summit. Mr Lai can afford the rhetorical softening because Taiwan's real leverage keeps hardening: TSMC's market capitalisation crossed $2 trillion, and the company is accelerating domestic expansion rather than dispersing its fabs abroad. As long as Taiwan's chip dominance grows at home, its president can murmur whatever formula Washington requires without weakening the island's strategic hand.

Japan's Sanae Takaichi pushed furthest, winning approval to export lethal weapons for the first time since 1945 and advancing intelligence reforms that would have been unthinkable a decade ago. Yet even she hedged domestically, nominating dovish academics to the Bank of Japan's board to keep monetary conditions loose—a signal that her security revolution will be financed by easy money rather than fiscal discipline. The combination is potent but fragile: protests against constitutional revision, though modest, remind her that the public tolerance underwriting her agenda is borrowed, not earned. Her gift-catalogue controversy was trivial in substance, but it showed how quickly the old LDP playbook of patronage can distract from a genuinely transformative programme.

South Korea's Lee Jae-myung, riding a 58% approval rating against a shattered opposition, appeared to face the fewest constraints of any leader in the region—and used his freedom to consolidate judicial control at home while performing statesmanship abroad. His law criminalising deliberate judicial misapplication, rammed through over a 24-hour filibuster, is the week's starkest example of domestic power deployed without diplomatic cost: no foreign partner will object to what looks like an anti-corruption measure, even as South Korean prosecutors see it as a leash. Mr Lee's AI-generated video for Brazil and his Independence Day appeal to Pyongyang were exercises in image, not risk.

The pattern is consistent enough to name. Across five capitals, leaders spent the week accumulating foreign-policy credentials—military exports, peace brokerage, alliance spending, diplomatic upgrades—while carefully avoiding the domestic fights that would test whether their boldness is more than posture. The region's governments are growing more assertive on the world stage precisely because assertiveness abroad remains cheaper than reform at home. The danger is that one day the bills will arrive simultaneously.

## frontline-eastern-europe

# The Decisive Drift Toward Security States

**When every government in a region reshuffles its priorities around war, the question is no longer whether they are serious about defence but whether their politics can bear the weight.**

The most revealing move of the week came not from a battlefield but from a personnel file. Volodymyr Zelensky's decision to replace his chief of staff, Andriy Yermak—a political dealmaker—with Kyrylo Budanov, his former spy chief, signals that Ukraine now treats peace negotiations as an extension of war rather than a departure from it. Mr Budanov's opening position—that lasting security requires the dissolution of Russia as an empire—is not a diplomatic overture but a maximalist framing designed to anchor talks before they begin. The choreography of calls with Donald Trump, a planned Geneva meeting followed by a trilateral summit in Abu Dhabi, and Mr Zelensky's simultaneous media blitz all point to a country that has decided the next phase of the conflict will be won or lost in conference rooms and television studios, and has staffed accordingly. That the IMF simultaneously approved $8.1 billion in new financing suggests international institutions agree Ukraine's war economy deserves long-term backing, not just emergency relief.

Yet the further one moves from the front line, the harder it becomes for governments to sustain the political discipline that wartime demands. Poland offers the starkest case. Karol Nawrocki, the president, is threatening to veto €43.7 billion in EU defence funding—the country's largest-ever military modernisation package—over sovereignty concerns, even as his own security services arrest a Belarusian spy targeting NATO infrastructure across three countries. The contradiction is plain: Poland's executive branch simultaneously insists on maximum national defence and blocks the means to pay for it. The real driver is domestic: the government cannot override presidential vetoes, so every security question becomes a proxy fight between the ruling coalition and the opposition. Jarosław Kaczyński's decision to haul his own former prime minister before an ethics committee for public dissent confirms that the Polish right is fracturing under the strain of being out of power in wartime, not uniting behind it.

The Czech Republic shows what happens when a new government decides that fiscal discipline trumps alliance solidarity. Andrej Babiš slashed €900 million from planned defence spending, dropping the budget to 2.1% of GDP—below the target his predecessor had set and well short of what Washington now expects. His government also stopped funding the Czech ammunition initiative for Ukraine, a programme that had been one of Prague's chief claims to transatlantic relevance. Mr Babiš's coalition is simultaneously expected to vote down lifting his parliamentary immunity in a fraud case. The pattern is a government that treats the alliance as a menu from which to order selectively, keeping big-ticket items like F-35 contracts while trimming everything that lacks a domestic constituency. Whether Mr Trump's Washington notices the gap between rhetoric and budgets will test how transactional the new American foreign policy really is.

Finland and the Baltic states are trying to resolve the same tension—between fiscal limits and security ambitions—through sheer effort of will. Alexander Stubb, Finland's president, spent the week shuttling between Kyiv and Paris arguing that Ukraine can win, while at home his finance minister called new deficit projections "shocking" and demanded at least €10 billion in cuts over the next parliament. Estonia managed to stumble into a nuclear-weapons debate it did not intend to have, with the foreign minister floating the idea of hosting NATO warheads and the defence minister retreating within days—a revealing glimpse of how deterrence policy in small states can be made and unmade by a single interview. Lithuania, meanwhile, is split between its foreign minister, who wants to join a US-led critical-minerals initiative to counter China, and its prime minister, who insists on waiting for Brussels. Each country is committed to security in principle but struggling with the institutional and fiscal machinery needed to deliver it.

Romania completes the picture. Its ruling coalition is fraying over the budget even as drones from the Ukrainian war breach Romanian airspace and the air force scrambles jets in response. The Social Democrats threaten to leave government; the central bank warns that inflation may rise again in 2026; and the far-right Alliance for the Union of Romanians hovers in the background, ready to absorb disaffected voters. Yet Romania's foreign-policy alignment with Washington has not wavered, and its military exercises with NATO allies continue on schedule. The pattern across the region is now unmistakable: every government from Tallinn to Bucharest has accepted that European security requires a permanent wartime footing, but almost none has a domestic politics capable of sustaining one without constant internal crisis. The constraint on eastern Europe's defence is no longer willingness. It is governance.

## near-east-south-asia

**The Diplomacy of Ducking**

The week's crises across the Middle East and South Asia share a single revealing feature: every government caught in the crossfire responded not by choosing sides but by reinforcing a posture of strategic ambiguity — and in every case, the posture held. Iranian missiles and drones struck Saudi and Emirati soil, Israel and Iran exchanged fire, and yet no targeted state allowed the moment to collapse its carefully constructed network of hedged relationships. The pattern suggests that multi-alignment is no longer a fair-weather luxury for middle powers; it has become load-bearing architecture, tested under live fire and, for now, still standing.

Saudi Arabia offered the starkest illustration of the gap between private appetite and public restraint. Mohammed bin Salman reportedly lobbied Donald Trump in multiple phone calls to strike Iran even as Riyadh's official statements called for diplomatic solutions. The crown prince's double game is rational: he wants American firepower without the appearance of having summoned it, preserving his standing with Gulf neighbours and future room to re-engage Tehran. That the Ras Tanura refinery — processing 550,000 barrels a day — could be shut by drones despite billions in defence spending only sharpened the incentive. Mr bin Salman cannot defend his kingdom alone, yet cannot afford to look like Washington's client. So he plays both hands simultaneously, a trick sustainable only as long as neither audience compares notes.

The UAE's response was more dramatic in its symbolism but equally calibrated in its logic. Abu Dhabi closed its embassy in Tehran for the first time since independence, a step that would once have signalled a decisive rupture. Yet even as Mohammed bin Zayed condemned the strikes, he convened Gulf partners rather than reaching for unilateral escalation, and the country's technology sector barely paused. G42 unveiled a sovereign financial cloud platform and a semiconductor-governance framework praised by the White House — initiatives that depend on sustained American trust. The embassy closure was punishment enough to satisfy domestic anger without foreclosing the option of eventual re-engagement with Iran. The Emiratis, in short, slammed a door they know has a handle on both sides.

India demonstrated that this hedging impulse is not confined to the Gulf. Narendra Modi chose to address the Israeli Knesset — a first for an Indian premier — at the very moment Iranian missiles were falling on Israeli and Gulf targets, yet his foreign minister worked the phones with Tehran in parallel. Mr Modi chaired a security meeting to assess threats to Indian nationals, hosted Canada's Mark Carney to repair a separate diplomatic breach, and oversaw military exercises that rehearsed the lessons of Operation Sindoor, all within a few days. The subtext was unmistakable: India will deepen defence ties with Israel, maintain energy links with Iran, mend fences with Canada and reduce dependence on American trade, treating each relationship as a separate ledger. The approach works until the ledgers collide, a risk that intensifies with every regional escalation.

Turkey, meanwhile, converted the crisis into a showcase for its own indispensability. Recep Tayyip Erdoğan telephoned Gulf royals and the European Commission president; his foreign minister spoke to counterparts from at least eleven countries. At home, the government layered fresh charges on the imprisoned Istanbul mayor, Ekrem İmamoğlu, while advancing a Kurdish peace process designed to harvest votes for constitutional change — domestic manoeuvres that require a stable external environment. Mr Erdoğan needs to be seen as a mediator abroad precisely because his domestic consolidation depends on a region that does not force him to pick a camp.

What the week exposes is not the fragility of multi-alignment but its deepening entrenchment. Each government absorbed a shock — missiles, diplomatic ruptures, regional escalation — and responded by reinforcing, not abandoning, its network of hedged bets. The risk is not that this architecture collapses under a single blow but that its success encourages ever-greater brinkmanship from aggressors who calculate, correctly, that no coalition of fence-sitters will form a united front against them.

## the-americas

**When Allies Hedge**

The week's most telling pattern across the Americas is not conflict with the United States but something subtler: middle powers racing to build leverage against Washington while still needing its cooperation. Canada, Brazil, Mexico and Chile each demonstrated that hedging against American power now requires active management on multiple fronts simultaneously — security, trade, diplomacy and domestic politics — and that the balancing act is growing harder to sustain.

Mark Carney's Asia tour is the clearest case. Canada's prime minister returned with $5.5 billion in trade deals, including a uranium contract with Saskatchewan, explicitly to reduce dependence on American commerce. Yet from Mumbai he endorsed American and Israeli strikes on Iran's nuclear programme, a reminder that diversifying trade partners does not mean diversifying security patrons. Pierre Poilievre, the Conservative leader, spotted the contradiction: if China cannot substitute for the American partnership, as he argued, then the diversification strategy is more aspiration than architecture. Canada's economy, which shrank 0.6% in the fourth quarter, lends his scepticism weight. Mr Carney's 12-point polling lead buys him time, but the structural bind — wanting distance from Washington commercially while clinging to it militarily — remains unresolved.

Mexico's Claudia Sheinbaum is managing the same tension with greater finesse and fewer options. She authorised 19 American Navy SEALs to train Mexican marines in Campeche and accepted intelligence sharing that helped kill the Jalisco cartel's leader, but condemned American strikes on Iran and is considering legal action against Elon Musk. The balancing act extends to the economy: Pemex slashed losses by 94% and foreign investment hit a record $40.9 billion, almost all from American capital. Ms Sheinbaum is cooperating with Washington precisely where she must — security and energy — while preserving rhetorical independence everywhere else. It works so long as American attention stays focused on drugs and trade rather than on Iran or ideology.

Chile enjoys no such luxury. Washington revoked the visas of three Chilean officials over a Chinese submarine cable project — the first such sanction since the return to democracy. Gabriel Boric called it unacceptable and invoked the Monroe Doctrine, yet the same week his defence ministry accepted a $99m American contract to maintain Chile's F-16s. The visa revocations exposed the flaw in Chile's traditional hedging: when Washington forces a binary choice between Chinese infrastructure and American security guarantees, non-alignment ceases to be a strategy and becomes a liability. Mr Boric condemned both sides equally after the Iran strikes, but equal condemnation is a posture, not a policy.

Brazil's predicament is different in degree but not in kind. Lula wants a meeting with Mr Trump and "equal treatment" from Washington, yet his government condemned the Iran strikes and his poll numbers are collapsing — tied with Flávio Bolsonaro in a hypothetical 2026 runoff, with a rejection rate above 50%. A corruption probe into his son sharpens the sense of a presidency losing altitude. Lula's room to manoeuvre abroad narrows as his standing at home weakens; a leader who cannot guarantee his own re-election has less to offer Washington and less leverage to resist it.

What connects these four countries is a shared discovery: hedging against American power was easier when Washington was distracted and the alternatives were cheap. Now the costs of diversification — diplomatic confrontation with the United States, dependence on Chinese infrastructure, fractured domestic coalitions — are rising faster than the benefits. Each government is improvising a different formula, but the underlying arithmetic is the same. Autonomy from a dominant neighbour demands not just new trading partners but a domestic political consensus willing to absorb the price, and in every case that consensus is thinner than the rhetoric suggests.

## western-europe

**Europe's Governing Classes Are Losing Ground Faster Than They Can Rearm**

The week's events across western Europe share an uncomfortable thread: governments are racing to project strength abroad while the domestic foundations beneath them crack. From France's nuclear grandstanding to Britain's by-election humiliation, the gap between external ambition and internal authority is widening — and no leader seems able to close it.

Emmanuel Macron offers the starkest case. He will soon stand at the Île Longue submarine base to declare France Europe's nuclear guardian, a claim turbocharged by American unreliability under Donald Trump. Yet this is a president whom 77% of his own citizens judge negatively, whose party was shut out of Washington's loop when America and Israel struck Iran, and who could not prevent Mr Trump's ambassador from snubbing the Quai d'Orsay. France produced a thousand military drones in under a year — impressive for a medium power — but Mr Macron attacked the European Commission's decision to apply the Mercosur trade deal as "unacceptable," revealing a leader who wields vetoes more easily than influence. Marine Le Pen, meanwhile, is calmly arranging her succession with Jordan Bardella, as though the Élysée were already a formality deferred only by a court verdict in July.

Britain's Labour Party, supposedly restored to dominance less than a year ago, lost one of its safest seats to the Green Party while Reform UK pushed it into third place. Keir Starmer vowed to fight on, but his government's response to the Iran crisis — permitting defensive use of British bases while refusing offensive strikes — captured the broader posture: partial commitment dressed up as principle. David Lammy's plan to clear 80,000 court cases with artificial intelligence and fewer jury trials is bold on paper, yet it arrives alongside a royal scandal, a chancellor with nothing to announce, and a prime minister whose authority is draining with each vote count. The Bank of England hints at rate cuts, but monetary easing cannot substitute for political credibility.

Germany illustrates how institutional safeguards can fail at the worst moment. A Cologne court blocked the domestic intelligence service from classifying the Alternative for Germany as a confirmed extremist organisation, handing the far right a propaganda victory even as its own members resign over nepotism scandals. Friedrich Merz, the chancellor, visited Beijing to complain about an €80 billion trade deficit and flew to Washington to manage tariff threats, projecting diplomatic seriousness. But his defence minister admitted the Bundeswehr is "absolutely undersized" and below a "survival threshold," while a €540 million drone purchase drew controversy because of a Trump-linked investor. Germany is simultaneously too weak to deter and too entangled to decouple.

Italy and Spain confirm the pattern from the south. Iranian missiles struck a Kuwaiti base hosting 300 Italian troops, the most direct threat to Italian forces in years, yet a leaked defence document conceded that Italy's 170,000-strong military needs to nearly double — over 18 years and €7 billion. Giorgia Meloni's coalition is fraying as her party drops to 28% and a breakaway movement led by Roberto Vannacci peels off voters from both governing partners. In Madrid, Yolanda Díaz's decision not to lead the left alliance into 2027 leaves a vacuum in the governing coalition just as Santiago Abascal purges Vox into a disciplined vehicle for far-right advance across Spain's south. Polling shows Vox displacing the Socialists as the second party in provinces the left once owned.

Even Scandinavia, where state capacity is high and trust in institutions has long been a given, is not immune. Sweden's navy disabled a Russian drone threatening a French carrier in its waters — a creditable debut as a NATO member — but the Tidö coalition's poll numbers are sliding, the Liberal Party risks falling below the parliamentary threshold, and the Sweden Democrats are deferring contentious migration pledges until after the next election. Norway raised its Ukraine aid to 12 billion kroner while calling American strikes on Iran illegal, a posture that advertises moral seriousness but also diplomatic isolation. The pattern across the continent is now plain: European governments are spending more on defence, talking louder about sovereignty, and losing the voters whose consent makes any of it sustainable. Military budgets can be legislated; legitimacy cannot.
