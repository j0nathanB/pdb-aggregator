**The Diplomacy of Ducking**

The week's crises across the Middle East and South Asia share a single revealing feature: every government caught in the crossfire responded not by choosing sides but by reinforcing a posture of strategic ambiguity — and in every case, the posture held. Iranian missiles and drones struck Saudi and Emirati soil, Israel and Iran exchanged fire, and yet no targeted state allowed the moment to collapse its carefully constructed network of hedged relationships. The pattern suggests that multi-alignment is no longer a fair-weather luxury for middle powers; it has become load-bearing architecture, tested under live fire and, for now, still standing.

Saudi Arabia offered the starkest illustration of the gap between private appetite and public restraint. Mohammed bin Salman reportedly lobbied Donald Trump in multiple phone calls to strike Iran even as Riyadh's official statements called for diplomatic solutions. The crown prince's double game is rational: he wants American firepower without the appearance of having summoned it, preserving his standing with Gulf neighbours and future room to re-engage Tehran. That the Ras Tanura refinery — processing 550,000 barrels a day — could be shut by drones despite billions in defence spending only sharpened the incentive. Mr bin Salman cannot defend his kingdom alone, yet cannot afford to look like Washington's client. So he plays both hands simultaneously, a trick sustainable only as long as neither audience compares notes.

The UAE's response was more dramatic in its symbolism but equally calibrated in its logic. Abu Dhabi closed its embassy in Tehran for the first time since independence, a step that would once have signalled a decisive rupture. Yet even as Mohammed bin Zayed condemned the strikes, he convened Gulf partners rather than reaching for unilateral escalation, and the country's technology sector barely paused. G42 unveiled a sovereign financial cloud platform and a semiconductor-governance framework praised by the White House — initiatives that depend on sustained American trust. The embassy closure was punishment enough to satisfy domestic anger without foreclosing the option of eventual re-engagement with Iran. The Emiratis, in short, slammed a door they know has a handle on both sides.

India demonstrated that this hedging impulse is not confined to the Gulf. Narendra Modi chose to address the Israeli Knesset — a first for an Indian premier — at the very moment Iranian missiles were falling on Israeli and Gulf targets, yet his foreign minister worked the phones with Tehran in parallel. Mr Modi chaired a security meeting to assess threats to Indian nationals, hosted Canada's Mark Carney to repair a separate diplomatic breach, and oversaw military exercises that rehearsed the lessons of Operation Sindoor, all within a few days. The subtext was unmistakable: India will deepen defence ties with Israel, maintain energy links with Iran, mend fences with Canada and reduce dependence on American trade, treating each relationship as a separate ledger. The approach works until the ledgers collide, a risk that intensifies with every regional escalation.

Turkey, meanwhile, converted the crisis into a showcase for its own indispensability. Recep Tayyip Erdoğan telephoned Gulf royals and the European Commission president; his foreign minister spoke to counterparts from at least eleven countries. At home, the government layered fresh charges on the imprisoned Istanbul mayor, Ekrem İmamoğlu, while advancing a Kurdish peace process designed to harvest votes for constitutional change — domestic manoeuvres that require a stable external environment. Mr Erdoğan needs to be seen as a mediator abroad precisely because his domestic consolidation depends on a region that does not force him to pick a camp.

What the week exposes is not the fragility of multi-alignment but its deepening entrenchment. Each government absorbed a shock — missiles, diplomatic ruptures, regional escalation — and responded by reinforcing, not abandoning, its network of hedged bets. The risk is not that this architecture collapses under a single blow but that its success encourages ever-greater brinkmanship from aggressors who calculate, correctly, that no coalition of fence-sitters will form a united front against them.
