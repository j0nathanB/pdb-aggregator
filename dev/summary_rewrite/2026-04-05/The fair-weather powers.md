The fair-weather powers                                                                                                                                      
                                                                                                                                                               
  Five weeks into Iran's war on the Gulf, a pattern is hardening that should unsettle anyone who believed the past decade's talk about middle-power agency. The
   governments that spent the 2020s branding themselves as indispensable hinges — hedgers, mediators, sovereign investors, reform showcases — are discovering  
  that the instruments they built for peacetime leverage do not convert into wartime power. A long war is a solvent. It is dissolving postures and revealing   
  which of these states built capacity and which built a story about themselves.                                                                               
                                                                                                                                                               
  Begin with the contradiction that makes the week impossible to read any other way. As Iranian missiles walked from the UAE's aluminium smelters onto its gas 
  plants — 2,700 projectiles this week alone, Habshan shut, one worker dead — Mubadala wired its share of a $575m round into an American fitness-tracker       
  company at a $10bn valuation. The Central Bank was simultaneously pumping AED 31bn into lenders exposed to Abu Dhabi property. A state that can in the same  
  week recapitalise its banks against a shooting war and underwrite a Silicon Valley unicorn is not confused; it is performing the only role it knows.    
  Sovereign wealth was the Emirati answer to every strategic question of the past twenty years, and Sheikh Mohammed bin Zayed is still answering with it,
  because the alternative is to admit that chequebook diplomacy has no reply to a ballistic missile.
                                                                                                                                                               
  Turkey shows the opposite move: a government that has understood the war as an opening, and is using it. While Mr Erdoğan shuttled between Mr Putin, Mr      
  Zelensky and NATO's Mr Rutte — the indispensable-broker routine that has become Ankara's brand — his coalition partner Devlet Bahçeli called for passing the 
  "Terror-free Turkey" framework that would clear the way to a fourth Erdoğan term. An Istanbul court released 18 defendants from the İmamoğlu trial but kept  
  the mayor himself inside. The mediation abroad is real; so is the constitutional spadework at home, and the two are the same project. A regional crisis gives
   a cornered ruler cover to do things that in peacetime would cost him. Mr Erdoğan is the only leader this week whose position has improved.
                                                                                                                                                               
  Saudi Arabia is the case where the war is stripping the paint. Prince Mohammed bin Salman is, privately, the war's keenest patron, calling it a "historic    
  opportunity" to break Tehran even as Donald Trump boasts to a Florida donor crowd that the crown prince is "kissing my ass". The same week, the kingdom      
  cancelled another $4.7bn of contracts for NEOM's Trojena ski resort and withdrew its 2029 Asian Winter Games bid. Vision 2030 was always a wager that oil    
  money could be spent twice — once on legitimacy abroad, once on transformation at home. A war it cannot afford to lose is forcing Prince Mohammed to choose,
  and Trojena is the first thing over the side. The Sky Sabre batteries arriving from Britain and the frantic calls by Prince Faisal bin Farhan to Pakistan,
  Kuwait, Russia, Japan and China describe a kingdom discovering that its security was an import all along.
                                                                                                                                                               
  India is the lone government this week that looks like a state rather than a brand. When the rupee broke 95 to the dollar under oil-price pressure from the  
  Strait of Hormuz, the Reserve Bank capped bank forex positions, banned certain forward contracts and dragged the currency back to 93.53 — its biggest weekly 
  gain in twelve years. Mr Modi chaired a run of Cabinet Committee on Security meetings; Mr Jaishankar worked the phones to Tehran, Doha and Abu Dhabi; Denis  
  Manturov arrived from Moscow with an offer of more oil and a $100bn trade target by 2030. A semiconductor fab opened in Gujarat, five state elections were
  being fought, a terror cell was rolled up in Punjab. None of this is graceful, but all of it is the behaviour of a government that owns its instruments
  rather than rents them. India is not a middle power having a good war; it is a state doing the ordinary, unglamorous work that the others confused with
  optional extras.
                                                                                                                                                               
  Pakistan is the warning that the prize can be the trap. General Asim Munir has made Islamabad the sole channel between Washington and Tehran, floated a      
  45-day ceasefire, and earned from Mr Trump the title of "favourite field marshal". The reward for this diplomatic coup was a frosty reception in Abu Dhabi,  
  where Emirati officials pointedly asked why Pakistan was brokering for the country shelling their gas plants — and hinted that $2bn of Gulf support was now  
  in play. At home the war arrived as a 42.7% petrol-price shock, protests in Lahore, a panicked 80-rupee cut and free buses for a month: the familiar    
  choreography of a government that has run out of room. Mediation looked like leverage until the bill came in from the patrons who actually pay Pakistan's
  rent.
                                                                                                                                                               
  The pattern is not that the war is hard on middle powers; wars are always hard. It is that this war is sorting them. The governments that mistook sovereign  
  wealth, brand diplomacy and megaproject theatre for the substance of statecraft are now spending those assets to buy time, and finding the exchange rate     
  cruel. The ones that built boring things — a central bank that can move a currency, an intelligence service that can roll up a cell, a cabinet that meets —  
  are absorbing the same shocks and still governing. Middle-power agency, it turns out, was never the posture. It was the plumbing underneath, and five weeks
  of Iranian missiles are showing who laid pipe and who hung a sign.
