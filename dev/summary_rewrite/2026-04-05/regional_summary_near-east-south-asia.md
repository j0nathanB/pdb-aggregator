**The Gulf War Next Door**

The Iran-UAE conflict is now the organising force of regional politics, and every government—belligerent or bystander—is discovering that the war's costs compound faster than its opportunities. Five weeks of Iranian bombardment have not produced a decisive military result, yet they have already reshaped energy infrastructure, currency markets, alliance politics and domestic governance from Abu Dhabi to Islamabad. Leaders who initially saw the crisis as a chance to advance their interests are finding that each week of fighting narrows their room for manoeuvre.

The UAE's response illustrates the trap most clearly. Crown Prince Khaled bin Mohamed's decision to take personal charge of ADNOC's contingency planning after strikes hit the Habshan gas facility and the Borouge petrochemical plant marks an escalation in political exposure: the country's leadership is now publicly identified with the resilience of specific industrial assets, a hostage to the next salvo. The Central Bank's injection of 31 billion dirhams into banks with heavy real-estate lending acknowledges that the war's second-order effects—investor flight, construction delays, insurance repricing—may prove costlier than the missiles themselves. Yet Mubadala still joined a $575m funding round for a Silicon Valley fitness firm, a sign less of confidence than of sovereign-wealth funds locked into deal pipelines they cannot easily pause.

Saudi Arabia's position is more contradictory still. Mohammed bin Salman reportedly calls the conflict a "historic opportunity" to weaken Tehran, yet his kingdom has quietly cancelled another $4.7bn in NEOM contracts and scrapped a bid for the 2029 Asian Winter Games—retrenchments that owe everything to the fiscal strain the war is deepening. Mr Trump's vulgar boast that the crown prince was "kissing my ass" laid bare the transactional core of the alliance at the worst possible moment, pushing Riyadh toward Britain's Sky Sabre air-defence systems and Ukrainian counter-drone expertise. A kingdom that once bought American loyalty with oil revenue now shops for protection on the open market, a demotion its leadership has not yet publicly acknowledged.

Turkey and India have positioned themselves as brokers rather than combatants, yet neither can insulate itself from the fallout. Mr Erdoğan's weekend marathon—Putin, Zelensky, Rutte, then his foreign minister in Damascus—advertised Ankara's diplomatic reach, but at home the constitutional manoeuvres to extend his presidency and the continued detention of Ekrem İmamoğlu suggest that regional mediation serves partly to distract from democratic erosion. India's Reserve Bank, meanwhile, resorted to emergency capital controls to arrest the rupee's slide below 95 to the dollar, a blunt admission that no amount of semiconductor inaugurations or "Naxal-free" declarations can offset a sustained energy-price shock. Mr Modi's frantic campaigning across five states on the eve of elections only underscores how quickly a distant Gulf war becomes a domestic political liability when you import 85% of your crude.

Pakistan's predicament is the most revealing. General Asim Munir has parlayed his country's back-channel to Tehran into rare American praise—Mr Trump called him "my favourite field marshal"—and a plausible claim to host ceasefire talks. But the UAE, which faces actual missiles, is openly questioning why Pakistan appears sympathetic to the aggressor, putting $2bn in Emirati financial support at risk. At home, a 43% fuel-price surge forced Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif into the familiar cycle of hike, protest and retreat, distributing relief through digital wallets as though a fintech fix could substitute for fiscal discipline. Mediation wins headlines; paying for the war's spillover costs power.

The pattern is plain. Each actor in this widening crisis believed it could extract advantage—strategic, diplomatic or commercial—from someone else's conflict. Five weeks on, the conflict is extracting advantage from all of them. Wars do not respect the neat categories of opportunity and cost that peacetime planners impose; they blur them, then erase them. The longer the fighting continues, the clearer it becomes that proximity to a war, whether measured in kilometres or in barrels of imported oil, is itself the decisive variable—and not one any of these governments can negotiate away.
