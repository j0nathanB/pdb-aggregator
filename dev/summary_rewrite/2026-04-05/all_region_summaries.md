## asia-pacific

**When the Oil Price Does the Diplomacy**

The week's events across the Indo-Pacific suggest a single, testable proposition: the Middle East energy shock is doing more to reshape alliances and domestic politics in the region than any strategic doctrine drafted in a foreign-ministry basement. Governments from Seoul to Canberra are using the crisis not merely to secure fuel supplies but to accelerate diplomatic diversification, ram through economic reforms and consolidate political power—moves that would have met stiffer resistance in calmer times.

South Korea illustrates the pattern most boldly. Lee Jae-myung declared a "wartime footing" and unveiled a 26.2 trillion won supplementary budget, yet the real story was the diplomacy that bookended it. Within days he had upgraded ties with France to a "Global Strategic Partnership," agreed to cooperate on keeping the Strait of Hormuz open and signed $10.2 billion in memorandums with Indonesia—calling Jakarta his "top strategic partner" in language plainly aimed at hedging against Middle Eastern suppliers. The opposition People Power Party, its approval at a miserable 18%, could do little but watch as Mr Lee floated constitutional amendments that would have been unthinkable a year ago. Energy panic, in short, has become the solvent for political obstacles that predated it.

Australia reveals the same dynamic from a different angle. Anthony Albanese delivered a prime-ministerial address of the sort usually reserved for war, urging fuel conservation and cutting excise—crisis theatre that wrong-footed an opposition already fractured over whether to engage with or merely oppose government policy. The Reserve Bank used the moment to announce its biggest payments overhaul in decades, banning card surcharges from 2026, while its board split 5-4 over rate rises driven by oil near $100 a barrel. The fuel shock has handed the government both a justification for intervention and a shield against criticism of it.

Taiwan and Japan show the strategic face of the same coin. Sanae Takaichi revealed that Japan had stockpiled eight months of oil and four months of naphtha, then sealed rare-earth and Hormuz-transit deals with France—quietly building redundancy against Chinese supply leverage. Taiwan's president inspected a fivefold increase in domestic missile production, yet the island's real vulnerability was political: the Nationalist Party chairwoman accepted Xi Jinping's invitation to visit Beijing even as her party blocked $40 billion in defence spending that American senators flew to Taipei to champion. Energy and security diversification proceed apace; the question is whether domestic politics can keep up.

Indonesia offers the most instructive case of a government turning crisis into opportunity without overreaching. Three peacekeepers died in Lebanon, oil hit $100, and President Prabowo Subianto responded by demanding a UN investigation, freezing subsidised fuel prices until 2026 and returning from Japan and South Korea with $34 billion in fresh commitments. Civil servants now work from home on Fridays to save money—a small, visible austerity that signals discipline without provoking unrest. The budget already assumed $100 oil, so the shock, paradoxically, validated the planners rather than panicking them.

The pattern is plain. Across the Indo-Pacific, governments are exploiting the energy crisis as an accelerant—for partnerships they already wanted, reforms they had shelved and political consolidations they could not previously justify. The danger is that expedience dressed as emergency becomes permanent, and that voters notice only after the oil price falls.

## frontline-eastern-europe

**When Allies Wobble, Small Wars Become Lonely**

The week across central and eastern Europe exposed a single uncomfortable truth: the countries most committed to resisting Russian aggression are discovering that their dependence on Western solidarity leaves them painfully exposed whenever that solidarity wavers. From Kyiv to Tallinn to Bucharest, governments found their room for manoeuvre shrinking—not because of anything Moscow did, but because allied attention, allied money and allied patience are finite resources now being drawn elsewhere.

Volodymyr Zelensky's whirlwind diplomacy illustrated the bind most starkly. His trip to Damascus and Istanbul, offering drone expertise in exchange for new partnerships, was not the confident outreach of a war leader flush with support; it was the hedging of one who senses his primary backer drifting. Mr Zelensky admitted as much, conceding that Ukraine is "not the priority for today" as Washington pivots toward Iran. The confirmation came swiftly: Kyrylo Budanov, who heads the presidential office, revealed that international partners had asked Ukraine to halt strikes on Russian oil refineries because the resulting price spikes hurt allied economies. A country fighting for survival now finds its target list edited by the fuel costs of countries nominally on its side. Ukraine's operational sovereignty, it turns out, extends only as far as its allies' petrol bills allow.

The Baltic states, meanwhile, learned that proximity to conflict carries risks no alliance charter quite anticipated. Ukrainian attack drones straying into Finnish, Estonian and Latvian airspace were an embarrassment for Kyiv but a revelation for the host nations. Finland's prime minister acknowledged defence gaps; Estonia scrambled to explain why allied fighters tracked but did not shoot down the intruders; Latvia triggered emergency alerts over a drone that turned back before crossing its border. Each incident was minor in isolation, yet together they demonstrated that NATO's north-eastern members lack the air-defence depth their geography demands—and that Washington, the alliance's indispensable armourer, is sending what Hanno Pevkur, Estonia's defence minister, called "mixed signals" about what it actually wants from them.

Domestic institutions buckled under the strain as readily as military ones. In Poland, President Nawrocki's refusal to swear in four of six Constitutional Tribunal judges picked by parliament opened a constitutional crisis that could paralyse judicial reform until 2027—an act of institutional sabotage dressed up as procedural scruple. In the Czech Republic, Prime Minister Babiš excluded President Pavel from the NATO summit delegation, a raw assertion of executive control over foreign policy that broke precedent and may yet break trust. Romania's governing coalition, already fragile, absorbed a €600m court judgment over refused Pfizer vaccines, handing the opposition a fiscal grenade just as the central bank warned it had spent over a billion euros defending the leu. Each government faces external pressure from the war economy; each is simultaneously undermining itself from within.

The pattern is not mere coincidence. These countries chose, rightly, to stake their security on Western alliances and their prosperity on European integration. But alliances work only when the strongest members remain engaged, and integration protects only when domestic governance holds firm. This week showed both conditions fraying at once. American attention wandered toward the Middle East. European economic solidarity bent under energy-price anxiety. And national politics—in Warsaw, Prague, Bucharest and Kyiv alike—proved that even the most justified external commitments cannot survive indefinitely on shaky institutional ground. The frontline states of Europe are learning a hard lesson: solidarity is not a stockpile you draw down in wartime but a living thing that must be fed, and the feeding depends on others' appetites.

## near-east-south-asia

**The Gulf War Next Door**

The week's events across the Middle East and South Asia share a single, testable claim: the Iran-UAE conflict is now the organising force of regional politics, and every government—belligerent or bystander—is discovering that the war's costs compound faster than its opportunities. Five weeks of Iranian bombardment have not produced a decisive military result, yet they have already reshaped energy infrastructure, currency markets, alliance politics and domestic governance from Abu Dhabi to Islamabad. Leaders who initially saw the crisis as a chance to advance their interests are finding that each week of fighting narrows their room for manoeuvre.

The UAE's response illustrates the trap most clearly. Crown Prince Khaled bin Mohamed's decision to take personal charge of ADNOC's contingency planning after strikes hit the Habshan gas facility and the Borouge petrochemical plant marks an escalation in political exposure: the country's leadership is now publicly identified with the resilience of specific industrial assets, a hostage to the next salvo. The Central Bank's injection of 31 billion dirhams into banks with heavy real-estate lending acknowledges that the war's second-order effects—investor flight, construction delays, insurance repricing—may prove costlier than the missiles themselves. Yet Mubadala still joined a $575m funding round for a Silicon Valley fitness firm, a sign less of confidence than of sovereign-wealth funds locked into deal pipelines they cannot easily pause.

Saudi Arabia's position is more contradictory still. Mohammed bin Salman reportedly calls the conflict a "historic opportunity" to weaken Tehran, yet his kingdom has quietly cancelled another $4.7bn in NEOM contracts and scrapped a bid for the 2029 Asian Winter Games—retrenchments that owe everything to the fiscal strain the war is deepening. Mr Trump's vulgar boast that the crown prince was "kissing my ass" laid bare the transactional core of the alliance at the worst possible moment, pushing Riyadh toward Britain's Sky Sabre air-defence systems and Ukrainian counter-drone expertise. A kingdom that once bought American loyalty with oil revenue now shops for protection on the open market, a demotion its leadership has not yet publicly acknowledged.

Turkey and India have positioned themselves as brokers rather than combatants, yet neither can insulate itself from the fallout. Mr Erdoğan's weekend marathon—Putin, Zelensky, Rutte, then his foreign minister in Damascus—advertised Ankara's diplomatic reach, but at home the constitutional manoeuvres to extend his presidency and the continued detention of Ekrem İmamoğlu suggest that regional mediation serves partly to distract from democratic erosion. India's Reserve Bank, meanwhile, resorted to emergency capital controls to arrest the rupee's slide below 95 to the dollar, a blunt admission that no amount of semiconductor inaugurations or "Naxal-free" declarations can offset a sustained energy-price shock. Mr Modi's frantic campaigning across five states on the eve of elections only underscores how quickly a distant Gulf war becomes a domestic political liability when you import 85% of your crude.

Pakistan's predicament is the most revealing. General Asim Munir has parlayed his country's back-channel to Tehran into rare American praise—Mr Trump called him "my favourite field marshal"—and a plausible claim to host ceasefire talks. But the UAE, which faces actual missiles, is openly questioning why Pakistan appears sympathetic to the aggressor, putting $2bn in Emirati financial support at risk. At home, a 43% fuel-price surge forced Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif into the familiar cycle of hike, protest and retreat, distributing relief through digital wallets as though a fintech fix could substitute for fiscal discipline. Mediation wins headlines; paying for the war's spillover costs power.

The pattern is plain. Each actor in this widening crisis believed it could extract advantage—strategic, diplomatic or commercial—from someone else's conflict. Five weeks on, the conflict is extracting advantage from all of them. Wars do not respect the neat categories of opportunity and cost that peacetime planners impose; they blur them, then erase them. The longer the fighting continues, the clearer it becomes that proximity to a war, whether measured in kilometres or in barrels of imported oil, is itself the decisive variable—and not one any of these governments can negotiate away.

## the-americas

**The Populist Grip Tightens When It Should Loosen**

Latin America's biggest economies, and the neighbours who orbit them, spent this week demonstrating a single uncomfortable truth: leaders who came to power promising to break old patterns are instead deepening the most dangerous one—the concentration of executive will over institutions meant to resist it. Whether left or right, civilian or military-adjacent, each government reached for the same lever: direct presidential command over prices, appointments, courts and corporations, treating checks on power as obstacles rather than architecture.

Brazil offers the starkest case. President Lula's public tantrum at Petrobras—branding routine fuel-price adjustments "stupidity and banditry" and threatening to cancel gas auctions—is not a policy disagreement but an assertion that the president sets pump prices. Petrobras is a listed company with minority shareholders and a governance framework rebuilt after the Lava Jato scandals. Mr Lula treats it as a ministry. His fury is sharpened by electoral arithmetic: polls show a dead heat with Flávio Bolsonaro in a 2026 runoff, and up to 17 ministers may resign to campaign. A president who cannot afford rising diesel costs before an election is a president who will subordinate corporate governance to the political calendar. Meanwhile the Supreme Court, theoretically the republic's referee, faces its own credibility crisis, with Mr Lula's nominee blocked and two sitting justices under corruption scrutiny. The institution meant to check the executive can barely check itself.

Chile's new president, José Antonio Kast, is building a different kind of concentration—military rather than partisan, but concentration all the same. Retired officers and police now occupy intelligence, defence and divisional posts across government at a density unseen since the return to democracy. The symbolism of Chilean pilots refuelling American F-35s is less about interoperability with Washington than about signalling that the armed forces are back inside the state. Mr Kast's approval rating has already slid to 42%, dragged down by a decision to sack the head of the National Women's Service while she undergoes cancer treatment—a blunder that managed to unite his own coalition against him. His response to fuel-price anger was a $225m subsidy package: different in ideology from Mr Lula's Petrobras intervention, identical in impulse. Record profits at Codelco, the state copper company, buy him time, but his quiet cancellation of a promised international audit of the previous government suggests that accountability is optional when it becomes inconvenient.

In Mexico, the central bank's 3-2 vote to cut rates despite inflation running at 4.63% reveals a board that has chosen growth over its mandate, a judgment sharpened by the knowledge that Pemex owes American firms more than $2.5bn on the eve of the USMCA trade review. President Claudia Sheinbaum reshuffled six cabinet posts in a single week and slotted a North America specialist into the foreign ministry after her minister's medical departure—efficient, yet also a reminder that Mexican institutional continuity depends on one person's preferences. That her security secretary, Omar García Harfuch, is now merchandised on towels and blankets as "Mexico's Batman" is comic, but the cult of the capable strongman is never only comic: it is the cultural soil in which executive overreach grows. Meanwhile in Canada, Mark Carney's whispered boast that he could "outlast" a mercury-poisoned Indigenous protester was a small cruelty, yet it captured something larger—the irritation of a leader who sees dissent as a scheduling problem, not a signal.

The pattern across these four countries is not ideological. Mr Lula governs from the left, Mr Kast from the right, Ms Sheinbaum from a populist centre and Mr Carney from a technocratic liberal tradition. What they share is the instinct to absorb rather than tolerate friction—to set prices by fiat, appoint loyalists over objections, cut rates against evidence, and treat institutions as instruments of the executive rather than counterweights to it. Each decision is defensible in isolation; taken together they describe a hemisphere in which the machinery of restraint is running, but no longer engaged.

## western-europe

# The Centre Cannot Hold

**Headline: Europe's mainstream parties are not being defeated by populists so much as hollowed out by their own contradictions**

The week's events across Europe suggest something more troubling than the familiar story of populist insurgency: the continent's governing parties are losing authority not because their opponents are strong, but because they themselves have become incoherent. From Berlin to Stockholm, centrist leaders are stumbling over self-inflicted crises, diplomatic blunders and institutional compromises that undermine the very credibility they claim to defend. The real danger is not that the far right is rising — it is that the mainstream has forgotten how to govern.

Germany offers the starkest case. Friedrich Merz's coalition has collapsed to a 15% approval rating, yet the AfD's overtaking of the Union in polls owes less to any populist masterstroke than to the chancellor's own fumbles. Mr Merz publicly claimed that Syria's president had proposed returning 80% of refugees, only for Ahmed al-Scharaa to contradict him in London. A government spokesman waved this away as a "sham conflict," a phrase that neatly captures the administration's relationship with facts. Meanwhile, the CDU is mired in a deepfake scandal in Niedersachsen and a Wehrmacht-nostalgia incident in Brandenburg — problems that suggest not a party under siege from the right, but one incapable of policing its own ranks.

Italy's Giorgia Meloni has managed things more deftly, but her week exposed the limits of improvisation in the absence of strategy. Blocking American bombers from Sigonella while touring Gulf capitals was tactically clever — it signalled independence to Arab partners without formally breaking with NATO. Yet calling it "technical compliance" fools nobody, and the contradiction between claiming alliance loyalty and denying operational access will eventually demand a reckoning. With Fratelli d'Italia slipping in polls and Marina Berlusconi circling Antonio Tajani's leadership of the coalition partner Forza Italia, Ms Meloni's balancing act grows more precarious. The central bank's warning of possible recession and 2.6% inflation from the Iran crisis leaves her little room for further acrobatics.

France illustrates how mainstream weakness feeds extremism more efficiently than extremist strength does. Polling showing Édouard Philippe narrowly beating Jordan Bardella in a hypothetical second round sounds reassuring until you note that Mr Bardella commands a 13-point first-round lead and the left is busy destroying itself. The Communist leader Fabien Roussel declared "a rupture" with Jean-Luc Mélenchon's France Unbowed, ensuring that left-wing fragmentation will deliver Mr Bardella an easier path. Emmanuel Macron, meanwhile, spent four days in Asia promoting "strategic autonomy" while his domestic political order crumbles. The president's detachment from France's gravest electoral challenge since 1958 is itself a symptom of the governing class's inability to prioritise.

In Scandinavia, the pattern repeats with Nordic precision. Sweden's Ulf Kristersson has promised the Sweden Democrats cabinet seats after next year's election, completing a decade-long migration from cordon sanitaire to coalition partner — a journey driven less by SD's brilliance than by the Moderates' need for a majority. In Norway, Jens Stoltenberg's memoir admission that he secretly proposed discussing NATO troop withdrawals from Eastern Europe with Russia in 2021 — without consulting the Baltic states — reveals that even the alliance's most trusted stewards were improvising behind closed doors. Jonas Gahr Støre's government then lost a parliamentary vote on fuel taxes to a cross-party revolt, yet the prime minister blithely announced he would seek re-election in 2029.

The pattern across Europe this week is not polarisation but enfeeblement. Governing parties are not losing arguments to populists; they are losing the capacity to make arguments at all. Diplomatic blunders go unexplained, coalition partners drift into rivalry, fiscal discipline yields to parliamentary ambush, and leaders treat foreign travel as a substitute for domestic authority. When the centre empties itself of substance, it should not be surprised to find others filling the space.
