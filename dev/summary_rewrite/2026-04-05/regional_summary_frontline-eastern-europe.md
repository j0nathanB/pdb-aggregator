**When Allies Wobble, Small Wars Become Lonely**

The week across central and eastern Europe exposed a single uncomfortable truth: the countries most committed to resisting Russian aggression are discovering that their dependence on Western solidarity leaves them painfully exposed whenever that solidarity wavers. From Kyiv to Tallinn to Bucharest, governments found their room for manoeuvre shrinking—not because of anything Moscow did, but because allied attention, allied money and allied patience are finite resources now being drawn elsewhere.

Volodymyr Zelensky's whirlwind diplomacy illustrated the bind most starkly. His trip to Damascus and Istanbul, offering drone expertise in exchange for new partnerships, was not the confident outreach of a war leader flush with support; it was the hedging of one who senses his primary backer drifting. Mr Zelensky admitted as much, conceding that Ukraine is "not the priority for today" as Washington pivots toward Iran. The confirmation came swiftly: Kyrylo Budanov, who heads the presidential office, revealed that international partners had asked Ukraine to halt strikes on Russian oil refineries because the resulting price spikes hurt allied economies. A country fighting for survival now finds its target list edited by the fuel costs of countries nominally on its side. Ukraine's operational sovereignty, it turns out, extends only as far as its allies' petrol bills allow.

The Baltic states, meanwhile, learned that proximity to conflict carries risks no alliance charter quite anticipated. Ukrainian attack drones straying into Finnish, Estonian and Latvian airspace were an embarrassment for Kyiv but a revelation for the host nations. Finland's prime minister acknowledged defence gaps; Estonia scrambled to explain why allied fighters tracked but did not shoot down the intruders; Latvia triggered emergency alerts over a drone that turned back before crossing its border. Each incident was minor in isolation, yet together they demonstrated that NATO's north-eastern members lack the air-defence depth their geography demands—and that Washington, the alliance's indispensable armourer, is sending what Hanno Pevkur, Estonia's defence minister, called "mixed signals" about what it actually wants from them.

Domestic institutions buckled under the strain as readily as military ones. In Poland, President Nawrocki's refusal to swear in four of six Constitutional Tribunal judges picked by parliament opened a constitutional crisis that could paralyse judicial reform until 2027—an act of institutional sabotage dressed up as procedural scruple. In the Czech Republic, Prime Minister Babiš excluded President Pavel from the NATO summit delegation, a raw assertion of executive control over foreign policy that broke precedent and may yet break trust. Romania's governing coalition, already fragile, absorbed a €600m court judgment over refused Pfizer vaccines, handing the opposition a fiscal grenade just as the central bank warned it had spent over a billion euros defending the leu. Each government faces external pressure from the war economy; each is simultaneously undermining itself from within.

The pattern is not mere coincidence. These countries chose, rightly, to stake their security on Western alliances and their prosperity on European integration. But alliances work only when the strongest members remain engaged, and integration protects only when domestic governance holds firm. This week showed both conditions fraying at once. American attention wandered toward the Middle East. European economic solidarity bent under energy-price anxiety. And national politics—in Warsaw, Prague, Bucharest and Kyiv alike—proved that even the most justified external commitments cannot survive indefinitely on shaky institutional ground. The frontline states of Europe are learning a hard lesson: solidarity is not a stockpile you draw down in wartime but a living thing that must be fed, and the feeding depends on others' appetites.
