**The Populist Grip Tightens When It Should Loosen**

Latin America's biggest economies, and the neighbours who orbit them, spent this week demonstrating a single uncomfortable truth: leaders who came to power promising to break old patterns are instead deepening the most dangerous one—the concentration of executive will over institutions meant to resist it. Whether left or right, civilian or military-adjacent, each government reached for the same lever: direct presidential command over prices, appointments, courts and corporations, treating checks on power as obstacles rather than architecture.

Brazil offers the starkest case. President Lula's public tantrum at Petrobras—branding routine fuel-price adjustments "stupidity and banditry" and threatening to cancel gas auctions—is not a policy disagreement but an assertion that the president sets pump prices. Petrobras is a listed company with minority shareholders and a governance framework rebuilt after the Lava Jato scandals. Mr Lula treats it as a ministry. His fury is sharpened by electoral arithmetic: polls show a dead heat with Flávio Bolsonaro in a 2026 runoff, and up to 17 ministers may resign to campaign. A president who cannot afford rising diesel costs before an election is a president who will subordinate corporate governance to the political calendar. Meanwhile the Supreme Court, theoretically the republic's referee, faces its own credibility crisis, with Mr Lula's nominee blocked and two sitting justices under corruption scrutiny. The institution meant to check the executive can barely check itself.

Chile's new president, José Antonio Kast, is building a different kind of concentration—military rather than partisan, but concentration all the same. Retired officers and police now occupy intelligence, defence and divisional posts across government at a density unseen since the return to democracy. The symbolism of Chilean pilots refuelling American F-35s is less about interoperability with Washington than about signalling that the armed forces are back inside the state. Mr Kast's approval rating has already slid to 42%, dragged down by a decision to sack the head of the National Women's Service while she undergoes cancer treatment—a blunder that managed to unite his own coalition against him. His response to fuel-price anger was a $225m subsidy package: different in ideology from Mr Lula's Petrobras intervention, identical in impulse. Record profits at Codelco, the state copper company, buy him time, but his quiet cancellation of a promised international audit of the previous government suggests that accountability is optional when it becomes inconvenient.

In Mexico, the central bank's 3-2 vote to cut rates despite inflation running at 4.63% reveals a board that has chosen growth over its mandate, a judgment sharpened by the knowledge that Pemex owes American firms more than $2.5bn on the eve of the USMCA trade review. President Claudia Sheinbaum reshuffled six cabinet posts in a single week and slotted a North America specialist into the foreign ministry after her minister's medical departure—efficient, yet also a reminder that Mexican institutional continuity depends on one person's preferences. That her security secretary, Omar García Harfuch, is now merchandised on towels and blankets as "Mexico's Batman" is comic, but the cult of the capable strongman is never only comic: it is the cultural soil in which executive overreach grows. Meanwhile in Canada, Mark Carney's whispered boast that he could "outlast" a mercury-poisoned Indigenous protester was a small cruelty, yet it captured something larger—the irritation of a leader who sees dissent as a scheduling problem, not a signal.

The pattern across these four countries is not ideological. Mr Lula governs from the left, Mr Kast from the right, Ms Sheinbaum from a populist centre and Mr Carney from a technocratic liberal tradition. What they share is the instinct to absorb rather than tolerate friction—to set prices by fiat, appoint loyalists over objections, cut rates against evidence, and treat institutions as instruments of the executive rather than counterweights to it. Each decision is defensible in isolation; taken together they describe a hemisphere in which the machinery of restraint is running, but no longer engaged.
