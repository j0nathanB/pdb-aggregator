# The Authority Surplus

**When leaders accumulate power faster than the institutions, alliances and markets that must sustain it, the result is not strength but fragility dressed in a landslide's clothing.**

From Tokyo to Ottawa, from Tallinn to Abu Dhabi, this week's headlines share an underlying arithmetic: governments are acquiring political authority—through elections, approval ratings, executive decrees or sheer transactional guile—at a pace that outstrips the institutional scaffolding needed to make that authority durable. The mismatch is not incidental. It is the defining feature of the current moment, and it is visible on every continent that held the world's attention this week. Power is being concentrated not because strongmen are seizing it but because voters, markets and alliance partners are handing it over—often enthusiastically—without anyone pausing to ask what happens when the invoices arrive.

Japan's Sanae Takaichi, commanding 316 of 465 lower-house seats, holds the largest single-party majority in postwar history, yet her mandate rests partly on a White House invitation and a Nikkei record high that flatter a programme of stimulus promises Japan's debt burden cannot indefinitely absorb. A two-thirds supermajority removes every legislative excuse; it also removes every coalition partner large enough to share blame. Indonesia's Prabowo Subianto enjoys 80% approval yet his state investment vehicle, Danantara, triggered the worst two-day stock-market rout since 1997, drew a Moody's downgrade and sent foreign capital fleeing—then ordered asset managers to buy shares, a contradiction so stark it could serve as a textbook illustration of the gap between popularity and credibility. South Korea's Lee Jae-myung governs by social-media ultimatum, summoning chaebol chairmen and extracting pledges while foreign investors dump 2.2 trillion won. In each case the mandate is real, the capacity to convert it into durable policy conspicuously unproven.

The same pattern, refracted through diplomacy rather than elections, defines the arc from New Delhi to the Gulf. India's trade deal with Washington—tariffs slashed from 50% to 18%, Quad frameworks deepened—delivers concrete gains but quietly narrows the strategic ambiguity Mr Modi has long prized. Turkey's Mr Erdogan courts the imprisoned PKK leader to secure Kurdish votes for constitutional amendments while prosecuting Istanbul's mayor on espionage charges: two halves of a single transaction that requires courting and crushing democratic constituencies in the same week. Saudi Arabia privately urges America to strike Iran while publicly refusing the use of its airspace, a posture sustainable only until one side calls the bluff. Abu Dhabi's $500 million stake in the Trump family's cryptocurrency venture is not hedging but direct financial entanglement with an administration, dressed up as sovereignty. Each middle power is extracting deals, arms and relevance at a pace unthinkable a decade ago—and each transaction creates a dependency that narrows future room for manoeuvre.

Eastern Europe, supposedly unified by the existential threat on its border, reveals how quickly wartime solidarity frays when the bills come due. Estonia's president broke with his own foreign minister over whether Ukraine should consider territorial concessions—an institutional split, not an ideological one, which makes it harder to dismiss. Lithuania's new prime minister called the Taiwanese representative office a mistake and signalled rapprochement with Beijing barely four years after opening it in principled defiance. Poland's president and prime minister are feuding over who defines alliance loyalty, even as Mr Tusk signs weapons-production deals in Kyiv. The Czech government survived a no-confidence vote while quietly delaying aircraft transfers to Ukraine under the guise of maintenance. Each country still arms and spends, but the political superstructure holding the hawkish consensus together is bending under the weight of duration.

Western Europe displays the sharpest version of the contradiction: governments projecting strength outward while haemorrhaging authority at home. Germany has stationed its first permanent combat brigade abroad since 1945, yet the CDU trails the AfD in polls and business leaders write letters of "blank horror" at Mr Merz's economic stewardship. Britain's defence machinery hums—Arctic missions, helicopter deals, steady interest rates—while the prime minister's chief of staff and communications director resign in the same week. France has placed record military orders of 38 billion euros but cannot pass a budget without invoking Article 49.3 to bypass parliament. Canada scraps its electric-vehicle mandate, replaces it with billions in rebates, and claws back subsidies from General Motors for responding to the market signals Ottawa now claims to trust. Brazil's president declares his re-election campaign a "war" while hedging simultaneously against China, Washington and Caracas. Chile's new conservative leader builds an emergency technocratic government because the congress he cannot control leaves him no other option.

The pattern, once named, is difficult to unsee. Across five continents this week, political leaders are accumulating mandates, approval ratings and executive prerogatives faster than they are building the coalitions, market credibility, institutional checks or alliance resilience those instruments of power require. Mandates this large tend to be self-consuming: they raise expectations they cannot meet, foreclose the compromises that extend political life, and leave no one else to blame when the next shock—a market rout, an alliance demand, a constitutional collision—exposes the gap between authority granted and authority earned. The world is not short of powerful leaders. It is short of leaders whose power can outlast the season in which it was won.
