**The Dealmakers' Dilemma**

The week's diplomacy across the arc from New Delhi to Abu Dhabi reveals a single, uncomfortable truth: middle powers are discovering that the transactional relationships they cultivate with Washington yield impressive short-term returns yet deepen the contradictions they must eventually resolve. Each government displayed virtuosity in playing multiple sides at once — and each, on closer inspection, paid a price in coherence that will compound over time.

India's trade deal with the United States is the most striking exhibit. By cutting American tariffs from 50% to 18% and opening markets for industrial and agricultural goods, Narendra Modi's government demonstrated that multi-alignment can deliver concrete results: the Bombay Stock Exchange cheered, the Reserve Bank of India held rates steady on rosier growth forecasts, and Adani Group shares surged. Yet the deal's fine print — rules of origin, non-tariff barrier provisions, deeper institutional partnership through the Quad — quietly narrows the space for the strategic ambiguity India has long prized. A country that defines itself by refusing to choose is signing frameworks that look remarkably like choices. Meanwhile, Parliament's descent into suspension-and-protest chaos over unpublished memoirs about India-China tensions illustrated a domestic politics incapable of debating what those choices actually mean.

Turkey's ruling coalition offers a darker version of the same logic. President Recep Tayyip Erdogan's partners are simultaneously extending an olive branch to the imprisoned PKK leader Abdullah Ocalan — with the nationalist MHP's Devlet Bahceli calling for his "hope right" and the release of the jailed former HDP leader Selahattin Demirtas — while tightening the judicial noose around Istanbul's mayor, Ekrem Imamoglu, with espionage charges carrying up to 20 years. The Kurdish peace process and the opposition's legal persecution are not contradictions; they are two halves of a single transaction. Kurdish political cooperation is the price of constitutional amendments that could extend Mr Erdogan's rule beyond 2028, and the elimination of Mr Imamoglu clears the electoral field. That this bargain requires courting and crushing different democratic constituencies in the same week speaks to a regime whose transactionalism has become its organising principle. Central bank reserves hitting a record $218 billion merely confirm that economic orthodoxy, too, survives only at the president's pleasure.

The Gulf states carry transactional diplomacy to its most refined — and most exposed — extreme. Saudi Arabia's Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman privately urged American officials not to back away from striking Iran, while publicly rejecting the use of Saudi territory or airspace for any such attack: the dual message is less strategic ambiguity than strategic duplicity, sustainable only so long as neither Washington nor Tehran calls the bluff. The fracturing Saudi-UAE relationship, visible in the withdrawal of some 30 Emirati defence firms from Saudi Arabia's World Defence Show, shows what happens when two transactional powers run out of transactions that benefit both. The split, rooted in Yemen and sharpened by a Saudi strike on a UAE-linked weapons shipment, has migrated from diplomacy to commerce — a pattern last seen in the Qatar blockade. Vision 2030's quiet retrenchment, with NEOM's consultants cut by 90% and the Line shrunk from 170 kilometres to a 2.4-kilometre pilot, suggests the crown prince is discovering that even sovereign wealth funds worth $930 billion cannot finance every side of every bet.

Abu Dhabi, for its part, has wagered most explicitly on the Trump family itself. Sheikh Tahnoun bin Zayed's $500 million purchase of a 49% stake in World Liberty Financial, the cryptocurrency venture of the president's relatives, represents not hedging but direct financial entanglement with an American administration. Pair that with OpenAI's collaboration with the Abu Dhabi-backed G42 on a government-tailored ChatGPT — complete with content restrictions aligned with monarchical sensitivities — and the UAE's strategy comes into focus: buy access, localise the technology, and call it sovereignty. Yet President Mohammed bin Zayed's postponement of a Japan visit over US-Iran tensions, and his diplomat Anwar Gargash's plea that the region needs no more confrontation, betray the anxiety underneath. A country hosting Russia-Ukraine-America peace talks while holding a crypto stake in the first family is not so much balancing as juggling.

The pattern is now plain. From New Delhi to Ankara to Riyadh to Abu Dhabi, ambitious middle powers are leveraging a fragmenting global order to extract deals, arms, technology and diplomatic relevance at a pace that would have been unthinkable a decade ago. But each transaction creates a dependency, each hedge narrows future room for manoeuvre, and each contradiction quietly accumulates. The question is not whether this model works — this week proves it does — but whether it can survive the moment when the parties on the other side of these bargains begin comparing notes.
