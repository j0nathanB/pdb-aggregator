## asia-pacific

**The Mandate Trap**

A week of landslide mandates and muscular state action across the Indo-Pacific suggests not that democratic governance is thriving but that concentrated executive power is becoming the region's default setting—and that the gap between political authority and sound policymaking is widening, not narrowing.

Japan's Sanae Takaichi now commands the largest single-party majority in postwar history, with 316 of 465 lower-house seats, yet the very scale of her triumph conceals a fragility. Mr Trump's pre-election endorsement and a White House invitation lend her a transactional legitimacy that depends on continued American goodwill—a commodity whose price has risen sharply. The Nikkei's record high flatters a mandate built partly on stimulus promises that must eventually collide with Japan's debt burden. A two-thirds supermajority removes legislative obstacles but also removes excuses: Ms Takaichi now owns every consequence, from constitutional revision to any friction with Beijing, with no coalition partner large enough to share the blame.

Indonesia offers the starkest warning of what happens when an outsized mandate meets unchecked ambition. President Prabowo Subianto's approval rating sits near 80%, yet his state-directed investment vehicle, Danantara, triggered the worst two-day stock-market rout since the Asian financial crisis of 1997-98. MSCI questioned transparency; Moody's cut the credit outlook to negative; foreign capital fled. That Danantara responded by ordering asset managers to buy shares and simultaneously broke ground on $7 billion of downstream projects captures the contradiction perfectly: the same institution meant to marshal national wealth is frightening the private capital it needs. Mr Prabowo's coalition has already endorsed him for a second term, but popularity earned before a crisis is a wasting asset once the crisis arrives.

South Korea's Lee Jae-myung is testing the same formula more deftly but no less aggressively. Approval at 63% has emboldened him to summon chaebol chairmen and extract 300 trillion won in regional investment pledges while publicly rebuking a finance minister for the sin of equivocation. His campaign to make holding multiple properties "impossible" is popular, but governing by social-media ultimatum risks policy by spectacle. Meanwhile a US congressional subpoena of Coupang and an investigation into Korean regulatory agencies hint that Washington's transactional framework prizes reciprocity over alliance loyalty—a constraint no domestic approval rating can override. Retail investors may be defending the KOSPI with 4.5 trillion won in net purchases, but foreign sellers dumping 2.2 trillion won suggest that external confidence is less easily conscripted.

Taiwan and Australia, in different ways, illustrate how even leaders without supermajorities are losing the buffers that once restrained executive overreach—or at least distributed its costs. In Taipei, President Lai Ching-te calls relations with Washington "rock solid" even as TSMC's decision to produce 3nm chips in Japan quietly dissolves the silicon shield that underpinned Taiwan's strategic indispensability. The opposition KMT's freelance engagement with Beijing and the legislature's blockade of the defence budget leave Mr Lai strong in rhetoric but hemmed in institutionally. In Canberra, the Reserve Bank's rate rise—unique among major economies—lands on a prime minister whose political comfort owes more to an opposition in freefall (the Coalition's primary vote has sunk to 18%, behind One Nation) than to any affirmative mandate. Mr Albanese is deepening security ties with Jakarta and selling off 67 defence sites to fund modernisation, yet a US Congressional Research Service report openly muses about withholding AUKUS submarines unless Australia commits to supporting America in a Taiwan contingency. Strength borrowed from a rival's weakness is not strength earned.

The pattern across these five capitals is consistent: leaders are accumulating political authority faster than they are building the institutional checks, market credibility or alliance resilience to sustain it. Mandates this large tend to be self-consuming. The question is not whether voters have granted power—they plainly have—but whether any of these governments can convert a permission slip into durable policy before the next shock, domestic or external, reveals how thin the margin really is.

## frontline-eastern-europe

# The Hardliners' Wobble

The week's news from central and eastern Europe points to a single, uncomfortable conclusion: the hawkish consensus that has unified the region since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine is fracturing—not because the threat has receded, but because the political costs of sustaining it have grown too high for any single institution to bear alone.

Estonia offers the clearest crack. President Alar Karis broke publicly with his own government by suggesting Ukraine should decide for itself about territorial concessions and calling for EU envoy participation in talks with Moscow. Foreign Minister Margus Tsahkna rejected the position within hours. This was no idle disagreement: Estonia spends 5% of GDP on defence, has blacklisted more than a thousand Russian fighters, and styles itself as the EU's most consistent Russia hawk. Yet even in Tallinn, the presidency now questions whether threat-maximalism is sustainable as a permanent posture. The split is institutional, not ideological—Mr Karis is no dove—which makes it harder to dismiss.

Lithuania's reversal is blunter still. Prime Minister Inga Ruginienė called her country's decision to host a Taiwanese representative office a mistake that cost Lithuania dearly, and signalled "first small steps" toward restoring relations with Beijing. The Chinese foreign ministry responded warmly. Lithuania opened that office in 2021 as an act of principled defiance; barely four years later, the new government treats it as a cautionary tale about acting without allies. The episode reveals how quickly small states recalculate when the diplomatic bills come due—even as NATO's multinational battlegroup near the Belarusian border swells to 5,000 troops and the three Baltic states dismantle bureaucratic barriers to cross-border military movement. Defence integration deepens at the very moment diplomatic bravado retreats.

Poland shows the pattern from the other direction: not a fracture in foreign consensus but an institutional collision over who controls the narrative. President Karol Nawrocki summoned a National Security Council meeting to investigate the Sejm Speaker's alleged "eastern contacts," aligning himself with the American ambassador's decision to sever contact with the same official. Prime Minister Donald Tusk fired back, insisting that "allies should respect rather than lecture each other." The dispute is nominally about one politician's associations, but its real subject is whether Washington or Warsaw defines what loyalty to the alliance requires. Mr Tusk's simultaneous trip to Kyiv—where he signed letters of intent for joint weapons production with Ukraine—demonstrated that Poland intends to be a defence-industrial partner, not a compliant client. Cohabitation has turned Poland's security establishment into a contested arena rather than a unified front.

Even the Czech Republic, where a new Babiš government survived a no-confidence vote and protests continued in support of President Petr Pavel, suggests accommodation rather than confrontation may prevail. Mr Babiš's coalition quietly delayed the transfer of L-159 aircraft to Ukraine under the guise of maintenance, while using creative accounting to claim defence spending of 2.07% of GDP when the real figure is closer to 1.7%. The Czech National Bank, meanwhile, held rates steady, and bond markets shrugged at a modestly wider deficit. Institutional independence at the central bank provides a stability anchor that political theatre above cannot dislodge—but the slow dilution of Ukraine commitments is real, even if it is dressed in technical language.

The pattern across the region is not a retreat from the West or a turn toward Moscow. It is something more subtle and, for NATO planners, potentially more troubling: the discovery that wartime solidarity is easier to declare than to institutionalise. Presidents quarrel with foreign ministers, prime ministers push back against ambassadors, coalition arithmetic quietly trims defence pledges, and diplomatic gambles are recast as errors. Each country still arms, trains and spends. But the political superstructure that held eastern Europe's hawkish consensus together is bending under the weight of duration, cost and competing ambitions. Unity, it turns out, is a depreciating asset.

## near-east-south-asia

**The Dealmakers' Dilemma**

The week's diplomacy across the arc from New Delhi to Abu Dhabi reveals a single, uncomfortable truth: middle powers are discovering that the transactional relationships they cultivate with Washington yield impressive short-term returns yet deepen the contradictions they must eventually resolve. Each government displayed virtuosity in playing multiple sides at once — and each, on closer inspection, paid a price in coherence that will compound over time.

India's trade deal with the United States is the most striking exhibit. By cutting American tariffs from 50% to 18% and opening markets for industrial and agricultural goods, Narendra Modi's government demonstrated that multi-alignment can deliver concrete results: the Bombay Stock Exchange cheered, the Reserve Bank of India held rates steady on rosier growth forecasts, and Adani Group shares surged. Yet the deal's fine print — rules of origin, non-tariff barrier provisions, deeper institutional partnership through the Quad — quietly narrows the space for the strategic ambiguity India has long prized. A country that defines itself by refusing to choose is signing frameworks that look remarkably like choices. Meanwhile, Parliament's descent into suspension-and-protest chaos over unpublished memoirs about India-China tensions illustrated a domestic politics incapable of debating what those choices actually mean.

Turkey's ruling coalition offers a darker version of the same logic. President Recep Tayyip Erdogan's partners are simultaneously extending an olive branch to the imprisoned PKK leader Abdullah Ocalan — with the nationalist MHP's Devlet Bahceli calling for his "hope right" and the release of the jailed former HDP leader Selahattin Demirtas — while tightening the judicial noose around Istanbul's mayor, Ekrem Imamoglu, with espionage charges carrying up to 20 years. The Kurdish peace process and the opposition's legal persecution are not contradictions; they are two halves of a single transaction. Kurdish political cooperation is the price of constitutional amendments that could extend Mr Erdogan's rule beyond 2028, and the elimination of Mr Imamoglu clears the electoral field. That this bargain requires courting and crushing different democratic constituencies in the same week speaks to a regime whose transactionalism has become its organising principle. Central bank reserves hitting a record $218 billion merely confirm that economic orthodoxy, too, survives only at the president's pleasure.

The Gulf states carry transactional diplomacy to its most refined — and most exposed — extreme. Saudi Arabia's Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman privately urged American officials not to back away from striking Iran, while publicly rejecting the use of Saudi territory or airspace for any such attack: the dual message is less strategic ambiguity than strategic duplicity, sustainable only so long as neither Washington nor Tehran calls the bluff. The fracturing Saudi-UAE relationship, visible in the withdrawal of some 30 Emirati defence firms from Saudi Arabia's World Defence Show, shows what happens when two transactional powers run out of transactions that benefit both. The split, rooted in Yemen and sharpened by a Saudi strike on a UAE-linked weapons shipment, has migrated from diplomacy to commerce — a pattern last seen in the Qatar blockade. Vision 2030's quiet retrenchment, with NEOM's consultants cut by 90% and the Line shrunk from 170 kilometres to a 2.4-kilometre pilot, suggests the crown prince is discovering that even sovereign wealth funds worth $930 billion cannot finance every side of every bet.

Abu Dhabi, for its part, has wagered most explicitly on the Trump family itself. Sheikh Tahnoun bin Zayed's $500 million purchase of a 49% stake in World Liberty Financial, the cryptocurrency venture of the president's relatives, represents not hedging but direct financial entanglement with an American administration. Pair that with OpenAI's collaboration with the Abu Dhabi-backed G42 on a government-tailored ChatGPT — complete with content restrictions aligned with monarchical sensitivities — and the UAE's strategy comes into focus: buy access, localise the technology, and call it sovereignty. Yet President Mohammed bin Zayed's postponement of a Japan visit over US-Iran tensions, and his diplomat Anwar Gargash's plea that the region needs no more confrontation, betray the anxiety underneath. A country hosting Russia-Ukraine-America peace talks while holding a crypto stake in the first family is not so much balancing as juggling.

The pattern is now plain. From New Delhi to Ankara to Riyadh to Abu Dhabi, ambitious middle powers are leveraging a fragmenting global order to extract deals, arms, technology and diplomatic relevance at a pace that would have been unthinkable a decade ago. But each transaction creates a dependency, each hedge narrows future room for manoeuvre, and each contradiction quietly accumulates. The question is not whether this model works — this week proves it does — but whether it can survive the moment when the parties on the other side of these bargains begin comparing notes.

## the-americas

**The Subsidy State Turns Pragmatist**

North America's governments like to talk about markets, sovereignty and structural reform. This week's evidence from across the continent suggests something more precise: states that spent years building elaborate regulatory and fiscal architectures are now retrofitting them on the fly, driven less by ideology than by the dawning recognition that their previous bets are not paying off fast enough. The pattern is not retrenchment but improvisation — a scramble to swap one set of levers for another while insisting the destination has not changed.

Canada offers the clearest case. Mark Carney's decision to scrap the Electric Vehicle Availability Standard and replace it with $2.3 billion in consumer rebates is not a pivot to laissez-faire; it is an admission that mandates cannot survive a trade war with the country's largest customer. The new package aims to cut Canada's marginal effective tax rate on investment to 13%, four points below America's, a bid plainly designed to keep factories open when tariffs threaten to close them. Yet the same government is clawing back subsidies from General Motors for cutting jobs in Ontario — punishing a firm for responding to the very market signals Ottawa now claims to trust. Governor Tiff Macklem's blunt warning that the pain ahead is "structural, not temporary" frames the contradiction honestly: Canada is not choosing between regulation and markets but frantically layering one atop the other, hoping the combination holds.

Mexico's balancing act is older and better rehearsed, but no less improvised. Pemex's debt fell to $84.5 billion, its lowest in 11 years, after five consecutive years of reduction — a genuine achievement that sits oddly alongside a central bank forced to hold rates at 7% because inflation will not converge to target until 2027. President Claudia Sheinbaum's government cooperates with Washington on Rio Grande water management while dispatching naval vessels to Cuba laden with humanitarian aid, a choreography designed to yield on substance where it must and assert independence where it can. The arrest of four municipal presidents linked to the Jalisco New Generation Cartel shows operational capacity, yet the very need for such spectacles confirms that the security apparatus remains locked in a war of attrition rather than winning a decisive peace.

Brazil's Lula da Silva, declaring his 2026 re-election bid a "war" and shelving his conciliatory persona, is the most candid about the logic at work. His simultaneous moves — sending the Mercosur-EU trade deal to Congress, allocating 30 billion reais for nuclear-submarine programmes, shrugging off Nicolás Maduro's fate as "not a priority" — are not pieces of a coherent grand strategy but hedges against multiple risks. The trade pact reduces dependence on China; the defence spending buys autonomy from Washington; the Venezuela stance avoids a fight with both. Finance Minister Fernando Haddad's preference for managing the campaign over running for São Paulo governor reveals where the real energy is flowing: not into institution-building but into the electoral machine that will determine whether any of these bets survive 2027.

Chile's incoming president, José Antonio Kast, appears at first glance to be the exception — a leader with a clear ideological compass, courting Giorgia Meloni and Viktor Orbán, staffing his government with 67% independents over party loyalists. Yet Marco Rubio's attendance at his inauguration, the first high-level American presence in 12 years, suggests that Mr Kast's conservatism is as much a bid for external patronage as a domestic programme. His "emergency government" structure, built around technocrats rather than coalition arithmetic, is itself an improvisation forced by a congress he cannot control. Even the military-service overhaul — two recruitment cycles a year, 75% pay increases — reads less like strategic modernisation than an urgent fix for a force that could not attract enough volunteers at the old price.

The through-line is not ideological convergence but tactical pragmatism under pressure. From Ottawa to Santiago, governments are quietly abandoning frameworks that were meant to last a generation and replacing them with whatever seems most likely to work next quarter. Call it the age of the policy retrofit: leaders who once sold grand visions now sell improvisation, and voters, facing tariffs, inflation and cartel violence, appear willing to accept the bargain — for now.

## western-europe

**The Rearming of Europe's Conscience**

The week's most revealing pattern across western Europe is not that governments are arming and asserting themselves abroad — that has been underway since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine — but that this external muscularity is advancing faster than the domestic political foundations can support it. Governments are projecting strength outward while haemorrhaging authority at home, and the gap between the two is widening in ways that will eventually force a reckoning.

Germany's deployment of a permanent combat brigade to Lithuania, its first since 1945, is the starkest case. Chancellor Friedrich Merz has in a matter of months stationed heavy armour 30 kilometres from the Belarusian border, proposed giving the Federal Intelligence Service offensive cyber capabilities that critics call unconstitutional, and toured the Gulf to replace values-based diplomacy with interest-based deal-making. Yet the CDU/CSU has slipped to 25% in the polls, behind the AfD at 26%, and the German business federation has written to express "blank horror" at the government's economic stewardship. Mr Merz's personal approval has fallen five points. He is building an expeditionary state on a crumbling electoral base, and Iran's foreign minister — who called him a man of "disgusting character" — may be crude, but he is not wrong to detect nervousness.

Britain offers a mirror image of the same problem, compressed into a single week. NATO began planning an Arctic mission, the defence secretary negotiated a billion-pound helicopter deal, and the Bank of England held rates steady — all the machinery of a functioning state humming along. Meanwhile the prime minister's chief of staff resigned, his communications director followed, the Scottish Labour leader called for Mr Starmer's head, and the King was heckled in public over the Epstein affair. The Mandelson crisis has not merely embarrassed Downing Street; it has collapsed the prime minister's inner circle while the government's external commitments roll forward on institutional autopilot.

France shows how far a leader can push this divergence before it becomes structural. Mr Macron's government has placed record military orders — 38 billion euros in 2025, with 42 billion projected for next year — and French intelligence is actively countering Russian disinformation operations. But Prime Minister Lecornu has now resorted to Article 49.3 three times to pass a budget, confirming that basic governance depends on circumventing parliament rather than persuading it. Marine Le Pen's appeal trial may yet leave her free to run in 2027, and Jordan Bardella is already positioning himself as heir, meaning the Rassemblement National can threaten from two directions at once. The Interior Ministry's reclassification of France Unbowed as "extreme left" is a small administrative act that reveals a large anxiety: when the centre cannot win arguments, it redraws categories.

The pattern repeats in smaller variations elsewhere. Spain's Mr Sánchez faces crowds shouting "thief" and "fascist" in Cádiz even as his country's banks post record profits and his government picks fights with Elon Musk; Vox has doubled its seats in Aragón to become kingmaker. In Sweden, Mr Kristersson announces joint air-defence procurement with Denmark and warns of Russian interference while his deputy prime minister calls him "petty" on television and the Sweden Democrats' leader declares himself ready for the premiership. In Italy, Ms Meloni holds 45% approval but watches a former general splinter her coalition partner's party while leaked Epstein documents tie Mr Salvini to Steve Bannon. Norway's prime minister publicly rebukes his own crown princess, a former premier faces corruption charges, and an ambassador resigns — all over the same transatlantic scandal — even as intelligence services declare the gravest security environment since the second world war.

What connects these cases is not simply that Europe is rearming, or that populism is rising, or that old scandals are resurfacing. It is that the political classes capable of sustaining long-term military and diplomatic commitments are losing the domestic legitimacy those commitments require. Armies can be deployed by executive decision; keeping them deployed demands durable coalitions, parliamentary majorities, and public trust. Across western Europe this week, all three are in shorter supply than the ammunition orders suggest. The continent is building a harder shell around a softer core, and that is a structure which does not hold.
