# A frontline state between integration and fragility

**Latvia stands as one of NATO's most exposed and internally complex frontline states** — a nation of 1.86 million people spending nearly 5% of GDP on defense while hemorrhaging population, dismantling its Soviet-era economic infrastructure, and managing a deeply bifurcated ethnic society. Since Russia's 2022 full-scale invasion of Ukraine, Latvia has undergone the most rapid security reorientation of any European state, simultaneously disconnecting from Russian energy grids, demolishing Soviet monuments, transitioning Russian-language schools to Latvian instruction, and scaling NATO presence from a battalion to a combat-capable brigade. Yet the country faces a demographic death spiral — having lost 30% of its population since 1990 — that threatens to undermine every element of its national strategy. Latvia's trajectory illuminates the acute dilemmas confronting small frontline democracies navigating US retrenchment and Russian aggression: how to deter a nuclear-armed neighbor while depopulating, how to integrate a large ethnic minority descended from imperial settlement, and how to sustain political cohesion when founding narratives remain bitterly contested.

## I. Political architecture: a fractured coalition on a war footing

Latvia is a parliamentary republic governed under the **Satversme (Constitution) of 1922** — the oldest functioning constitution in Central and Eastern Europe, suspended during Soviet occupation and restored in 1990–1993. The 100-seat **Saeima** (unicameral parliament) is elected by proportional representation across five constituencies with a 5% threshold.

**President Edgars Rinkēvičs**, inaugurated July 8, 2023, serves as head of state with semi-executive powers: supreme commander of the armed forces, chair of the National Security Council, and power to nominate the prime minister and return legislation to the Saeima. Mr. Rinkēvičs, born 1973, was Latvia's longest-serving Foreign Minister (2011–2023, spanning four cabinets) and became the **first openly gay head of state in EU history** after coming out in 2014. Elected by the Saeima with 52 of 100 votes after three rounds, he left New Unity upon assuming office.

**Prime Minister Evika Siliņa** (New Unity), in office since September 15, 2023, leads a three-party coalition holding a slim 53-seat majority. The coalition comprises **New Unity (JV, 26 seats)**, the centre-right EPP-affiliated party that tripled its representation in the October 2022 elections on the strength of Latvia's Ukraine response; the **Union of Greens and Farmers (ZZS, 16 seats)**, an agrarian-centrist party with deep ties to convicted oligarch Aivars Lembergs; and **the Progressives (10 seats)**, a left-liberal Greens/EFA party that, despite being the smallest coalition partner, holds the strategically critical Defence Ministry under Minister Andris Sprūds. The coalition formed after PM Krišjānis Kariņš resigned in August 2023 after a dispute over Rinkēvičs's presidential candidacy.

The opposition comprises four parties. **The National Alliance (NA, 13 seats)** is the ethno-nationalist right, affiliated with ECR, whose MEP Roberts Zīle serves as European Parliament Vice-President. The **United List (AS, 15 seats)** is a centrist-regionalist alliance of the Latvian Green Party and Latvian Regional Alliance, formed from the non-Lembergs wing of the former ZZS. **For Stability! (11 seats)**, a populist party founded by expelled Harmony member Aleksejs Rosļikovs, has replaced Harmony as the primary voice of Russian-speaking voters. **Latvia First (LPV, 9 seats)**, led by oligarch Ainārs Šlesers, is a right-wing populist, Eurosceptic formation affiliated with Patriots for Europe in the European Parliament.

The most consequential political development is **Harmony's collapse**. The party that won the largest share in three consecutive elections (2011, 2014, 2018) as the representative of Latvia's Russian-speaking constituency fell below the 5% threshold in 2022 with just 4.9%, losing all parliamentary seats. The party never entered a governing coalition despite repeated pluralities — excluded by an informal cordon sanitaire rooted in Harmony's 2009 cooperation agreement with Putin's United Russia, its refusal to characterize the Soviet period as "occupation," and ambiguous positions on Russia-Ukraine relations. Leader Nils Ušakovs, the ethnic Russian former mayor of Riga (2009–2019, dismissed for financial mismanagement), now serves as an MEP.

Coalition stability is tenuous. In October 2025, ZZS broke with its coalition partners by voting to withdraw from the Istanbul Convention (56–32), a bill introduced by opposition party Latvia First. President Rinkēvičs returned the legislation, and the Saeima postponed the issue until after the October 2026 elections. The coalition remains technically intact but functionally fractured.

<strong>Key state institutions</strong>

The **Constitution Protection Bureau (SAB)** is Latvia's domestic security and counterintelligence service, focused primarily on Russian intelligence threats and hybrid warfare. Its annual reports extensively document Russian espionage operations and information campaigns targeting Latvia's Russian-speaking population.

The **KNAB (Corruption Prevention Bureau)**, set up in 2002 and headed by Jēkabs Straume since 2017, is the primary anti-corruption authority. KNAB investigations drove the prosecution of oligarch **Aivars Lembergs** — former Ventspils mayor (1988–2021), convicted February 2021 of bribery and money laundering (€14 million+), US-sanctioned under the Global Magnitsky Act (December 2019), and still ZZS's behind-the-scenes power. Two other oligarchic figures shaped Latvia's post-independence political economy: Mr. Šlesers (now leading Latvia First from the Saeima) and former PM **Andris Šķēle** (largely retired from active politics).

The **National Armed Forces (NBS)** have undergone radical expansion. Peacetime strength stands at about **7,870 professional soldiers** and **10,000 National Guard members**, with a target of 31,000 by 2030. Latvia **reinstated conscription on January 1, 2024** — 11-month service for males born after January 1, 2004, with 300 conscripts in the first year, scaling to 4,000 annually by 2028. The **defense budget has surged from 0.9% of GDP in 2014 to 4.91% of GDP (€2.16 billion) in 2026** — among the highest in NATO — with plans to reach 5% by 2027.

The **Bank of Latvia**, governed by Mārtiņš Kazāks (reappointed February 2025 for a second term), absorbed the Financial and Capital Market Commission (FCMC) on January 1, 2023, consolidating monetary policy and financial supervision. Mr. Kazāks's predecessor, Ilmārs Rimšēvičs, was convicted of bribery — part of the money laundering scandals that engulfed Latvia's banking sector.

<strong>Media and external influence</strong>

Latvia's public broadcaster, **Latvijas Sabiedriskie mediji (LSM)**, formed January 1, 2025 from the merger of Latvian Radio and Latvian Television, operates LTV1 (12.3% viewership share), LTV7, six radio channels, and LSM.lv. Russian-language public media is being phased out: LTV7 went Latvian-only from January 2025; **Latvian Radio 4 (the Russian-language channel) ceased broadcasting January 1, 2026**. The National Security Concept (September 2023) mandates all public media content in "Latvian and languages belonging to the European cultural space" from 2026. Major private outlets include **Delfi.lv** (owned by Estonian Ekspress Grupp, used by \~64% of population), **TV3** (All Media Latvia / Providence Equity Partners), and Tvnet.lv (Estonian Postimees Grupp). Post-2022, Latvia banned **41+ Russian TV channels** and restricted 71 websites. The ban on independent Russian channel **TV Rain (Dozhd)**, which had relocated to Riga, proved particularly controversial.

**NATO's presence** transformed from a battalion-sized Enhanced Forward Presence to a **combat-capable Multinational Brigade Latvia (MNB-LVA)** on July 3, 2024 — 3,500+ troops from 14 nations led by Canada as framework nation. Canada has committed **\$230 million CAD** in infrastructure investment and plans to increase its contingent to 2,600 troops by 2026. Sweden deployed its first major NATO battalion in January 2025.

## II. Founding mythology: occupation, resistance, and unresolved pasts

Latvia's national identity rests on a foundational narrative of interrupted statehood — the "occupation thesis" that treats 1940–1991 not as incorporation into the USSR but as **illegal military occupation** under which the Republic of Latvia continued to exist de jure. This doctrine shapes everything from citizenship law to education policy to diplomatic posture.

The Republic was proclaimed **November 18, 1918** at the Latvian National Theatre in Riga. The ensuing **War of Independence (1918–1920)** — 628 days of fighting against Bolsheviks, Baltic Germans, and White Russian forces — cost about 3,000 killed and displaced 800,000 people. The **Treaty of Riga (August 11, 1920)** with Soviet Russia recognized Latvia's independence "for all future time." Latvia joined the League of Nations in 1921 and adopted its constitution on February 15, 1922.

Democratic governance lasted only sixteen years. On the night of **May 15–16, 1934**, Prime Minister **Kārlis Ulmanis**, with the support of War Minister Jānis Balodis and the Aizsargi paramilitary, executed a **bloodless coup**, suspending the Satversme, dissolving the Saeima and all political parties, and arresting about 1,080 opponents. By April 1936, Ulmanis had merged the offices of president and prime minister, adopting the title **Vadonis** ("Leader"). His authoritarian period (1934–1940) remains ambiguously commemorated — associated with economic modernization and agricultural prosperity, yet representing the first rupture of democratic governance.

The **Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact (August 23, 1939)** and its secret protocols assigned Latvia to the Soviet sphere. On June 16, 1940, Moscow delivered an ultimatum; the next day, about 100,000 Soviet troops entered Latvia. Ulmanis ordered no resistance and was deported to the USSR, dying in captivity on **September 20, 1942**. A puppet government under Augusts Kirhenšteins staged rigged elections, and on August 5, 1940, Latvia was formally annexed as the Latvian SSR. The United States **never recognized this annexation** — the Welles Declaration of July 23, 1940 maintained recognition of Latvia's pre-war statehood throughout the entire 51-year occupation.

Two waves of mass deportation form the deepest trauma in Latvian collective memory. On **June 13–14, 1941**, the NKVD deported about **15,000–15,600 people**, including 2,400 children under 10 and 20% of Latvia's last legal government; 34–40% of deportees perished. On **March 25–28, 1949**, Operation Priboi deported **42,125–43,000 people** (2.2% of the population) — 94.5% ethnic Latvians, 67.7% categorized as "kulaks" — using 31 freight trains under the guard of 3,300 MGB operatives and 18,000 military personnel. The deportation's immediate purpose was achieved: by year's end, **93% of Latvian farms were collectivized**. Both dates — June 14 and March 25 — are now official commemoration days.

<strong>The Holocaust and collaboration</strong>

The Nazi occupation (1941–1944/45) brought systematic genocide. Latvia's pre-war Jewish population numbered about **93,000–94,000**. By war's end, roughly **70,000 had been murdered** — a destruction rate exceeding 90% of those who did not flee to the USSR interior. Only an estimated 200 Jews survived on Latvian soil. The **Rumbula massacre (November 30 and December 8, 1941)** — about 25,000 Jews shot in Rumbula forest — was the second-largest two-day Holocaust massacre after Babi Yar, directed by SS-Gruppenführer Friedrich Jeckeln with significant participation from the **Arajs Kommando**, an all-volunteer ethnic Latvian auxiliary of 1,000–1,500 men founded by Viktors Arājs. The Arajs Kommando burned Riga's synagogues on July 4, 1941 (now Latvia's Holocaust Memorial Day) and participated in mass shootings across the countryside. Some 150 Holocaust death sites are documented across Latvia; **46,500 Jews were murdered** in Biķernieki Forest alone.

The **Latvian Legion** — two Waffen-SS divisions (15th and 19th) totaling nearly **150,000 men, of whom 80–85% were conscripts** — remains the most contested element of Latvia's wartime history. The Nuremberg Tribunal declared the SS a criminal organization but exempted those forcibly conscripted who committed no crimes. The US Displaced Persons Commission ruled in September 1950 that Baltic Waffen-SS units were "separate" from the German SS. **March 16** — the date in 1944 when both Latvian divisions first fought together — was briefly an official remembrance day (designated 1998, rescinded 2000) and continues as an unofficial annual march to Riga's Freedom Monument, typically drawing 1,000–2,500 participants. The government's position holds that the Legion "was not involved in Holocaust crimes," while acknowledging that some legionnaires previously served in auxiliary police units that were. Simon Wiesenthal Center director Efraim Zuroff has noted this crucial distinction and overlap.

Latvia has recognized its Holocaust history through institutional means — a presidential apology during a visit to Israel (President Guntis Ulmanis, 1998), a Commission of Historians set up in November 1998, and **826 Latvians recognized as Righteous Among the Nations** by Yad Vashem. The Žanis Lipke Memorial in Riga honors a Latvian who rescued Jews. Critics note that no Latvian Holocaust collaborator has been prosecuted since independence.

<strong>Independence restoration</strong>

The **Singing Revolution** crystallized with the founding of the **Latvian Popular Front (Tautas fronte)** on October 9, 1988, which grew to 250,000 members and reached across ethnic lines (48% of Latvia's population was non-Latvian). The **Baltic Way of August 23, 1989** — about **2 million people** forming a 675-kilometer human chain from Tallinn through Riga to Vilnius — remains the defining image of Baltic independence movements. On May 4, 1990, the Supreme Council adopted the **Declaration on the Restoration of Independence** with 138 votes in favor (134 required). A referendum on March 3, 1991 delivered **73.68% support** for independence. During the **January 1991 barricades**, some 32,000 defenders erected barriers around Riga's parliament, TV tower, and strategic buildings after Soviet OMON forces attacked; **5–7 people were killed** on January 20, including filmmaker Andris Slapiņš. Full independence came on **August 21, 1991**, during the Moscow coup attempt. Iceland recognized Latvia first (August 22); the Soviet Union followed on September 6; UN admission came September 17, 1991.

The **Museum of the Occupation of Latvia**, founded 1993 in the former Red Latvian Riflemen Museum building, holds over 70,000 historical items and operates the Corner House (former KGB headquarters) exhibition. Alongside the naturalization exam — which requires acknowledging that Latvia was occupied by the Soviet Union — and the Satversme's 2014 preamble explicitly referencing state continuity, these institutions maintain the occupation thesis as lived civic doctrine rather than abstract legal theory.

## III. Geography that dictates strategy

Latvia occupies **64,573 km²** of the East European Plain between the Baltic Sea and Russia, with 1,866 km of total boundary. The country borders Estonia (339 km north), Lithuania (588 km south), Russia's Pskov Oblast (**283.6 km east**), and Belarus (**172.9 km southeast**). The terrain is overwhelmingly flat — highest point Gaiziņkalns at just 311.6 meters — with significant marshland, peatbog (about 10% of territory), and heavy forest cover (**52–54% of territory**, nearly doubling from 24.7% in 1923). Agricultural land covers roughly 29–31% of territory, producing wheat, rapeseed, barley, potatoes, and dairy.

The **eastern border with Russia** is Latvia's most critical security concern. The terrain — rivers, swamps, and forests — channels military movement to road corridors, providing some natural defensive advantage. Since 2015, Latvia has been building a **280-km border fence**, reportedly completed by end of 2025, supplemented by anti-tank ditches, "dragon's teeth" obstacles, and a planned **Baltic Defence Line** of strongholds about 1 km from the Russian border, budgeted at **\$326 million over five years**. The **Belarus border** gained new significance after the disputed 2020 Belarusian elections and Minsk's weaponization of migration; Latvia completed a \~145-km fence along this border by July 2024 and has closed border crossings.

The **Suwalki Gap** — a 65-km stretch of Polish-Lithuanian border sandwiched between Russia's Kaliningrad exclave and Belarus — is NATO's most discussed vulnerability. In a conflict scenario, a two-pronged Russian operation from Kaliningrad and Belarus could sever the only land corridor connecting all three Baltic states to the rest of NATO, forcing reliance on sea and air resupply threatened by Russia's Kaliningrad-based A2/AD capabilities (S-400, Iskander, anti-ship missiles). A 2016 RAND study concluded Russian forces could reach Baltic capitals within 60 hours without prior reinforcement. NATO's response includes the US-led battlegroup at Orzysz, Poland, regional defense plans approved at the 2023 Vilnius Summit, and Military Mobility initiatives upgrading infrastructure for rapid reinforcement.

Latvia's **three major ports** — Riga (18.1 million tonnes in 2024), Ventspils (8.2 million), and Liepāja (7 million) — historically served as Russia-Europe transit points handling over 60 million tonnes annually at peak. That role has collapsed: **total 2024 port cargo was 36 million tonnes, down 6.8%**, with rail freight plunging 35.2% year-on-year to just 6.9 million tonnes as Russian transit disappeared. The government's 2025–2027 roadmap targets recovery to 47 million tonnes through reorientation toward manufacturing, offshore wind servicing, and north-south logistics. **Liepāja** has acquired particular strategic importance as a NATO naval asset, with **€160 million** allocated for military port development beginning in 2028.

The **Gulf of Riga**, partially enclosed by Estonia's Saaremaa island, houses Latvia's capital and primary port. Kaliningrad lies about 250–300 km from Latvia's southwestern coast, placing all three Baltic states within range of Kaliningrad-based missiles. Latvia's **Baltic Sea EEZ** of about 28,000 km² holds significance for fisheries, offshore wind development, and submarine cable security — an acute concern after the Estlink 2 sabotage (December 25, 2024) and Balticconnector damage (October 2023).

## IV. Layers of empire, layers of institutional inheritance

Latvia's institutional DNA carries sediment from every imperial power that has ruled its territory. The medieval **Livonian Confederation (1228–1561)** established Riga as a Hanseatic League port (admitted 1282) and introduced German-speaking governance structures that persisted for seven centuries. The remarkable **Duchy of Courland and Semigallia (1561–1795)** — a Polish-Lithuanian vassal — built one of Europe's largest merchant fleets under Duke Jakob Kettler and set up colonies in **Tobago (1654)** and **Gambia (1651)** before decline in the Northern Wars.

**Swedish rule over Vidzeme** (17th century, confirmed by the Truce of Altmark in 1629) is remembered as the "good Swedish times" for reforms limiting noble power and expanding education. The **Great Northern War (1700–1721)** devastated Latvia — up to 40% of the population died from famine and plague — and transferred Vidzeme to Russia via the **Treaty of Nystad (1721)**. Russia absorbed Latgale in the First Partition of Poland (1772) and Courland in the Third Partition (1795).

The **Baltic German Ritterschaften** (noble corporations) dominated economic and political life under Russian rule, controlling vast estates while making up only about 7% of the population. Russification under Alexander III and Nicholas II (from 1881) imposed Russian-language education and judicial systems, inadvertently catalyzing Latvian national consciousness. The **Young Latvians (Jaunlatvieši)** movement of the 1850s–1860s, led by Krišjānis Valdemārs and Krišjānis Barons, produced the **First Latvian Song Festival in 1873** — a tradition now recognized by UNESCO. The **1905 Revolution was particularly intense in Latvia**: \~300 manor houses were burned by Latvian peasants, \~1,000 armed clashes occurred, and punitive expeditions executed over **2,500 Latvians** without trial.

The **Soviet institutional legacy** remains structurally consequential. Soviet authorities built Latvia's industrial base — the VEF electronics factory, RAF minibus factory (producing \~300,000 vehicles over 36 years), Rīgas Vagonbūves railway works — while deliberately importing a Russian-speaking workforce that reduced ethnic Latvians from **75% of the population in 1935 to 52% by 1989**. The KGB operated extensive informant networks; Latvian authorities seized about 4,500 agent registration cards after independence, used by the Center for Documentation of Totalitarianism Consequences (CDCT) for lustration vetting of officials and electoral candidates.

Post-1991 institutional transformation proceeded through three channels: restoration of pre-Soviet institutions (the 1922 Satversme, 1937 Civil Code), adoption of the EU acquis communautaire upon accession (May 1, 2004), and NATO integration (March 29, 2004). Latvia's lustration regime — barring former KGB agents from elections, senior civil service, and security positions — took a middle path between Estonian mildness and Lithuanian stringency, largely upheld by the European Court of Human Rights.

## V. The ethnic fracture line runs deep

Latvia's most consequential internal cleavage is ethnic and linguistic, and it is **strongly reinforcing across geography, politics, media, religion, and historical memory**. Ethnic Latvians constitute **63.7%** of the population (beginning of 2025, CSB data), ethnic Russians **\~23%**, with Belarusians (\~3%), Ukrainians (\~3%, including recent refugees), Poles (\~2%), and Lithuanians (\~1.2%) comprising the remainder. The Russian-speaking population — broader than ethnic Russians alone — accounts for **37.2% of those speaking Russian at home** (2011 census).

The geographic distribution is stark. Russians concentrate in **Riga (\~42% of city population)**, **Daugavpils (majority Russian, Latvia's second city)**, and the **Latgale region** (Russians \~39%, native Russian speakers at 55.5%). Rural western and central Latvia (Kurzeme, Vidzeme) remains overwhelmingly Latvian. This ethnic geography overlaps with economic geography: Latgale is Latvia's poorest region, with the highest elderly share (24.4%), highest unemployment, and smallest child population.

The **non-citizen (nepilsoņi) system** is the institutional expression of the occupation thesis applied to demographics. In October 1991, citizenship was restored exclusively to pre-1940 citizens and their descendants, immediately excluding about **700,000–800,000 Soviet-era settlers**. Non-citizens hold a unique legal status — not citizens, not aliens, not stateless under Latvian law — with permanent residency, social benefits, and a distinctive "alien's passport" but **no voting rights in any elections**, no eligibility for public office, and restrictions on certain professions. As of January 2024, **180,455 non-citizens** remained in Latvia (8.9% of population), down from \~730,000 (29%) in 1995. The decline reflects naturalization (over 150,000 persons since 1995), emigration, aging mortality, and since January 2020, automatic citizenship for children born in Latvia to non-citizen parents. International organizations have consistently criticized the system: the OSCE noted "a continuing democratic deficit," the Council of Europe urged local voting rights for non-citizens (Resolution, November 2006), and Amnesty International has described non-citizens as stateless — a characterization Latvia rejects.

The **2012 language referendum** — an attempt to make Russian a second official language — crystallized ethnic polarization. Promoted by the "Native Language" movement linked to Vladimir Linderman, it was rejected by **75.04% of voters** on February 18, 2012, with a record 71.12% turnout. The sole exception was **Latgale, where 55.8% voted in favor**. Non-citizens could not vote, a fact Russia cited in criticism. The referendum deepened rather than bridged the ethnic divide: Latvians mobilized massively in opposition while Harmony leader Ušakovs's support for the initiative permanently damaged his cross-ethnic appeal.

**Russian-language education reform** has accelerated dramatically since 2022. The original 2004 reform required 60% of secondary instruction in Latvian. In September 2022, the Saeima mandated **full transition to Latvian-only instruction across all public and private education** (preschool through grade 9), with phased implementation completing by **September 2025**. The ECHR upheld the reforms, ruling that protecting Latvian after 50+ years of Soviet Russification constituted a legitimate objective.

Post-2022 dynamics have reshaped the ethnic landscape. The **Riga Victory Monument** — a 79-meter Soviet obelisk erected in 1985, focal point of Russian-speaking May 9 celebrations — was **demolished August 22–25, 2022** after unauthorized pro-Russian gatherings after the Ukraine invasion. The Saeima adopted legislation requiring removal of 69 Soviet/Nazi glorifying objects by November 2022. Russian state media was banned (41+ channels, 71 websites). Most important, **permanent residence permits for Russian and Belarusian citizens were voided** as of September 1, 2023 unless holders passed a Latvian language test at A2 level — affecting about 25,000–30,000 people. By 2025, the majority had completed requirements, with only \~500 permits expired and just 10 individuals found residing illegally.

The cleavage is mitigated by two factors: **91% of minorities speak at least basic Latvian** (76% fluently), with near-universal competence among younger generations; and everyday integration occurs naturally in mixed workplaces and neighborhoods. But political, media, and memory divides remain profound — parallel media ecosystems, near-total ethnic voting patterns, and fundamentally opposed interpretations of the 20th century.

## VI. A country losing its people

Latvia's demographic trajectory is an existential strategic constraint. The population has fallen from a peak of about **2.66 million in 1990 to 1.857 million at the beginning of 2025** — a **30% decline** representing one of the steepest depopulation rates in human history for a country not experiencing famine or war. In the first half of 2025 alone, the population dropped by a further 26,500.

The decline has three drivers. **Emigration** surged after EU accession in 2004, peaked during the devastating 2008–2009 financial crisis (net migration reached about -35,000/year in 2009–2010), and stabilized only around 2018. The diaspora now numbers an estimated **420,000 Latvians abroad** — equivalent to 23% of the domestic population — concentrated in the UK (52%), Ireland (15%), and Germany (12%). Brain drain is severe: 45% of emigrants held tertiary education by 2014, and over one-third of highly educated Latvians aged 25–34 emigrated between 2000 and 2014. **Natural decline** has been negative since 1992, with 2023 recording only **14,000 births — the lowest in 100 years** — against about 27,000–28,000 deaths annually. The total fertility rate stands at about **1.36 children per woman**, far below the 2.1 replacement level. The anomalous positive net migration of 2022 (driven by \~38,000 Ukrainian refugees) was temporary; mid-2025 data shows renewed population decline as Ukrainian visa/permit expirations drive departures.

The aging structure is alarming. **Median age has reached 43.5–44.3 years**; the elderly population (65+) is 21.9% and rising, while children (0–14) have fallen to 15.1%. The **old-age dependency ratio** — 36% — is projected to reach **61% by 2070**, the largest increase among EU states. Latvia has the **highest 65+ at-risk-of-poverty rate in the EU**. The pension system's replacement rate is projected to **collapse from 56% to 24% between 2022 and 2050** — the steepest decline in any EU country. Male life expectancy is about 70.5 years, among the EU's lowest, with a \~10-year gender gap.

Riga dominates the urban structure, with about 590,000 residents (declining) in the city and roughly one-third of the national population in the metropolitan area. All other cities are shrinking: Daugavpils (\~80,000), Liepāja (\~66,000), Jelgava (\~55,000), Ventspils (\~32,000, with the highest elderly share at 24.9%). Only Riga suburban municipalities — Mārupe, Ādaži, Ropaži — show population growth.

**Eurostat projections (EUROPOP2023)** forecast Latvia's population falling to about **1.5 million by 2045**, below Estonia's by 2060, and to just **1.16 million by 2100** — a 37.8% decline from 2022. This demographic clock creates cascading pressures: shrinking military-age cohorts make it difficult to fill 2,000 annual recruits (prompting consideration of diaspora conscription); the labor market faces a structural deficit as 85,000 people aged 20–24 enter against 135,000 aged 60–64 exiting; and regional depopulation threatens to hollow out Latgale — the very region most critical for border defense.

## VII. An economy reorienting under strain

Latvia's economic transformation mirrors its geopolitical reorientation — a wrenching pivot away from its historical role as Russia's transit corridor toward integration with Western European supply chains, achieved through successive crises.

The macroeconomic picture shows a **\$43.5 billion nominal GDP** (2024) with GDP per capita of about **\$23,400 nominal / \$46,600 PPP**. But the economy **contracted 0.4% in 2024**, with recovery forecast at 1.0–2.6% for 2025. Latvia joined the **Eurozone on January 1, 2014**, and public debt remains manageable at **47.7% of GDP**, though it is rising toward 50–55% by 2027 as defense spending generates deficits of 3.1–4.3% of GDP. Inequality is pronounced: the **Gini coefficient of 34.2** is the EU's third-highest, and the at-risk-of-poverty rate stands at 22.5%.

Latvia endured one of the world's most severe financial crises in 2008–2009, when **GDP fell about 18%** after the collapse of Parex Bank (then the second-largest) and the bursting of an unsustainable credit/real-estate bubble. A €7.5 billion IMF/EU bailout was conditioned on maintaining the currency peg to the euro and implementing "internal devaluation" — severe wage and spending cuts rather than currency devaluation. GDP did not return to pre-crisis levels until 2017–2018, but the austerity was vindicated by rapid subsequent growth (5.5% in 2011, 5.6% in 2012 — the EU's fastest).

The transit economy's collapse is the defining structural shift. Latvia's ports and railways were designed to handle **80+ million tonnes annually** of Russian goods — oil, coal, fertilizers, potash. Russia's development of its own ports (Ust-Luga, Primorsk) began diverting cargo before 2014; the Crimea annexation accelerated the decline; and post-2022 sanctions effectively ended the business. **Railway cargo fell from 50 million tonnes (2015) to 6.9 million tonnes (2024)** — an 86% collapse. Total port cargo declined from historical peaks above 60 million to **36 million tonnes in 2024**. Ventspils, historically the premier Russian oil transit port, saw volumes drop 32.2% in early 2024 alone, its oil terminal largely idled. Russian imports to Latvia fell from **\$2.1 billion (2021) to \$414 million (2024)**.

The **banking sector cleanup** was equally dramatic. Latvia's banking system served as a "main laundering platform for stolen Russian money" through the mid-2010s, with non-resident deposits — primarily from Russia/CIS via shell companies — reaching about **50% of total banking deposits**. The crisis culminated in February 2018 when the US Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) designated **ABLV Bank** as an institution of "primary money laundering concern," noting that its annual flows nearly equaled Latvia's GDP (\~€22–23 billion). ABLV self-liquidated; Central Bank Governor Rimšēvičs was arrested for bribery the same month. Trasta Komercbank (2016), Norvik Bank (2019), and Baltic International Bank followed into closure. Non-resident deposits were reduced to minimal levels. The current banking landscape is dominated by Nordic capital: **Swedbank** (€9.3 billion assets), **SEB** (€5.6 billion), and **Citadele** (€4.9 billion, domestically owned after being carved from the collapsed Parex Bank).

Key sectors in the reoriented economy include **services (61–64% of GDP)**, with a growing IT sector (6,900+ companies, \~6% of GDP) bolstered by a Microsoft AI hub memorandum signed December 2024. **Wood and timber processing** is the largest manufacturing subsector, leveraging Latvia's 52–54% forest coverage; wood products are the top merchandise export. Manufacturing overall contributes about 11–12% of GDP, including food processing, pharmaceuticals (Grindeks), and machinery. **Agriculture accounts for 3.6–5% of GDP**, with a 2024 grain harvest of 3.2 million tonnes. Trade has reoriented overwhelmingly toward the EU: top import partners are Lithuania (20.6%), Germany (11.6%), and Poland (11.2%), with \~85% of FDI from EU member states (Sweden alone accounts for 31.1% of FDI stock). Latvia's minimum wage has risen from €500 to **€740/month** as of January 2025, with plans for €860 by 2028, though the average net wage of **€1,127** remains less than half the EU average.

## VIII. Infrastructure pivots from east to west

Latvia's physical infrastructure was designed by the Russian Empire and Soviet Union to channel goods and energy eastward. The national rail network uses **1,520mm Russian broad gauge**, incompatible with Europe's 1,435mm standard. Natural gas pipelines (1,190 km of transmission network operated by Conexus Baltic Grid) historically fed Russian gas; the **Ventspils-Polotsk oil pipeline** (6 million tonnes/year capacity) carried Russian crude to the Baltic. Most critically, the electricity grid was synchronized with the Russian/Belarusian IPS/UPS system through the **BRELL agreement**, with frequency regulation controlled from Moscow.

The single most consequential infrastructure milestone of recent years was the **electricity grid desynchronization from Russia on February 8, 2025** — ahead of the original end-2025 deadline. At 09:09 on February 8, the three Baltic states permanently disconnected from the Russian-controlled IPS/UPS system; by 14:05 on February 9, they had successfully synchronized with the Continental European Synchronous Area. This was achieved through EU-funded interconnectors totaling over **€1.2 billion**: the **LitPol Link** (500 MW, Lithuania-Poland), **NordBalt cable** (Sweden-Lithuania), **Estlink 1 and 2** (Estonia-Finland), and internal reinforcements including Latvia's Kurzeme Ring (330 kV, \~330 km). Post-disconnection, Kaliningrad became an electricity island.

**Rail Baltica** — the 870-km European standard gauge rail line from Tallinn through Riga to Warsaw, with a maximum speed of 249 km/h and full electrification — is the Baltic states' most strategically important infrastructure project and their most troubled. RB Rail AS, the joint venture managing the project, has entered full construction across all three countries: 43% of the mainline was construction-ready by end of 2025. Latvia's 265-km section includes the Riga Central Hub (reconstruction underway), Riga Airport terminal, and mainline construction near Iecava. A **€1.77 billion electrification contract** was signed with the COBELEC consortium in August 2025. But the project is well behind original timelines; the current Phase I target is **single-track operations by 2030**, at a total cost of **€15.3 billion** (with \~€5.6 billion in EU Connecting Europe Facility funding). Phase II — full double track with direct Riga and Vilnius connections — depends on additional funding. Rail Baltica is explicitly designated critical for NATO military mobility.

Energy security has been substantially achieved. Latvia **banned Russian natural gas imports from January 1, 2023** (having previously imported 100%). Current supply comes through the **Klaipėda LNG terminal** (Lithuania, operational since 2014, capacity 10.25 mcm/day, primarily US LNG), the **GIPL pipeline** (Gas Interconnection Poland-Lithuania, operational May 2022, 508 km, 2.4 bcm/year), and the **enhanced Latvia-Lithuania interconnection (ELLI project)**, which increased gas transport capacity to Latvia by 30% to 90 GWh/day. The **Inčukalns underground gas storage facility** — the only functional gas storage in the Baltic states, with **maximum capacity of 4.47 bcm** (active gas \~2.32 bcm) — serves as the region's strategic buffer, its five-year bundled capacity fully booked through 2031. Latvia's proposed Skulte LNG terminal was shelved in October 2023 when existing regional infrastructure was deemed enough.

Latvia's renewable energy mix is among Europe's greenest, with **\~45% of total energy consumption from renewables**. The **Daugava River cascade** — Pļaviņas HPP (908 MW, largest in the Baltics), Riga HPP (402 MW), and Ķegums HPP (248 MW) — produced 3.7 TWh in 2023, representing 59.3% of electricity generation. Wind capacity stands at about 136 MW across 19 operational farms, with the planned **ELWIND offshore wind farm** targeting **1 GW capacity** (auction planned for 2028). Biomass contributes over 10% of electricity capacity, leveraging the country's forest resources.

Digital infrastructure is a bright spot. Latvia ranks among Europe's **top seven countries for fiber penetration** — 91% FTTP coverage versus the EU average of 56%, with 76% of fixed broadband subscriptions over fiber. **5G coverage reached about 72% of the population** by 2023. The **Ādaži military base** hosts a dedicated 5G military test site affiliated with NATO's DIANA innovation program. E-governance services score 87/100 for citizens (above EU average), though Latvia lags Estonia's more comprehensive digital government ecosystem. **Cybersecurity** is a critical and growing concern: since Russia's 2022 invasion, cyber incidents have increased **sixfold**. CERT.LV, operating under the Ministry of Defence, leads what Latvia calls EU-leading threat hunting operations, having analyzed over 100,000 devices across 25 organizations by end of 2023, confirming advanced persistent threat (state-backed attacker) presence in one-third.

## IX. The timeline pressures converge

Latvia's strategic position is defined by the convergence of several temporal pressures. The demographic clock is the most unforgiving: every year of population decline shrinks the tax base funding defense expansion, reduces the pool of military-age recruits needed to fill a growing force structure, and deepens the pension crisis that threatens social stability. The infrastructure reorientation — substantially achieved for energy (BRELL disconnection, Russian gas ban) but incomplete for transport (Rail Baltica's 2030 target) — must be completed before any potential conflict. The ethnic integration timeline has accelerated under post-2022 pressure, with Russian-language education completing its transition to Latvian-only by September 2025, but the generation of elderly non-citizens excluded from political participation will take decades to age out. And the defense buildup — from 0.9% of GDP in 2014 to a planned 5% by 2027, with NATO's brigade now in place at Ādaži — races against the uncertain timeline of Russian capabilities reconstitution after Ukraine losses.

Latvia's experience suggests that for small frontline democracies, the window for institutional transformation opened by a galvanizing external threat (Russia's 2022 invasion) is narrow. The political will to spend 5% of GDP on defense, demolish Soviet monuments, shut down Russian-language broadcasting, and void Russian citizens' residence permits exists now — sustained by coalition consensus and public fear. Whether it persists through the October 2026 elections, against a backdrop of coalition fracture, demographic exhaustion, and potential Western fatigue, is the central question for Latvia's next chapter as a frontline state navigating great-power retrenchment.

## X. A flat, forested, flood-prone landscape that shapes strategic calculus

<strong>Climate and terrain define both agricultural limits and defense planning</strong>

Latvia's geography is dominated by two facts: it is extraordinarily flat and extraordinarily forested. Classified predominantly as **Dfb humid continental** (83.7% of territory) with a temperate oceanic strip along the western coast, the country experiences average temperatures of 7.6°C in Riga, January lows reaching -29.5°C (Daugavpils, January 2024 — coldest in 76 years), and about 675–780 mm of annual precipitation. The growing season runs roughly May through September, supporting grain production (3.2 million tonnes harvested in 2024, up 16.1% year-on-year), dairy farming, and rapeseed cultivation. Agriculture contributes 3.8% of GDP and employs 6.8% of the active population, but the short season requires expensive artificial crop drying.

Forest cover stands at **52–55% of territory** (variation depends on classification methodology), making Latvia the fourth-most-forested country in Europe. Standing timber volume has increased 3.8 times over 80 years, and forest area has doubled since 1923. Wood products constituted **17.3% of total export value in 2023** (€3.3 billion), making forestry Latvia's single largest export sector. The state company LVM manages 46% of forests; the remainder is privately held. Forest policy carries political weight — the 1986–87 mass protests against a proposed Daugava dam that would have flooded forested river valleys became a catalyst for the independence movement. Today, tension persists between EU Biodiversity Strategy 2030 conservation targets and the timber industry's economic centrality.

Latvia's peatlands cover **10–12% of national territory** (more than 6,800 individual peatlands storing 1.7 billion tonnes of peat material), but 63% are drained, making them a big emissions source rather than a carbon sink. The LULUCF sector faces a projected **gap of about 4.8 MtCO₂-equivalent** against its 2030 EU target — Latvia's single most difficult climate compliance challenge. Multiple EU LIFE projects are underway to rewet degraded peatlands, with the LIFE PeatCarbon initiative targeting 5,076 hectares of restoration and 37,117 tonnes of annual CO₂-equivalent reduction.

<strong>The Daugava river creates Latvia's most consequential transboundary dependency</strong>

The Daugava (Western Dvina) is Latvia's strategic artery: 1,020 km total length, 352 km within Latvian territory, with a basin spanning Russia (21–23%), Belarus (38–39%), and Latvia (27–28%). **More than 60% of Latvia's annual river water volume originates in neighboring countries.** Three Soviet-era hydroelectric dams on the Latvian stretch — Pļaviņas, Ķegums, and Riga — generate up to 70% of Latvia's renewable electricity and about 90% of all domestically produced power.

This dependency carries acute vulnerability. In November 1990, a polymer complex in Navapolatsk, Belarus spilled 128 tonnes of cyanide derivatives into the Daugava with no warning to Latvia — detected only through dead fish. Formal transboundary water cooperation with Belarus and Russia is now effectively frozen due to the war in Ukraine. Latvia's Daugava River Basin Management Plan, required under the EU Water Framework Directive, lacks transboundary cooperation sections — a gap the European Commission flagged in 2019 and which remains unresolved.

Latvia holds the **HELCOM Chairmanship** and hosted the organization's 50th-anniversary ministerial meeting in Riga in April 2024. But Baltic Sea environmental cooperation operates in a "strategic pause" — work continues in "H9 format" (excluding Russia) while eutrophication from Daugava nutrient loads, harmful algal blooms, and marine biodiversity loss remain pressing concerns.

<strong>Climate change is intensifying weather extremes and coastal erosion</strong>

Average annual temperature has risen about **1.7°C since 1991**. Precipitation variability is increasing sharply: July 2024 brought Jelgava three months' worth of rain in 24 hours, causing severe urban flooding and multi-day power outages. Late July 2024 storms flattened harvest-ready fields. By 2100, sea levels along Latvia's 496.5 km coastline are projected to rise 32.6–50.3 cm, with coastal retreat of 47–72 meters. Storm surge flooding could become 20–70 times more frequent without adaptation.

Latvia's renewable energy profile is strong by EU standards: **43.2% of gross final energy consumption from renewables in 2023** (fourth-highest in EU), rising to 45.5% in 2024. Solar generation doubled year-on-year in 2024 (536 GWh). The updated National Energy and Climate Plan (July 2024) set an ambitious 61% renewables target for 2030. But total GHG emissions of 10.0 MtCO₂-equivalent remain stubbornly concentrated in transport (24% of total, barely changed since 1990), and only 17% of emissions fall under the EU Emissions Trading System — an unusually low share that concentrates compliance pressure on the Effort Sharing Regulation.

Latvia established a dedicated **Ministry of Climate and Energy in January 2023**, and conducted its first-ever nationwide cellular broadcast emergency notification test in November 2025. Environmental monitoring capacity remains constrained by the country's small size and limited resources, with public climate awareness low — only 22% of Latvians identified climate change as a top-four global problem (versus 46% EU average).

## XI. From laundering hub to sanctions enforcer — the illicit networks reckoning

<strong>The ABLV collapse triggered the most dramatic banking sector cleanup in EU history</strong>

Latvia's role as a money laundering conduit reached its apex in 2015, when **non-resident deposits exceeded 50% of all banking system deposits, totaling about €12.4 billion** — predominantly from Russia and CIS countries. The system's implosion came on February 13, 2018, when FinCEN designated ABLV Bank — Latvia's third-largest lender — as a "foreign bank of primary money laundering concern," finding the bank had "institutionalized money laundering as a pillar of its business practices." Specific findings included management-orchestrated laundering, transactions linked to North Korea's ballistic missile procurement, and bribery of Latvian officials to obstruct AML enforcement.

ABLV entered voluntary liquidation within days. In June 2023, co-owner Ernests Bernis and seven others were indicted for laundering **€2.1 billion** — the case remains before the courts. FinCEN formally withdrew its finding in September 2024, determining ABLV "no longer poses a threat to the U.S. financial system." The broader cleanup was sweeping: shell company accounts prohibited, the Financial Intelligence Unit made independent, non-resident deposits slashed from 53.4% (2015) to under 13% (2022), and multiple banks serving non-residents lost licenses. Latvia became the **first MONEYVAL-reviewed state to implement all 40 FATF recommendations**, and a November 2024 on-site evaluation acknowledged "substantial expertise and strong inter-agency cooperation" in ML investigations — which rose to 124 cases in 2024, compared to just 21 brought to court across 2013–2017.

<strong>Three oligarchs defined Latvia's state capture era — two are now criminally convicted or indicted</strong>

Latvia's post-Soviet political economy was shaped by three figures whose overlapping business empires and political parties constituted a textbook case of state capture. **Aivars Lembergs**, mayor of Ventspils from 1988 to 2021, controlled port transit operations (primarily Russian oil and fertilizer) through 29+ undeclared offshore companies, owned media outlets including six newspapers, and built a political alliance through the Union of Greens and Farmers (ZZS). Convicted in February 2021 after a 14-year trial, sentenced to five years (reduced to four on appeal in September 2023), Lembergs is also under U.S. Magnitsky Act sanctions since December 2019. His ZZS party remains in the current governing coalition.

**Ainārs Šlesers**, a former Deputy PM and Transport Minister, is implicated in the "Ridzene hotel" wiretap recordings that revealed systematic deal-making among the oligarchs, including trading political favors, concealing business ownership, and attempting to control the national airline airBaltic. He faces prosecution in the Digital TV fraud case alongside **Andris Šķēle**, three-time prime minister, whose People's Party controlled Latvian politics for a decade and whose associates orchestrated the acquisition of the Diena newspaper — Latvia's leading independent daily — in what was widely viewed as an attempt to silence critical journalism. Court proceedings in 2024 confirmed that all three oligarchs were beneficial owners behind Diena's financing.

The oligarchic era's structural peak ended with the 2011 parliamentary dissolution — only the second in Latvian history — which broke the oligarch parties' parliamentary lock. KNAB (the Corruption Prevention Bureau) has shown marked improvement under current leadership: 45 criminal proceedings initiated in 2025 (highest in five years), 35 sent to prosecution against 53 individuals (highest in a decade), and average investigation time cut to five months. Latvia's Transparency International CPI score of **59/100 (2024)** places it in the EU's middle tier — behind Lithuania (63) but ahead of several older member states.

<strong>Sanctions evasion has emerged as the new frontier of illicit activity</strong>

Latvia's 283 km border with Russia and 173 km border with Belarus create inherent vulnerability as a sanctions circumvention vector. A 2025 investigation revealed Latvia-based KLC Group exported about **\$1.3 million in industrial automation equipment** (Siemens, Schneider Electric, Intel products) to Russian defense contractors via Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan. Latvia's response has been aggressive: the FIU was designated as competent authority for sanctions implementation from April 2024, criminal penalties for sanctions evasion involving dual-use goods were raised to 12 years imprisonment (March 2025), and the Customs Board imposed 247 administrative penalties by June 2025. The institutional capacity built during the banking cleanup is now being redirected toward sanctions enforcement — a strategic asset that Latvia's EU partners increasingly recognize.

Russian intelligence activity in Latvia has intensified sharply since 2022. The VDD (State Security Service) detained **10 espionage suspects in 2023 alone** — matching the total of the previous seven years combined. Cases include a Russian citizen gathering intelligence on military sites and critical infrastructure (detained December 2023, found with explosives), four suspected Russian-directed saboteurs who committed arson against a defense company, and MEP Tatjana Ždanoka, under investigation since 2024 for systematically reporting to FSB officers since at least 2004. The SAB's 2024 annual report assessed that Russian sabotage operations across Europe "significantly increased" and warned of capabilities targeting critical infrastructure.

## XII. An information ecosystem undergoing wartime restructuring

<strong>The January 2025 public media merger created Latvia's largest-ever news organization</strong>

Latvia's media landscape was reshaped when LTV, Latvijas Radio, and LSM.lv merged into a single entity — **Latvijas Sabiedriskais Medijs (LSM)** — on January 2, 2025, following a Saeima vote of 75 to 1 in January 2024. The merged entity is governed by a five-member Board of Directors chaired by Baiba Zūzena (formerly CEO of All Media Latvia), with unified editorial leadership under Editor-in-Chief Anita Brauna (appointed April 2025). Funding, fixed at 0.13% of GDP, reached **€70.27 million in 2025** — a historically large increase from €47.64 million in 2024. The broadcaster exited the advertising market entirely in 2021, removing commercial pressure but deepening state funding dependence.

The commercial landscape is dominated by TV3 Group (owned by Providence Equity Partners via Bitė Group, Lithuania), which operates Latvia's most-watched commercial channel and controls 38.7% of the domestic TV advertising market. Delfi.lv (owned by Estonia's Ekspress Grupp) is the leading commercial news portal. The weekly magazine *Ir*, founded by journalists who departed after oligarchs captured legacy newspapers, has emerged as Latvia's highest-quality independent analytical publication alongside Re:Baltica, the country's premier investigative journalism outfit and a member of both OCCRP and the Global Investigative Journalism Network.

<strong>Banning Russian media created an information vacuum that digital platforms partially fill</strong>

Latvia's approach to Russian-language media has been the most aggressive in Europe. By June 2022, all Russia-registered television channels were banned under Electronic Mass Media Law amendments targeting countries that threaten another state's territorial integrity. The independent Russian channel TV Rain (Dozhd), which relocated to Riga after fleeing Russia, had its license revoked in December 2022 after an anchor made on-air comments interpreted as supporting Russian forces — a decision RSF and international press freedom organizations criticized as disproportionate. A Latvian court overturned the revocation in July 2025, though TV Rain had already moved operations to Amsterdam.

The most consequential policy shift came with the September 2023 National Security Concept, which mandated that from January 2026, all public media content must be produced only in Latvian and "languages belonging to the European cultural space" — explicitly excluding Russian. LTV7 dropped Russian-language programming in January 2025; **Radio 4, Latvia's Russian-language public radio station, ceased broadcasting on January 1, 2026**, after playing the national anthem. The digital-only LSM+ portal (launched August 2024) provides content in Russian, English, Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Polish, but its reach among older Russian-speaking demographics is uncertain.

This poses an intelligence dilemma. About **25–30% of Latvia's population** is Russian-speaking. The EBU, RSF, IPI, and European Federation of Journalists issued joint statements of "extreme concern." Freedom House warned that "Russian speakers will be left without a dependable source of news and information in Russian." The vacuum has been partially filled by Telegram, where at least 14 channels were identified spreading pro-Kremlin narratives after the 2022 invasion, and by satellite dishes that can still receive Russian federal channels (technically not punishable for personal viewing). Social media penetration is high: 93.9% internet access, with WhatsApp (77%), YouTube (75.6%), Facebook (48.1%), and TikTok (42.4%) as leading platforms. Telegram's role is especially significant among Russian speakers.

Latvia's RSF Press Freedom Index ranking stands at **15th globally (2025, score 81.82)**, but RSF characterizes the situation as "two-speed freedom" — qualitatively different media environments for Latvian-speakers and Russian-speakers. The NATO Strategic Communications Centre of Excellence, headquartered in Riga since 2014 under Director Jānis Sārts, published NATO's first strategic communications fundamentals doctrine in 2024 and operates as the alliance's primary research hub on information warfare, with experts from 17 nations.

## XIII. Institutional embeddedness so deep that defection is structurally impossible

<strong>Latvia's compliance record across Western institutions is above average and improving</strong>

Latvia occupies a position of maximum institutional integration: simultaneous membership in the EU (2004), Eurozone (2014), NATO (2004), Schengen (2007), OECD (2016), Council of Europe (1995), and WTO (1999), layered with participation in the Three Seas Initiative, Baltic Assembly, NB8, JEF, Bucharest Nine, and European Sky Shield Initiative. The EU Single Market Scoreboard (December 2025) shows Latvia as one of only three member states performing better than the EU average across all infringement indicators. Its compliance trajectory has improved over time, though transposition delays occasionally occur — Latvia was among 23 states receiving formal notice for late NIS2 Directive transposition in October 2024, and resolved the issue by July 2025.

EU cohesion funding for 2021–2027 totals **€4.6 billion** (€5.4 billion including co-financing), covering renewable energy, research, social infrastructure, and maritime programs. Latvia's GDP per capita has converged from 47% of the EU average at accession to about 72% — the single most powerful data point showing the material benefits of membership. Eurozone participation, adopted at the height of the euro crisis, eliminated speculative currency attacks, reduced sovereign borrowing costs, and provided ECB liquidity access — critical advantages for a small, open economy that experienced an 18% GDP contraction in 2009.

<strong>The ECHR's "constitutional identity" doctrine is a significant legal innovation</strong>

Latvia's relationship with the European Court of Human Rights has produced landmark jurisprudence on minority rights. In **Savickis and Others v. Latvia (2022)**, the Grand Chamber found no violation in differential pension treatment of non-citizens — the first time in ECHR history that "protection of constitutional identity" was accepted as a legitimate aim for differential treatment, despite seven dissenting judges. Subsequent cases (Valiullina, 2023; Džibuti, 2023) upheld Latvia's education reforms mandating Latvian-language instruction, further expanding the constitutional identity doctrine. Academic criticism has been severe — described as "hijacking human rights" in Verfassungsblog — but the rulings provide Latvia significant legal cover for its language and minority policies.

The non-citizen (nepilsoņi) population, once numbering 193,000, has declined through naturalization and attrition. Non-citizens retain permanent residence rights but cannot vote nationally or hold certain government positions. The 2022 education reform effectively ended Russian-language minority schooling, with a September 2025 compliance deadline.

<strong>Baltic-Nordic security convergence has created a qualitatively new regional bloc</strong>

The NB8 format (Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Iceland, Latvia, Lithuania, Norway, Sweden) has evolved from a consultative forum into what the Atlantic Council (January 2026) characterized as "Europe's reliable security engine." With Finland and Sweden's NATO accession, all eight members are now NATO allies for the first time. Operational outputs include a **joint \$500 million military equipment package for Ukraine** (November 2025), regular NB8+Ukraine joint statements, and expanding engagement with the Weimar Triangle (first NB8+Weimar foreign ministers meeting, April 2025).

The UK-led Joint Expeditionary Force, encompassing all NB8 states plus the Netherlands, held Exercise Joint Protector 25 in Latvia with several thousand troops. The 3+3 format (Canada-Germany-UK as framework nations with Latvia-Lithuania-Estonia as host nations) provides dedicated coordination for the three NATO forward-deployed brigades. Latvia's contribution to these frameworks extends beyond hosting: it co-leads the international Drone Coalition with the UK (20 participating countries) and is recognized for niche expertise in strategic communications.

Rail Baltica remains the flagship Three Seas Initiative infrastructure project — 265 km of new European-gauge rail in Latvia (870 km total across the Baltics) — though cost estimates have ballooned from €3.5 billion to **€23.6 billion**. The military mobility dimension was elevated post-2022. A parallel strategic milestone: the Baltic states completed electricity grid synchronization with continental Europe in **February 2025**, permanently disconnecting from the Russian/Belarusian grid.

<strong>Defection from Western institutions would be economically and existentially fatal</strong>

Latvia's institutional lock-in operates across mutually reinforcing dimensions. EU departure would forfeit €4.6 billion in cohesion funding, disrupt the dominant trading relationship, and create an immediate currency crisis (the eurozone has no withdrawal mechanism). NATO exit would remove the 3,500+ multinational brigade, eliminating the only credible deterrent against a Russian military with which Latvia shares a 283-kilometer border. Latvia's 7,870-person active military cannot independently defend the country. Energy desynchronization from Russia (completed February 2025) means survival depends on European grid access. Trade reorientation away from Russia (completed post-2022) means economic viability depends on EU market access. Defense spending at nearly 5% of GDP is sustainable only through joint procurement programs and allied cost-sharing arrangements. **Latvia's institutional defection is structurally impossible without state collapse** — the economic cost alone would approach 15–20% of GDP, layered on existential security exposure.

## XIV. A military undergoing the fastest transformation in NATO

<strong>From 0.9% to 4.91% of GDP in twelve years — Latvia's defense spending trajectory has no NATO parallel</strong>

Latvia's defense budget trajectory is among the most dramatic in alliance history. From a post-financial-crisis nadir of **0.9% of GDP in 2014**, spending reached 3.3% in 2024 (€1,359 million), 3.80% in 2025 (€1,593 million), and **4.91% in 2026 (€2,158 million)** — the first time exceeding €2 billion, approved by the Saeima on December 4, 2025. Latvia is targeting 5% by 2027, aligned with the NATO Hague Summit commitment (June 2025) that set a 5% guideline (3.5% core defense plus 1.5% defense-related spending) by 2035. Latvia, Poland, and Lithuania are the only NATO allies meeting the 3.5% core defense target.

The 2026 allocation breaks down as 53% (€1.14 billion) for capability development, €135 million for military infrastructure (including the new Sēlija training area), €55 million for eastern border fortification, €28 million for cybersecurity, and €110 million (0.25% of GDP) for Ukraine support. The SAB's assessment that Russia could pose a direct military threat within five years if the Ukraine war freezes is the primary driver. Fiscal consequences are real: general government deficit is forecast at 4.3% of GDP by 2027, with debt-to-GDP rising from 46.6% (2024) to an estimated 54.6%.

<strong>Conscription's return has been more successful than anticipated but faces scaling challenges</strong>

Latvia reinstated mandatory military service effective January 1, 2024 — the last Baltic state to do so. Male citizens aged 18–27 serve 11 months (the longest in the Baltics), with alternatives including five-year National Guard service or reserve officer programs for university students. Volunteers receive **€600/month plus a €1,100 completion bonus** and free tertiary education; conscripts receive €300/month.

Implementation has relied overwhelmingly on volunteers. Across the first four intakes (July 2023 through January 2025), 1,076 recruits entered service — but only **64 were compulsorily drafted** via random selection, with the remainder volunteering. The service completion rate stands at 95%. Public support reached 64% in March 2024 but declined to 53.8% by April 2025, with a striking linguistic divide: 70.7% of Latvian speakers support conscription versus just 23.7% of Russian speakers. Scaling targets call for 4,000 annual conscripts by 2028 and 7,500 within five years, requiring much more infrastructure and training capacity.

<strong>The NAF Development Plan envisions a force of 31,000 peacetime and 61,000 wartime</strong>

The National Armed Forces Development Plan 2025–2036 (adopted July 23, 2024) sets peacetime strength targets of **8,000 professional soldiers, 4,000 national defense service members, 12,000 National Guards, and 7,000 high-readiness reservists** — totaling 31,000 by 2030, with wartime mobilization capacity of 61,000. Current strength stands at about 7,870 professionals and 10,000 National Guards (the latter growing from 8,100 in early 2022, with plans announced in March 2025 to double to 20,000 through simplified entry requirements).

Major procurement programs are transforming combat capability across all domains:

- **ASCOD Hunter infantry fighting vehicles**: 84 ordered from GDELS (Spain) in two contracts totaling €760 million, with first delivery June 2026 and local assembly at the Defence Partnership Latvia facility in Valmiera
- **M142 HIMARS**: 6 launchers plus ATACMS missiles (\$179.8 million, delivery from 2027) — all three Baltic states purchasing jointly
- **Archer 155mm self-propelled howitzers**: 18 systems from BAE Systems/Sweden, delivery from 2026 with Swedish interim leasing
- **IRIS-T SLM medium-range air defense**: Joint procurement with Estonia from Diehl Defence, deliveries 2025–2026
- **Naval Strike Missile coastal defense**: Kongsberg/Raytheon, about \$110 million, operational around 2027
- **Patria 6x6 armored personnel carriers**: 200+ ordered under the CAVS program, with a full-cycle production facility opened in Valmiera in May 2024 — the only complete armored vehicle plant in the Baltics

The Defence Partnership Latvia facility in Valmiera represents a qualitative shift: Latvia now possesses genuine domestic defense industrial capacity for the first time, producing both Patria 6x6 APCs and assembling ASCOD IFVs with a 30% local content requirement. A Rheinmetall-partnered **155mm ammunition factory** began construction in Iecava in early 2026.

<strong>NATO's most multinational brigade anchors the forward defense posture</strong>

The NATO Multinational Brigade Latvia (MNB-LVA), formally stood up in July 2024, evolved from the Enhanced Forward Presence battlegroup established in 2017 with Canada as framework nation. It is now **NATO's most multinational military unit**, with 14 contributing nations and about 3,500 troops. Canada plans 2,200 persistently deployed personnel by 2026, with PM Mark Carney extending Operation REASSURANCE through 2029 (August 2025). Denmark and Sweden rotate mechanized battalions — Sweden's deployment marking its first-ever NATO force contribution. Spain provides a reinforced armored infantry company with Leopard 2E tanks.

The **Baltic Defence Line** — a joint Estonian-Latvian-Lithuanian defensive fortification along the eastern border — began construction in May 2024 following a January 2024 joint defense ministers' announcement. Latvia's five-year investment totals €303 million, covering anti-tank ditches, dragon's teeth obstacles, Czech hedgehogs, reinforced guard posts, and 25 anti-mobility material parks. A 280-kilometer border fence was completed by end-2025. Poland joined the initiative with its "East Shield" program, creating a roughly 1,360-kilometer defensive perimeter. Foreign Minister Baiba Braže acknowledged the full Baltic Defence Line could take "up to a decade" to complete.

<strong>Three intelligence services face an escalating Russian threat with growing but still limited capacity</strong>

Latvia's security architecture rests on three services with distinct mandates. The **Constitution Protection Bureau (SAB)** handles intelligence, counterintelligence, and state secrets protection; its 2024 report assessed that Russian intelligence services are "developing capabilities to organize sabotage with greater intensity, scale, and increasingly important targets." The **State Security Service (VDD)** conducts counterintelligence, counterterrorism, and is the only service with pre-trial investigation authority; it detained 10 espionage suspects in 2023 alone, including a Russian citizen found with explosives near military sites. The **Defence Intelligence and Security Service (MIDD)** provides military intelligence and security vetting, including authority to disqualify conscripts on security grounds.

Cyber defense operates at a high and rising threat level. CERT.LV processed 671 incidents in Q3 2025, with Russian hacktivist DDoS attacks now an "integral part" of Latvian cyberspace. The National Guard's Cybersecurity Unit (established 2013) is expanding, and the 2026 budget allocates €28 million for cybersecurity. Latvia participates in NATO CCDCOE exercises (Locked Shields) and hosted a Canadian-Latvian Threat Hunting Workshop in November 2025.

The comprehensive defense concept — *valsts visaptverošā aizsardzība* — integrates military, civilian, economic, psychological, and societal dimensions, modeled on the Finnish total defense approach. A mandatory "State Defence Subject" was introduced in secondary schools from September 2024, reaching **42,000 pupils** in the 2025/2026 academic year. The Jaunsardze (Youth Guard) movement, with about 8,000 members aged 10–21, is Latvia's largest youth organization.

---

<strong>Latvia as bellwether for the European security transition</strong>

Latvia's transformation across all five domains reveals a state that has made an irreversible civilizational bet on the Western institutional order, and is now paying the fiscal, social, and political costs of that bet with unusual intensity. Three dynamics stand out.

First, **Latvia's defense spending commitment is economically unsustainable without continued allied engagement.** A country of 1.8 million people spending nearly 5% of GDP on defense while running a 4.3% fiscal deficit is performing a burden-sharing function that far exceeds its economic weight. If major NATO allies fail to match Latvia's proportional commitment, domestic political support — already declining from 64% to 54% on conscription — will erode.

Second, **the information ecosystem restructuring represents a high-risk experiment.** Banning all Russian-language public broadcasting while 25–30% of the population speaks Russian creates an information vacuum that Telegram and satellite workarounds partially fill with precisely the narratives Latvia seeks to counter. The digital-only LSM+ alternative may prove insufficient for older demographics. Latvia is effectively betting that linguistic integration can be accelerated through media policy — a strategy with no clear precedent at this scale.

Third, **Latvia's institutional lock-in is its most significant strategic asset.** The simultaneous embeddedness in the EU, Eurozone, NATO, Schengen, and multiple regional frameworks creates such dense interdependence that institutional defection is impossible without state collapse. This is not a constraint but a feature — it provides Latvia with a credibility that few small states can match, enabling it to punch well above its demographic weight in alliance deliberations. The concurrent electricity grid desynchronization from Russia (February 2025) and physical border fortification represent the material completion of a geopolitical reorientation that began in 1991. Latvia's frontier position means its security architecture is being stress-tested first — the results will shape NATO strategy for the entire eastern flank.

## XVI. Civil society and dissent infrastructure

<strong>The Singing Revolution bequeathed durable institutional DNA</strong>

Latvia's modern civil society traces directly to the 1986–91 national awakening. The **Environmental Protection Club (VAK)**, founded **28 February 1987**, channeled opposition to Soviet megaprojects — the Daugava hydroelectric dam and Riga metro — into the first mass political mobilization. Helsinki-86, founded the same year, organized the landmark **14 June 1987 flower-laying at the Freedom Monument**, widely considered the spark of the Third Awakening. The **Latvian Popular Front (LTF)**, established summer 1988 with **250,000 members**, provided the organizational architecture for independence. The **Baltic Way** of 23 August 1989 — two million people forming a 600 km human chain — remains the defining image.

The **Barricades of January 1991** cemented civil society's role in statebuilding. After Soviet OMON attacked the Lithuanian TV tower on 13 January, LTF leader **Dainis Īvāns** broadcast a 4:45 AM radio call to defend strategic buildings. Up to **700,000 people** (one-third of the population) participated. Six people died, including filmmaker Andris Slapiņš. Over **32,000** Barricade Defenders received commemorative medals. The 35th anniversary in January 2026 featured bonfire-lighting at the Saeima and Īvāns himself speaking at events, showing the living continuity between independence-era mobilization and current democratic identity.

<strong>Current landscape: vibrant journalism, underfunded NGOs</strong>

Latvia's civil society in 2026 features **25,600 registered NGOs**, though only roughly half are active and most have no employees. The sector's financial activity is declining, creating a structural vulnerability beneath surface-level organizational density.

The think tank ecosystem punches above its weight. **Centre for Public Policy PROVIDUS** (est. 2002) leads on governance and anti-corruption research, funded by the German Embassy, European Commission, OECD, and Open Society Institute. The **Latvian Institute of International Affairs (LIIA)**, directed by **Dr. Karlis Bukovskis** since 2022, publishes the annual *Foreign and Security Policy Yearbook* and hosts the Riga Dialogue and Riga Security Forum. LIIA receives project funding from NED, Freedom House, the German Marshall Fund, and Konrad Adenauer Foundation. The **NATO Strategic Communications Centre of Excellence**, headquartered in Riga, provides an additional layer of analytical capacity.

Investigative journalism is Latvia's civil society crown jewel. **Re:Baltica** (founded 2011 by Inga Spriņģe and Kristīna Rizga, managed by Sanita Jemberga) operates as a cross-Baltic investigative hub and IFCN-verified fact-checker through **Re:Check**. Its investigations into Russian sabotage packages routed through Riga and sanctioned oligarch Pyotr Aven's visitors to Latvia have had direct policy impact. **Ir magazine** provides sustained political analysis. The public broadcaster **LSM**'s "De Facto" program serves as the primary broadcast investigative vehicle. Re:Baltica's 2025 innovation — providing content to regional media to address news deserts ahead of June 2025 municipal elections — signals awareness of the information-gap threat in a country where media is heavily Riga-concentrated.

Labor organization remains weak. The **Free Trade Union Confederation (LBAS)** has about **100,000 members** (down from 275,000 in 1995), organized across 20 industry affiliates. The largest, **LIZDA** (education), accounts for 30,220 members. Only \~7% of members are under 35. The **Employers' Confederation (LDDK)**, led by president **Andris Bite**, represents firms employing 44% of the workforce. Both participate in the **National Tripartite Cooperation Council (NTSP)**, but labor's political leverage is modest.

<strong>Funding gap and foreign support architecture</strong>

A critical funding gap emerged in 2024–2025 when the **Active Citizens Fund** (EEA/Norway Grants) ended. The new €9.88 million Civil Society Support Fund under the 2021–2028 EEA/Norway Grants agreement (signed 12 September 2024) will not distribute until 2025 at earliest. The state **National NGO Fund** provides only \~€2.5 million annually, and Civic Alliance–Latvia has called for at least €4.1 million in additional state funding.

German political foundations have a strong presence. **Konrad Adenauer Foundation** has operated a Baltic regional office from Riga since 1993 and co-organizes the annual Riga Conference. **Friedrich Ebert Foundation** co-publishes LIIA's yearbook. **Heinrich Böll Foundation** supports Riga Conference events. The **Open Society Foundations** fund PROVIDUS and Re:Baltica projects. The **Sigrid Rausing Trust** (UK) funds Re:Baltica. NED funding is primarily indirect, channeled through think tanks rather than direct democracy programming — appropriate given Latvia's consolidated democratic status. The Trump administration's 2025 USAID cuts have broader regional implications but affect Latvia less directly than non-EU states.

Latvia **has not adopted a "foreign agent" law**. Discussions on an NGO transparency register are ongoing (DELNA/Transparency International Latvia monitors this), but parliament has shown awareness of how such laws have been misused in Russia and Georgia. At the EU level, the European Commission's proposed Directive on transparency of foreign influence initially generated CSO opposition.

<strong>Religious institutions: the Orthodox autocephaly revolution</strong>

The most consequential church-state development since independence was the **8 September 2022** Saeima vote mandating full autocephaly for the **Latvian Orthodox Church (LOC)**, severing ties with the Moscow Patriarchate. The law took effect **1 January 2023**. Metropolitan **Aleksandrs (Kudryashov)**, head since 1990, accepted the changes and amended the LOC charter, though he reportedly still commemorates Patriarch Kirill during liturgy. The Latvian government published records showing Aleksandrs was recruited as a KGB agent in the 1980s. The law requires the LOC to notify the President's Chancellery of hierarchical appointments — implying national security vetting. Moscow called it "false autocephaly" and "blatant state interference" but has no operational lever to reverse it.

The **Evangelical Lutheran Church of Latvia (LELB)**, with about **250,000 members**, is the largest Protestant denomination. Theologically conservative (affiliated with LCMS/Missouri Synod rather than LWF mainstream), the LELB positions itself as a moral arbiter, particularly on social issues. Its conservative stance influenced the decades-long delay in passing civil unions (finally enacted 2023). The **Catholic Church** is strongest in Latgale but less politically prominent than the Lutheran and Orthodox institutions.

<strong>Russian-speaking civil society: accelerating marginalization</strong>

The post-2022 period has dramatically reshaped the Russian-speaking community's civic space. **Moscow House** in Riga — headquarters of Russia's "compatriot policy" — was transferred to state ownership by Saeima vote on 11 January 2024. The education transition to Latvian-only instruction proceeded on schedule: preschool from September 2023, general education from September 2025, with Russian as a second foreign language ending from 2026. From 2026, public media funding is allocated exclusively to Latvian and "languages belonging to the European cultural space," effectively ending Russian-language public broadcasting.

About **180,000 residents** held non-citizen status as of January 2024 (65.69% ethnic Russian). New immigration rules require Russian citizens to secure EU long-term residence, show A2 Latvian proficiency, and pass security screening by June 2025. Of \~25,000 Russian citizens affected, \~16,000 secured permanent residence, \~2,600 left voluntarily, and **841 received formal deportation notices** in October 2025. A recent study found pervasive "apathy, disbelief that their voice matters" among Russian-speakers. A protest against school language transition in August 2024 drew only 30 people — a stark marker of political disengagement.

<strong>Istanbul Convention protests mark civil society's largest mobilization since independence</strong>

The **October 2025 Istanbul Convention crisis** produced the largest protest action in Latvia in years. When the Saeima voted **56–32** on 31 October to withdraw from the Convention (ratified only in November 2023), over **10,000 people** gathered in Riga and other cities — with solidarity protests in the US and Australia. CSOs working on violence prevention faced intense disinformation campaigns. President Rinkēvičs exercised his suspensive veto on 3 November, and parliament agreed to postpone reconsideration until after the October 2026 elections. This mobilization showed that Latvia's civil society retains significant rapid-response capacity on progressive social issues, even as conservative backlash on "gender ideology" intensifies.

**Jaunsardze** (Young Guard), the state-funded military youth cadet body under the Ministry of Defence, has grown dramatically post-2022. Open to ages 10–21, it has trained an estimated **26,000 young people** by 2025, targeting 34,000. Activities now include drone (UAV) operator training. The program has signed cooperation agreements with **UK Army Cadet Forces** and **Poland's Central Military Recruitment Center**. On the progressive end, the youth organization **"Protests"** (affiliated with the Federation of Young European Greens) organizes climate marches, LGBTQ+ rights actions, and housing affordability protests.

## XVII. Alliance genealogy and patron-client relationships

<strong>From failed interwar neutrality to NATO's third-highest spender</strong>

Latvia's alliance posture follows directly from its 20th-century experience. The interwar republic's attempts at neutrality between Germany and the Soviet Union ended catastrophically with the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact's secret protocol assigning Latvia to Moscow's sphere. The lesson — that small-state neutrality invites predation — is the foundational axiom of post-1991 foreign policy. Latvia joined NATO's Partnership for Peace in 1994, received a Membership Action Plan in 1999, and acceded on **29 March 2004**.

The defense spending trajectory tells the strategic story. From **0.94% GDP in 2014** (with only 7.6% on equipment), Latvia climbed to the NATO 2% minimum by 2018 and then accelerated dramatically after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine: **3.3% GDP (€1.36 billion)** in 2024, **3.73–3.80% GDP (€1.59 billion)** in 2025, and a Saeima-approved **4.91% GDP (€2.16 billion)** for 2026. This makes Latvia the **third-highest NATO spender** by GDP share, behind only Poland (4.48%) and Lithuania (4.0%). Equipment procurement has risen from 7.6% to **37%** of the defense budget. The €2.16 billion 2026 budget allocates 53% (€1.14 billion) to capability enhancement, with major line items including **€303 million** for fighting vehicles, **€200 million** for air defense, **€181 million** for munitions, and **€159 million** for indirect fire systems.

<strong>NATO's forward posture: from tripwire to brigade</strong>

The NATO force presence in Latvia has undergone qualitative transformation. What began as an **Enhanced Forward Presence battlegroup** (2017) under Canadian framework nation leadership became the **Multinational Brigade Latvia (MNB-LVA)**, formally established **3 July 2024** with transfer of authority on **17 October 2024**. The brigade comprises about **3,000 personnel from 14 NATO nations**: Albania, Canada, Czechia, Denmark, Iceland, Italy, Latvia, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Poland, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, and Sweden. **Exercise RESOLUTE WARRIOR 25** (1–10 November 2025) tested the brigade's first deployment beyond Camp Ādaži, with \~2,400 troops operating across eastern Latvia with live-fire demonstrations.

Major procurement transforms Latvia's capability profile. The **\$179.8 million HIMARS contract** (signed 20 December 2023) delivers six launchers plus **ATACMS tactical ballistic missiles** (300 km range), with deliveries beginning 2027. Coordinated with Estonia (six HIMARS, delivered April 2025) and Lithuania (eight HIMARS, \~\$495 million), this creates about **20 HIMARS launchers across the Baltics** under a joint capability agreement signed in Riga, January 2024. The **IRIS-T SLM** air defense system (\~€600 million, from Germany's Diehl Defence, delivery 2026) was jointly procured with Estonia. The **Naval Strike Missile** coastal defense (\$105 million, Raytheon/Kongsberg, delivery 2026–2027), **84 ASCOD infantry fighting vehicles** (two batches of 42, €387 million each, with 30% local content), and **six Bayraktar TB2 drones** round out the transformation.

<strong>The United States: aligned messaging, underlying anxiety</strong>

Latvia has navigated the Trump 2.0 era with deliberate strategic messaging. On **24 January 2025**, Secretary Marco Rubio's first round of allied consultations included FM **Baiba Braže**, and both sides agreed to expand cooperation. Latvia has adopted Trump's "peace through strength" language and aligned with US energy priorities — over one-third of gas imports now come from US LNG sources. Ms. Braže stated in February 2025: "We are interested in strengthening energy cooperation with our strategic partner, the United States, including increasing LNG imports."

The **Baltic Security Initiative** provides the institutional backbone. US Congress approved **\$228 million** for Baltic defense capabilities in 2024, and the BSI was enshrined in the NDAA in December 2025 with bipartisan support (312 of 435 House members). Combined FY2023–2024 DoD security assistance to the three Baltic states exceeded **\$450 million**. Active US Foreign Military Sales cases with Latvia total about **\$339 million**. Latvia receives \~\$3.5 million annually in International Military Education and Training (IMET), with 150 Baltic students at US military academies.

The underlying anxiety is real but managed. Trump's ambiguous stance on Article 5 and temporary suspension of Ukraine military aid generated concern. Latvia hedges by accelerating European defense cooperation while maintaining maximum alignment with Washington to preserve the US commitment. No direct bilateral friction has been reported.

<strong>Canada: the indispensable framework partner</strong>

**Canada's commitment to Latvia is its largest overseas military mission** and arguably the most consequential component of Canadian defense policy outside North America. About **2,000 Canadian troops** serve under Operation REASSURANCE, with a target of **2,200 by 2026**. PM **Mark Carney** visited Latvia on **26 August 2025**, renewing Operation REASSURANCE for three years and building on PM Trudeau's **\$2.6 billion commitment** (July 2023). The **\$230 million CAD infrastructure investment** includes a **\$58 million** 2nd Line Maintenance Facility at Ādaži (17,000 sq m, groundbreaking 31 October 2025) — Canada's largest infrastructure project abroad in decades. Equipment deployed includes **15 Leopard 2A4M tanks**, Griffon and Chinook helicopters, and Spike anti-armor missiles. In December 2025, reports emerged that the Canadian Armed Forces is studying setting up a **permanent base in Latvia**.

<strong>The United Kingdom: drone coalition and JEF</strong>

The Latvia-UK relationship centers on two frameworks. The **International Drone Coalition**, co-led by Latvia and the UK, was formally established **14 February 2024** during a Ramstein meeting. It has grown to **18 member states**, delivered **€1.8 billion** in drone support to Ukraine in 2024, and committed **€2.75 billion** for 2025. Latvia contributed €20 million for drone buys plus €10 million for joint Latvian-Ukrainian industry projects. The **Drone Summit 2025** in Riga (28 May) drew 1,000+ participants and \~100 companies. Latvia opened an **Autonomous Systems Competence Center** on 3 September 2025. This co-leadership role gives Latvia outsized alliance influence for a country of 1.8 million.

Latvia is a founding member of the UK-led **Joint Expeditionary Force (JEF)** (2014 Letter of Intent), a 10-nation framework providing sub-Article 5 response options. JEF's **Baltic Sentry** enhanced vigilance activity, launched January 2025 after suspected sabotage of the Estlink 2 cable, shows the framework's operational relevance.

<strong>EU dependency and Nordic-Baltic convergence</strong>

Latvia receives **€4.6 billion in EU Cohesion Policy funding** for 2021–2027 (€5.4 billion with national co-financing), representing roughly **50% of total public investment**. Classified as a "less developed" region (GDP per capita below 75% EU average), Latvia qualifies for maximum co-financing rates up to 85%. This creates a deep structural dependency on EU transfers that shapes Latvia's Council voting behavior: consistently pro-Atlanticist, pro-Ukraine, pro-sanctions, and supportive of Ukraine's EU membership path.

The **Nordic-Baltic Eight (NB8)** has transformed from a talk-shop into a credible security actor. With Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) joining NATO, all eight members are now allies. The Baltic Sea is colloquially a "NATO lake." In November 2025, NB8 countries contributed a joint **\~\$500 million** to the Prioritized Ukraine Requirements List. The **NB8+Weimar Triangle** format (adding Poland, Germany, France) held its first FM-level meeting in Denmark in 2025, signaling the format's expanding ambition.

<strong>Zero hedging: firm Western commitment without alternatives</strong>

Latvia exhibits **zero multi-vector hedging** toward non-Western powers. It withdrew from China's 16+1 format on **11 August 2022** alongside Estonia, citing incompatibility with strategic objectives. Latvia has no relationship with BRICS or SCO. The only Global South outreach signal was NB8 foreign ministers' joint visit to New Delhi in February 2024. Latvia's strategy is entirely within the Western alliance framework, but it is **diversifying within the West** — deepening European, Nordic, and Canadian ties as insurance against potential US disengagement while maintaining maximum alignment with Washington. This is classic small-state bandwagoning with intra-alliance diversification, not geopolitical hedging.

The most exposed US dependency channels, in order: (1) military hardware supply chain (HIMARS, Black Hawks, ATACMS, \$339 million in active FMS cases); (2) intelligence and situational awareness capabilities; (3) extended deterrence and Article 5 psychological credibility; (4) BSI congressional funding (\$228 million annually); and (5) the nuclear umbrella, for which no European replacement exists.

## XVIII. Constitutional crises and regime transition patterns

<strong>Five regimes in a century: the pattern of external imposition and internal fragility</strong>

Latvia's regime history turns on the interplay of external great-power predation and internal institutional weakness. The **1918 republic** suffered extreme parliamentary fragmentation (22–24 parties in a 100-seat Saeima), producing 13 governments and nine prime ministers in 12 years. This instability enabled PM **Kārlis Ulmanis** to stage a bloodless coup on **15–16 May 1934**, dissolving the Saeima, banning all political parties (including his own), and merging the presidency with the premiership in 1936. The authoritarian regime was relatively benign by contemporary European standards, focused on "Latvianizing" the economy through state corporations.

External imposition then erased sovereignty entirely. The **Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact** (23 August 1939) assigned Latvia to the Soviet sphere. Soviet occupation began 17 June 1940, followed by sham elections and forced incorporation by 5 August 1940. The "Year of Terror" saw \~35,000 deportees. Nazi occupation (1941–44) brought the Holocaust and Reichskommissariat administration. The second Soviet occupation (1944–91) imposed collectivization, Russification, and mass immigration that reduced ethnic Latvians to barely a majority.

The independence restoration of **4 May 1990** (transitional declaration) and **21 August 1991** (full restoration) was constitutionally framed as continuity, not creation. The 1922 Satversme was reaffirmed and fully reinstated for the June 1993 elections, with citizenship restricted to pre-1940 citizens and descendants.

<strong>Seventeen prime ministers in 33 years: coalition instability as structural feature</strong>

Post-1991 Latvia has had **17 prime ministers** from 1990 to the present, with average government duration of roughly 1.5–2 years. No party has ever won more than \~33% of the vote. Coalitions typically comprise 3–4 parties spanning centre-right to right. Key figures include **Valdis Dombrovskis** (longest tenure, \~5 years, 2009–2014), **Krišjānis Kariņš** (\~4.5 years across two cabinets, 2019–2023), and current PM **Evika Siliņa** (September 2023–present). Typical collapse triggers include budget disagreements, corruption scandals, personality clashes, and oligarch influence. The ethnic Russian-majority party Harmony was systematically excluded from government throughout its period as the largest party (2011–2018) — an informal cordon sanitaire.

<strong>The 2011 dissolution: Latvia's anti-oligarch watershed</strong>

The only lawful Saeima dissolution in Latvian history occurred on **28 May 2011** when President **Valdis Zatlers** invoked Articles 48–50 after parliament voted to protect corruption-suspected MP **Ainārs Šlesers** from immunity-lifting. Mr. Zatlers declared parliament "feels comfortable in its atmosphere of lies." Under the constitution, dissolution required referendum confirmation — with the president automatically removed if voters rejected it. The Saeima retaliated by electing ZZS-linked **Andris Bērziņš** as Mr. Zatlers's replacement on 2 June. In the **23 July referendum**, **94.3%** voted for dissolution (turnout \~44%). The September 2011 snap elections eliminated the oligarch parties of Mr. Šlesers and Mr. Šķēle from parliament entirely, producing landmark anti-corruption reforms including public party financing and stricter donation rules.

The irony of 2026 politics is that **Šlesers has returned** via his **Latvia First (LPV)** party, founded August 2021, now polling at **\~14.8%** — second place. His Christian-fundamentalist, EU-skeptic, right-wing populist positioning shows the resilience of informal power networks despite institutional reform.

<strong>The Siliņa government: ideologically heterogeneous, politically fragile</strong>

PM **Evika Siliņa** formed Latvia's 42nd cabinet on **15 September 2023**, winning confidence 53–39 with an unusual coalition: **New Unity (JV)** (centre-right, PM + 7 ministries), **ZZS** (agrarian/centre-right, 4 ministries + Speaker Daiga Mieriņa), and **The Progressives** (centre-left, 3 ministries). Key ministers include Defense Minister **Andris Sprūds**, FM **Baiba Braže** (who replaced Kariņš after his March 2024 resignation over a charter-flight scandal), and Finance Minister **Arvils Ašeradens**.

The **Istanbul Convention crisis** of October 2025 exposed the coalition's central fissure. ZZS MPs broke ranks to vote **with the opposition** for Convention withdrawal, producing the **56–32** vote of 31 October that would have made Latvia the first EU country to withdraw. President Rinkēvičs's suspensive veto and parliament's agreement to postpone until after the 2026 elections saved the coalition, which survived to pass the 2026 budget on 4 December (52–42). Despite predictions that the government would not survive the parliamentary term, the coalition has endured about 2.5 years — relatively long by Latvian standards.

<strong>October 2026 election: seven parties, knife-edge arithmetic</strong>

The **3 October 2026** election projects to return seven parties above the 5% threshold. Based on SKDS polling data (November 2025 trend):

- **New Unity (JV)**: \~16.5% (\~22 seats) — governing, centre-right
- **Latvia First (LPV)**: \~14.8% (\~20 seats) — Mr. Šlesers's right-wing populist vehicle
- **Progressives**: \~11.8% (\~16 seats) — governing, centre-left
- **National Alliance**: \~11.1% (\~15 seats) — right/nationalist, opposition
- **ZZS**: \~8.1% (\~11 seats) — governing, agrarian
- **Sovereign Power (SV)**: \~6.8% (\~9 seats) — right-populist, EU-skeptic
- **For Stability! (S!)**: \~5.2% (\~7 seats) — replacing Harmony as main Russian-speaker party

The current JV+ZZS+Progressives coalition would hold \~49 seats — below the 51-seat majority, requiring a fourth partner. The opposition bloc (LPV+NA+S!+SV) would hold \~51 seats but is ideologically incoherent. A JV+LPV+NA combination (57 seats) is arithmetically viable but would require JV to partner with the very oligarch interests the 2011 dissolution targeted. Campaign issues center on defense spending, the Istanbul Convention, economic stagnation, and corruption.

<strong>Democratic consolidation: solid but not impregnable</strong>

Latvia's democratic indicators are strong but reveal specific weaknesses. **Freedom House** scored Latvia **89/100** (Free) in 2025, up from 88 in 2024 partly due to the civil unions law taking effect. The **Corruption Perceptions Index** score of **59/100** (rank 38th) is the worst among the Baltic states (Estonia: 76, Lithuania: 63) and fell short of Latvia's own target of 64. The EU Rule of Law Report flagged two persistent concerns: inadequate safeguards against political influence in **Supreme Court judge appointments** (no progress in 2024 or 2025) and the absence of a long-term anti-corruption planning document (not expected until 2028). The Judicial Academy became operational 1 January 2025, and the specialized Economic Court resolves cases in 6.9 months versus 30 months in the general judiciary. The **Constitutional Court** (Satversmes tiesa), led by President **Irēna Kucina** since November 2024, has adjudicated over 574 cases and upheld the education language transition in July 2024.

<strong>Presidential power: Rinkēvičs as activist diplomat-president</strong>

President **Edgars Rinkēvičs** (sworn in 8 July 2023) brings unprecedented foreign-policy depth to a constitutionally constrained office. As Latvia's longest-serving Foreign Minister (2011–2023) and a US National Defense University graduate, he has actively used his powers: nominating PM Siliņa, exercising his suspensive veto on the Istanbul Convention withdrawal, and chairing a reinvigorated **National Security Council**. His most consequential achievement is securing Latvia's **election to the UN Security Council** in June 2025 with 178 of 188 votes — the country's first-ever UNSC seat, with a presidency scheduled for **November 2026** (coinciding with the election campaign). Approval ratings have fluctuated: +45 net in February 2025, declining 7.7 points by June 2025, with the lowest support in Latgale (under 40%).

The NSC, chaired by the president and including the Speaker, PM, foreign, defense, and interior ministers, plus parliamentary committee chairs, makes **advisory/recommendatory decisions only** — except for binding recommendations on the Constitution Protection Bureau director. A new **National Crisis Management Center** under direct PM authority, approved early 2025, marks a big step toward centralizing crisis coordination.

## XIX. Collective memory, grievance, and sacred narratives

<strong>The occupation thesis as constitutional bedrock</strong>

Latvia's foundational political narrative is the **State Continuity Doctrine**: the Republic of Latvia proclaimed on 18 November 1918 never ceased to exist as a legal entity during the Soviet (1940–41, 1944–91) and Nazi (1941–44) occupations. The 1990 declaration was explicitly a "restoration," not a creation, of statehood. International legitimacy rests on the **Welles Declaration** (23 July 1940), by which the US refused to recognize Soviet annexation, and on consistent Western maintenance of Baltic diplomatic missions throughout the occupation period. The **European Court of Human Rights** upheld the doctrine in **Savickis and Others v. Latvia** (Grand Chamber, June 2022) and accepted Latvia's "constitutional identity" argument as justification for differential treatment of non-citizens in 2023.

The doctrine's policy consequences are far-reaching. Only pre-1940 citizens and descendants received automatic citizenship, creating the **non-citizen (nepilsoņi)** status that applied to \~300,000 people at independence. Latvia's government concluded in 2016 that the Soviet occupation inflicted **€185 billion** in economic damages. The **2014 Constitutional Preamble** enshrines the narrative in constitutional text: "The people of Latvia did not recognise the occupation regimes, resisted them and regained their freedom."

The **Museum of the Occupation of Latvia** (founded 1993, privately funded, **75,000+ items** including 2,300+ video testimonials) is the main vehicle for passing on this narrative. Its location at Riflemen Square in the former Soviet-era Museum of the Red Latvian Riflemen is itself a spatial reclamation. The museum covers the Holocaust, though some observers note it may underemphasize the extent of Latvian participation in killings. Leading scholar on the doctrine is **Dr. Ineta Ziemele**, former Constitutional Court president and former ECHR judge.

<strong>Deportation memory as living politics</strong>

The **June 14, 1941** deportation removed **15,443 Latvians** (\~0.80% of population) in a single night, with families separated — men to Gulag camps, women and children to Siberian settlements. Mortality rates were extreme, estimated at \~60%. The **March 25, 1949** Operation Priboi was larger: about **42,000 people** deported, over 70% women or children under 16, to break Forest Brothers resistance and force collectivization (93% of farms collectivized by year's end). Latvia lost about **20% of its population** during WWII from combined Soviet and Nazi occupations.

Both dates are observed as **Commemoration Day for the Victims of Communist Genocide** — the terminological choice "communist genocide" deliberately maps onto the Double Genocide framework. Processions from the Occupation Museum to the Freedom Monument are attended by the President, Speaker, and PM, with black-ribboned Latvian flags. In 2021 (80th anniversary of the first deportation), over **15,000 names** of deportees were read aloud across all 119 municipalities. The first public commemoration on 14 June 1987, organized by Helsinki-86, is considered a foundational spark of the national awakening — connecting deportation memory directly to independence.

<strong>Holocaust memory: recognition under persistent tension</strong>

The Holocaust destroyed about **70,000** of Latvia's \~93,000 pre-war Jewish population — over 90% of Latvian Jewry. The **Rumbula massacre** (30 November and 8 December 1941) killed about **25,000 Jews** — the second-largest two-day Holocaust atrocity after Babi Yar. It was carried out by Einsatzgruppe A under Friedrich Jeckeln, with direct assistance from the **Arajs Kommando** — a Latvian SD auxiliary unit of 300–500 members that directly killed **at least 26,000 people**, participated in the burning of Riga's Great Choral Synagogue (4 July 1941), and later conducted anti-partisan operations in Belarus.

Latvia's Holocaust recognition architecture is institutionally robust but emotionally shallow. The **Commission of the Historians of Latvia** (set up in 1998 under presidential authority) published **27+ volumes** across sub-commissions covering both Soviet and Nazi-era crimes. The Rumbula memorial (2002), Bikernieki memorial (2001), **239 memorial plaques** at mass killing sites, recognition of over **400 Righteous Among the Nations** (including Žanis Lipke, whose memorial museum operates in Riga), and observance of international remembrance days all show formal institutional commitment. But as Ilya Lensky, director of the Museum "Jews in Latvia," observed: "We recognize collaboration" because "our older brothers in Europe, United States are watching us" — acknowledging the EU/NATO accession conditionality that drove recognition.

The core analytical tension: a far greater proportion of Latvia's population has personal or familial experience with Soviet deportations than with the Holocaust, creating an asymmetry in public empathy. More than **40** Soviet-convicted Arajs Kommando members were **rehabilitated by Latvian courts** after independence despite evidence, a fact that undermines recognition credibility. A 2014 musical celebrating pre-war aviator **Herberts Cukurs** — who was the Arajs Kommando deputy commander assassinated by Mossad in 1965 — showed how Latvian public culture can compartmentalize collaboration history.

<strong>March 16: declining but unresolved</strong>

The **Latvian Legion** (15th and 19th Waffen Grenadier Divisions of the SS) comprised about **87,550 men** by July 1944, of whom **80–85% were conscripts**. The symbolic date — 16 March — commemorates the 1944 Battle of Velikaya River, the only time both divisions fought together under a Latvian commander. The commemoration was briefly designated an official remembrance day by the Saeima in 1998, revoked in 2000 under international pressure. The current status: March 16 is **not an official day**, senior officials do not attend in official capacity, but private commemoration continues. The 2025 procession drew about **250 participants** — well down from peaks. The **1950 US Displaced Persons Commission ruling** that "Baltic Waffen SS Units are to be considered as separate and distinct in purpose, ideology, activities and qualifications for membership from the German SS" provides the legal backstop, but the transfer of Arajs Kommando members into the Legion in 1943 creates an indelible historical link between killing units and the conscript force.

<strong>Victory Monument demolition: the cathartic rupture of 2022</strong>

The **79-metre Victory Monument** (erected 1985, architect Alexandr Bugaev) — informally called "Moscow's Finger" or *okupeklis* (occupation + monument) — served as the primary symbolic flashpoint between ethnic communities. By 2005, **\~260,000 people** gathered there for May 9 Victory Day celebrations. The demolition timeline accelerated after Russia's February 2022 invasion: Saeima suspended the Latvia-Russia memorial preservation agreement on 12 May (68–18), Riga City Council voted to dismantle on 13 May, and the Saeima passed the **Law on Prohibition and Dismantling of Objects Glorifying Soviet and Nazi Regimes** on 16 June with a 15 November deadline.

On **25 August 2022 at 16:42**, the obelisk was toppled without explosives. A June 2022 survey showed 49% supported demolition (**72% of ethnic Latvians vs. 9% of ethnic Russians**); 25% opposed (76% of ethnic Russians vs. 10% of ethnic Latvians). Russia's Investigative Committee launched a criminal case; FM Rinkēvičs quipped that Russia would receive "a dead donkey's ears, not compensation." The UN Human Rights Committee's request to halt removal was ignored. Across Latvia, **more than 100 Soviet monuments** were dismantled by the November deadline. The Constitutional Court upheld the law's constitutionality in **December 2023**, including the requirement that municipalities partially finance demolition (which amounted to only 0.018% of Daugavpils's budget).

<strong>Language as existential marker and the 2012 trauma</strong>

The **18 February 2012 language referendum** — triggered by a pro-Russian NGO collecting 187,378 signatures for Russian as a second state language — produced one of Latvia's highest-ever turnouts (**71.12%**) and a decisive rejection: **74.80% voted no** (821,722 votes). The regional breakdown revealed the ethnic geography: Kurzeme rejected 91.53%, while Latgale — the only region with a Russian-speaker majority — voted 55.80% in favor. President Bērziņš had threatened to resign if the measure passed. The political trauma drove the **2014 Constitutional Preamble** amendment enshrining Latvian as the sole official language. The ECtHR upheld subsequent Latvian-language education reforms in **Valiullina v. Latvia** (November 2023), accepting the constitutional identity argument.

<strong>The Double Genocide controversy and Western misreadings</strong>

The "Double Genocide" framework — asserting moral and legal equivalence between Nazi and Soviet crimes — is institutionally embedded in Latvia's commemorative architecture through the "Communist Genocide" terminology for deportations, the Commission of Historians' dual mandate, and Latvia's support for the **Prague Declaration** (June 2008) and **August 23** as the European Day of Remembrance for Victims of Stalinism and Nazism. Critics led by **Dovid Katz** (Defending History) argue this constitutes "Holocaust obfuscation without denying a single death," while **Efraim Zuroff** (Simon Wiesenthal Center) calls the Prague Declaration "sinister." The **Seventy Years Declaration** (2012) was issued as a counter-document. The core analytical criticism: while Soviet deportations were genuine atrocities targeting political/social classes, the Baltic peoples survived as nations — they were not subjected to an annihilation campaign. The Holocaust, by contrast, sought total racial extermination.

Western analytical misreadings typically apply post-colonial or minority-rights frameworks that fail to account for Latvia's occupation experience. The Latvian perspective frames language policy, citizenship restrictions, and monument removal not as ethnic discrimination but as decolonization — restoring a state that was colonized through population transfer and cultural suppression. The **Quincy Institute** (2025) characterized the Baltics' "maximalist anti-Russia stance" as risking "unintended escalation," but this reading underweights how far occupation memory amounts to a genuine, historically grounded threat perception rather than mere hawkishness.

<strong>How memory drives alliance behavior</strong>

Collective memory shapes foreign policy directly and openly. Latvia's national security adviser under PM Siliņa, **Airis Rikveilis**, stated: "This is not going to be 1940." Latvia's embassy in Berlin organized a February 2026 event titled "Latvia's Bitter Experience of Russian Occupation: Stories from the Past that Warn Europe Today" — using memory as a diplomatic tool. The UNSC seat is used as a platform for the occupation narrative's contemporary implications. European Parliament President Roberta Metsola acknowledged it took "way too long" for others to listen to the Baltics about the Russian threat. Latvia's 4.91% GDP defense spending, the Baltic Defence Line fortifications, border fencing, and urban shelter signage all reflect a society whose strategic culture is shaped not by abstract threat assessment but by lived intergenerational trauma.

## XX. Current political and security developments (2024–2026)

<strong>Economic recovery not enough to ease electoral pressure</strong>

Latvia's economy stagnated in 2024 (\~-0.3% initial estimate, later revised upward) before recovering to **2.5% YoY growth** in Q3 2025 and **2.9%** in Q4. Full-year 2025 growth settled at about **1.7%** (Bank of Latvia, revised down from 2.6%). The 2026 forecast ranges from 1.7% (European Commission) to 2.8% (Bank of Latvia), with the government budgeting 2.1%. Inflation fell sharply to 1.4% in 2024, rebounded to \~3.5% in 2025 on food and heating tariffs, and moderated to **2.9% HICP** by January 2026. Unemployment fell to **6.7%** in Q4 2025, with tight labor market conditions driving \~5.8% nominal wage growth. The fiscal position is strained: the budget deficit reaches **3.3% GDP** in 2026 due to defense spending, with public debt rising from 46.6% (2024) toward \~54.6% by 2027. The EU national escape clause was activated to let defense spending exceed fiscal rules.

<strong>Espionage surge and the Ždanoka case</strong>

Latvia has seen an **espionage surge** with 22 arrests since 2023, including three in early 2025. The most prominent case involves **Tatjana Ždanoka**, former Latvian MEP (2004–2024), whom leaked emails published by The Insider and Re:Baltica in January 2024 revealed as an FSB agent since at least 2004–2005, with two known handlers: Dmitry Gladey (2005–2013) and Sergei Beltyukov (2013–2017). The State Security Service (VDD) opened criminal proceedings in February 2024. The European Parliament imposed sanctions (€1,750) and limited her activities. As of early 2026, the investigation is complicated by a pending Belgian court ruling on the legality of a search at Ždanoka's Brussels residence. She denies all allegations.

Other espionage cases include a **72-year-old Russian citizen** sentenced to 8 years for gathering intelligence on critical infrastructure near Riga Airport (with explosives found), an Estonian and Ukrainian citizen detained for infrastructure monitoring, and a Latvian man arrested in September 2025 for sharing NATO troop locations and security protocols with Russian contacts via Telegram. VDD identifies Russia as the greatest security threat and notes growing use of Telegram for intelligence recruitment.

<strong>Energy grid synchronization transforms the strategic landscape</strong>

On **8–9 February 2025**, the Baltic states permanently disconnected from the Russian-controlled IPS/UPS (BRELL) electricity grid and synchronized with the Continental European Network — ending about 60 years of dependence on Moscow-controlled grid dispatch. The project cost exceeded **€1.2 billion** in EU co-financing through the Connecting Europe Facility. Kaliningrad became an electrical island. A November 2025 **Baltic 3+1 Energy Dialogue** with the US Secretary of Energy discussed LNG imports, critical infrastructure security, and small modular nuclear reactors. Suspected sabotage of the Estlink 2 cable (Christmas Day 2024) and a Latvia-Sweden fiber optic cable (January 2025) prompted NATO's **Baltic Sentry** enhanced vigilance activity.

<strong>Ukraine: unwavering support with per-capita leadership</strong>

Latvia's total support to Ukraine since February 2022 exceeds **€859 million** (0.7% of annual GDP for 2022–2024): military aid €540 million, humanitarian/government support €82 million, refugee assistance €225 million, and reform/reconstruction €12 million. In 2024, Latvia exceeded its 0.25% GDP military support pledge, delivering **€170 million** versus €112 million pledged. The 2025 target rose to 0.30% GDP; 2026 returns to 0.25%. Equipment delivered includes **42 Patria 6×6 APCs**, CVR(T) reconnaissance vehicles, self-propelled howitzers, radars, and about **5,000 drones** in 2024. Latvia authorized Ukraine to use all provided weapons to strike inside Russian territory.

Latvia's position on peace negotiations is unambiguous: advocacy for a "just, lasting and comprehensive peace" ensuring Ukraine's rights under the UN Charter, promotion of Ukraine's Peace Formula, and **opposition to territorial concessions**. The two countries signed a bilateral security agreement in April 2024 (10-year commitment). Latvia's UNSC seat, beginning January 2026, provides a unique platform; FM Braže warned in March 2026 that US/Israel strikes on Iran must not push Ukraine off the international agenda.

About **32,000 Ukrainian refugees** reside in Latvia with temporary protection. UNHCR's 2025 survey found 62% employment, 41% with effective Latvian communication skills, 80% school enrollment, and 72% intending to remain. But public support for receiving refugees declined from **73.5%** (December 2022) to **46.8%** (late 2024).

<strong>Russia and Belarus: near-zero diplomatic relations, active hybrid threat</strong>

Latvia has virtually no diplomatic relations with Russia — embassy staff drastically reduced, Russia declared a "state sponsor of terrorism." The Belarus border has high fencing, with Latvia restricting pedestrian/non-motorized crossings at three remaining checkpoints in March 2025. The 2026 defense budget allocates **€55 million** for eastern border reinforcement. Persistent DDoS attacks from Russia-linked hacktivists (NoName057, Anonymous Guys) target government and critical infrastructure, surging with each Ukraine aid announcement. Latvia updated its Criminal Law in mid-2024 to criminalize deepfake political manipulation. About **20% of Russian-speaking viewers** still access banned Russian state TV via VPNs.

## XXI. Bilateral relationships with monitored middle powers

<strong>Latvia-Estonia: the deepest bilateral partnership</strong>

The Latvia-Estonia relationship is the most integrated among Latvia's bilateral ties. A **second joint government session** (27 August 2025, Tartu) produced joint decisions across five domains: security/crisis preparedness, connectivity, EU MFF negotiations, skills/competitiveness, and digital solutions. Joint projects include the **Baltic Defence Line** (Latvia investing €303 million over five years; 280 km fence on the Russia border completed by end-2025), **Baltic Innovation Fund 3** (launched September 2025), the fourth Estonia-Latvia electricity transmission link under construction, and coordinated fuel supply security and export/investment action plans. Military cooperation operates through the Baltic Defence College (Tartu), NATO StratCom CoE (Riga), MND North (Ādaži/Karup), and joint HIMARS and IRIS-T procurement. Friction is minimal — limited to EU MFF negotiation coordination.

<strong>Latvia-Lithuania: Rail Baltica strains an otherwise strong relationship</strong>

Security cooperation mirrors the Estonia relationship: Baltic Defence Line, coordinated HIMARS procurement (January 2024 MoU), Baltic Council of Ministers (Latvia chairing 2025), and near-identical Russia/Ukraine positioning. Energy interconnection is strong after grid synchronization, with Klaipėda LNG and the Lithuania-Poland GIPL pipeline ensuring regional gas access.

**Rail Baltica** is the primary friction point. The 870 km high-speed rail project (Tallinn-Riga-Kaunas-Polish border) has suffered severe cost overruns — from \~€6 billion a decade ago to potentially **€15.3 billion** for Phase 1 (€23.8 billion total), with Latvia's share at \~€5.5 billion. Phase 1 single-track completion is now targeted for 2030, pushed from the original 2025 timeline. European Court of Auditors reports note delays are likely to increase further, with no implementation schedule for Phase 2. Latvia's section may not be fully operational before 2035. An EU Commission implementing decision (9 July 2025) set new milestones. The parties signed a €1.77 billion electrification contract in September 2025. The project's dual-use significance for NATO military mobility (25-ton axle load for tanks, €51 million EU military mobility fund to Latvia in January 2025) keeps political commitment high despite frustrations.

<strong>Latvia-Poland: Suwalki solidarity without bilateral friction</strong>

The Suwalki Gap — the \~65 km corridor between Poland and Lithuania connecting the Baltics to the rest of NATO — defines the Latvia-Poland security relationship. Poland's **East Shield** program (\$2.5 billion, 700 km of fortifications along Russia/Belarus borders, launched November 2024) directly benefits Baltic security. Polish troops contribute an armored company (Leopard 2PL tanks) to MNB-LVA. Both countries are members of the **Bucharest Nine**, **Three Seas Initiative**, and **European Sky Shield Initiative**. Political alignment on Russia, Ukraine, and defense spending is near-total. No bilateral friction points stand out.

<strong>Latvia-Sweden: a landmark first NATO deployment</strong>

Sweden's first major NATO deployment since its **7 March 2024** accession is to Latvia. The Swedish Parliament approved long-term battalion rotations on 12 December 2024, with **550 troops** from the 71st Mechanized Infantry Battalion arriving 18 January 2025 with CV90 infantry fighting vehicles, APC360 APCs, and Stridsvagn 122 (Leopard 2) tanks. The transfer of authority ceremony (7 February 2025) was attended by PM Kristersson, Defence Minister Jonsson, FM Malmer Stenergard, and Chief of Defence Claesson — underlining its political weight. **King Carl XVI Gustaf** visited Camp Valdemar at Ādaži. Sweden and Denmark now alternate battalion responsibility on \~6-month rotations. A second Swedish deployment with enhanced air defense systems (CV90 LVKV 90) is planned for 2026. Both countries participate in the **CAVS Framework** for Patria 6×6 procurement (Finland-Sweden-Latvia core, expanded to include Germany, Denmark, UK, and Norway by September 2025).

<strong>Latvia-Denmark: structural defense integration through MND North</strong>

Denmark is co-framework nation with Latvia and Estonia for **Multinational Division North**, with HQ elements at Ādaži and Karup. Denmark contributes a rotating battalion to MNB-LVA (peak deployment 850 troops in 2024) and alternates with Sweden. A **state visit by King Frederick X and Queen Mary** (28–29 October 2025) produced a letter of intent to expand defense cooperation. Denmark joined the CAVS Framework (April 2025) and is a Drone Coalition member and contributor to the common fund.

<strong>Latvia-Canada: the defining bilateral defense relationship</strong>

Canada's role as framework nation for MNB-LVA, with \~2,000 troops, \$2.6 billion total commitment, \$230 million CAD infrastructure investment, and potential permanent basing, makes this Latvia's **single most important bilateral defense relationship**. PM Carney's August 2025 visit renewing Operation REASSURANCE for three years and the December 2025 study on permanent basing signal deepening commitment. Canada deployed 15 Leopard 2A4M tanks (end 2023), Griffon and Chinook helicopters, and serves as the organizational hub for 14 contributing nations.

<strong>Latvia-UK: co-leadership and sub-threshold response</strong>

Beyond the Drone Coalition co-leadership (€4.5 billion cumulative commitments, 18 member states) and JEF founding membership, the UK-Latvia relationship operates through the 3+3 framework nation format (UK leads the Estonian battlegroup, providing indirect regional security). The UK joined the CAVS Framework in September 2025 and contributes CVR(T) vehicles to Ukraine via Latvia. The UK-Norway December 2025 frigate deal (£10 billion) enhances Baltic Sea naval security.

<strong>Latvia-Finland: defense industry integration</strong>

Finland's **Patria** opened a 6×6 armored vehicle production facility in **Valmiera** in 2024 — significant Finnish FDI creating an operational defense-industrial link. Finnish FM **Elina Valtonen** visited Riga on 28 January 2025, meeting FM Braže and addressing the diplomatic corps on Nordic-Baltic security. Both are founding ESSI members and CAVS Framework participants. The NB8 format and joint PURL contribution (\~\$500 million NB8-wide) provide multilateral reinforcement.

<strong>Latvia-Ukraine: patron relationship, not peer partnership</strong>

Latvia's relationship with Ukraine has moved beyond traditional bilateral ties into a patron-client dynamic in the security domain — with Latvia as patron despite its far smaller size. Latvia's 0.25–0.30% GDP annual military commitment, co-leadership of the Drone Coalition, training of **4,000+ Ukrainian soldiers** since 2022 (with 4,000 planned for 2025), and participation in training the **NB8 Nordic-Baltic Brigade** (3,000–5,000 Ukrainian personnel) give Latvia influence disproportionate to its population. The €15 million reconstruction commitment focused on Chernihiv region, Jaunsardze's August 2025 camp for 52 children of killed or wounded Ukrainian soldiers, and authorization to strike inside Russian territory with Latvian-provided weapons all signal deep political solidarity.

## XXII. Cross-cutting strategic frameworks

Latvia participates in an unusually dense web of multilateral frameworks for a country of 1.8 million: **NATO** (since 2004), the **EU** (since 2004, eurozone since 2014), **NB8** (increasingly the primary coordination platform), **JEF** (UK-led, 10 nations), **ESSI** (European Sky Shield), **CAVS** (armored vehicle procurement), **Bucharest Nine**, **Three Seas Initiative**, **Baltic Council of Ministers/Baltic Assembly**, and now the **UN Security Council** (2026–2027). This institutional density provides redundancy — if any single framework weakens, multiple alternatives exist to maintain security cooperation, information sharing, and political coordination. The NB8's emergence as a de facto defense coordination mechanism (moving beyond its origins as a political talk-shop) is perhaps the most significant structural development since 2022.

## XXIII. Conclusion: a small state at the edge of strategic transformation

Latvia in 2026 occupies a paradoxical position. Its external security architecture has never been stronger — NATO brigade-level presence, approaching 5% GDP defense spending, UNSC membership, deep Nordic-Baltic integration, and a Canadian framework partner studying permanent basing. But its internal politics face compounding pressures: coalition fragility, the return of oligarch-linked populism via Mr. Šlesers's Latvia First, progressive-conservative polarization over the Istanbul Convention, Russian-speaking community disengagement, and an economy growing too slowly to cushion the fiscal strain of defense investment.

Three dynamics deserve close monitoring through the October 2026 election cycle. First, **the cordon sanitaire's durability**: whether mainstream parties will partner with Latvia First despite Mr. Šlesers's history, as arithmetic may demand. Second, **defense spending sustainability**: 4.91% GDP is extraordinary for a country with 59/100 corruption perceptions and a projected 3.3% budget deficit — the social contract supporting this burden depends on perceived threat credibility. Third, **the Russian-speaking community's trajectory**: with 180,000 non-citizens, 841 facing deportation, Russian eliminated from education and media, and documented political apathy, the integration challenge has shifted from active resistance to passive alienation — a different but potentially more durable problem.

Latvia's strategic culture — forged in occupation, deportation, and contested memory — produces a clarity of threat perception and alliance commitment that larger, less historically traumatized states struggle to match. The question for the next decade is whether that clarity can be maintained against the entropy of populism, fiscal strain, and alliance uncertainty.
