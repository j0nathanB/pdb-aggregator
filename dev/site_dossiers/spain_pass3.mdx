## Section 13: HISTORY OF DISSENT & CIVIL SOCIETY INFRASTRUCTURE

Spain's civil society operates through robust institutional channels characteristic of a consolidated European democracy. CIVICUS rates Spain's civic space as "open."[^1] The analytical frame here is not revolutionary incubation but the capacity, reach, and independence of organized interests — and the specific gaps where that capacity thins under stress. Three features distinguish Spain's civil society landscape: labor unions with legal leverage far exceeding their membership; a Catholic institutional infrastructure whose organizational weight increasingly diverges from its popular legitimacy; and a post-2011 digital civil society whose mobilization capacity has shifted from anti-austerity to housing and anti-tourism.

The labor movement remains the most structurally significant civil society sector. **Comisiones Obreras (CCOO)**, led by General Secretary Unai Sordo, reported 1,087,714 members as of January 2025; **Unión General de Trabajadores (UGT)**, led by Pepe Álvarez, brings combined CCOO-UGT membership to approximately 2.07 million by 2024 — up 12.5% from 2015.[^2] Union density stands at roughly 16.2%, low by European standards. However, Spain's "audience model" — where legal representativeness derives from works council elections rather than membership rolls — combined with the *eficacia general* system extending collectively bargained conditions to all workers in a sector regardless of union affiliation, gives unions structural power disproportionate to their headcount. Collective bargaining coverage reaches approximately 92% of employees, among the EU's highest.[^3] The 2021 labor reform — which slashed temporary employment from 23.9% to 14.8% in its first year — was negotiated through tripartite social dialogue (government, CCOO/UGT, employers' CEOE/CEPYME), and union leaders described it as "the greatest advance in workers' rights in the history of democratic Spain."[^4] The minimum wage has risen 61% since 2018, from €735.90 to €1,221/month in 2026. However, the 12.5% membership growth since 2015 represents only half the 24% growth in total employees, meaning density has actually declined in relative terms. Union reach remains weakest among the growing precariat: temporary workers, gig economy participants, and undocumented migrants fall largely outside organized labor's orbit.

The Basque Country operates a **separate union ecosystem** that does not participate in Spanish-state social dialogue. **ELA** (~105,000 members) is the dominant Basque union, aligned with Basque nationalist politics; **LAB** (~50,000 members) aligns with EH Bildu's political orbit. Together they organized six general strikes between 2009 and 2013 — a confrontational posture with no equivalent in the rest of Spain.[^5]

On the employer side, the **CEOE** (Confederación Española de Organizaciones Empresariales) under President Antonio Garamendi functions as a veto player on economic legislation. The 37.5-hour work week proposal — which would have affected 12.5 million private-sector workers at an estimated cost of €11 billion — exemplifies the dynamic: CEOE unanimously rejected it, Garamendi declared the government was engaged in "monologue, not dialogue," and Congress voted it down in September 2025 after PP, Vox, and Junts fully amended the bill.[^6] CEOE's internal politics bear watching: the General Assembly abolished term limits in 2023, but CEPYME's new president Ángela de Miguel (elected May 2025 over incumbent Gerardo Cuerva) reportedly supports an alternative CEOE candidacy, potentially fragmenting employer-side coordination.[^7]

The **Catholic Church** retains enormous institutional infrastructure despite precipitous secularization. Approximately 5,800 publicly funded Catholic schools (*colegios concertados*) — representing 76% of all concertado schools — educate roughly 26% of Spanish children.[^8] **Cáritas Española**, the Church's social services arm, is Spain's largest non-governmental welfare provider: in 2024, it accompanied over 2.1 million people (1.18 million in Spain, 1.0 million internationally), invested €486.9 million, and maintained a network of 70 diocesan offices, 5,400+ parish groups, and 71,000 volunteers.[^9] During the October 2024 DANA floods, Cáritas deployed €10 million in emergency aid within six months, benefiting 16,000+ people. The Church's institutional role in education and welfare gives it structural salience that its declining spiritual authority alone would not justify. Catholic self-identification has fallen from ~90% in the 1970s to 55–56% in 2025 (CIS data); only 18.8% identify as practicing Catholics. Among 18–29-year-olds, Catholic identification dropped from 60% (2002) to 32% (2024). Non-believers, atheists, and agnostics combined now constitute approximately 39–40% of the population.[^10] The vocational crisis is acute: average priest age is approximately 65, with only ~168 new ordinations annually, and over 6,000 parishes lack a full-time priest. Catholic marriages collapsed from near-universality to 18% in 2023. Under the 2020 education law (LOMLOE), religion class no longer counts toward GPA — effectively making it extracurricular and accelerating institutional decline in education.

The **15-M/Indignados movement** (May 15, 2011) was Spain's most significant civil society eruption since the Transition. Its lasting organizational legacies include **Podemos** (founded January 2014, now succeeded by Sumar as the junior coalition partner), **Barcelona en Comú** (founded June 2014 by PAH co-founder Ada Colau, who served as Barcelona mayor 2015–2023), and the broader tenant union movement that inherited the PAH's anti-eviction infrastructure.[^11] The **PAH** (Plataforma de Afectados por la Hipoteca) achieved 90% public approval through nonviolent eviction-blocking and successfully stopped over 1,000 evictions during the mortgage crisis. Its energy has now migrated from anti-eviction to anti-rent-inflation activism.

**Housing has replaced employment as the primary mobilization issue.** On April 5, 2025, hundreds of thousands marched in over 40 cities — an estimated 150,000+ in Madrid and 100,000+ in Barcelona — under the banner "Let's End the Housing Business," representing the largest street mobilizations since 15-M itself.[^12] The **Sindicato de Inquilinas e Inquilinos** (Madrid Tenants' Union, founded 2017) and **Sindicat de Llogateres** (Catalan Tenants' Union, ~4,000 members) are the organizational anchors. Rents have doubled over the past decade; home prices rose 47% between 2015 and 2023 with another 8% in 2024; 40% of renters spend over 40% of income on housing; and public/social housing constitutes under 2% of total stock versus an OECD average of 7%.[^13] Anti-tourism protests erupted across Spain's most visited destinations in 2024–2025, with a Southern European alliance (**SET** — Sud d'Europa contra la Turistificació) staging simultaneous protests across 16 cities in June 2025.[^14]

The **Ley Mordaza** (Organic Law 4/2015 on Citizen Security) remains the single most significant state-imposed constraint on civic space. Passed by PP with an absolute majority, it has generated approximately 300,000 sanctions in its first decade, including fines up to €30,000 for unauthorized use of police images and penalties for "lack of respect" to officers or unapproved protests.[^15] Despite repeated PSOE promises of repeal, reform has stalled: a 2023 bill failed when ERC and Bildu rejected it as insufficient, and the law remains substantially intact as of early 2026. International bodies including the UN, Amnesty International, and ARTICLE 19 have flagged it as a constraint on freedom of assembly and expression.

Spain's think tank ecosystem is modest by Anglo-Saxon standards (~53 institutions total), with relatively low policy influence. **Real Instituto Elcano** (directed by Charles Powell, top-50 globally per Penn TTCSP) is the establishment foreign policy voice with center-right orientation, funded by IBEX 35 companies and the state. **CIDOB** (Barcelona, ranked #37 globally) is Spain's top-ranked think tank, focused on Mediterranean and global affairs. **Fundación Alternativas** is linked to PSOE figures (Felipe González, Javier Solana). **FAES**, chaired by former PM José María Aznar, serves as PP's ideological laboratory.[^16]

Spain has **not enacted foreign agent laws** or restrictions on international NGO funding. Major international organizations — Greenpeace Spain, Oxfam Intermón, Amnesty International Spain, MSF (with significant Barcelona operations) — operate freely.[^1] This permissive legal environment contrasts with the trend toward foreign-influence restrictions in other European states.

In the regional civil society space, Catalan organizations have shifted from confrontational to institutional mode. **Òmnium Cultural** claims 190,000+ members organized in 52 regional offices; **Assemblea Nacional Catalana (ANC)** claims approximately 100,000 members. Both have experienced declining mobilization capacity post-2017, with annual Diada demonstrations shrinking significantly since the *procés* peak.[^17] The election of PSC's Salvador Illa as Catalan president (August 2024) and the amnesty law have channeled independence activism into parliamentary rather than street politics. In the Basque Country, the broader *abertzale* movement has pivoted to socioeconomic issues — housing, pensions, labor rights, Basque language promotion — supported by extensive *ikastolas*, cultural associations, and Basque-language media.

[HIGH CONFIDENCE]

## Section 14: PATRON-CLIENT HISTORY & ALLIANCE GENEALOGY

Spain's alignment posture is best characterized as **firm alliance commitment with selective defection capacity**. It is embedded in NATO and the EU as core institutional frameworks, but unlike most Western European allies, it has demonstrated a willingness to deny the United States operational access to jointly operated military facilities — a capacity that derives not from multi-vector hedging but from the depth of anti-war sentiment in Spanish political culture, the legal architecture of the basing agreements, and the EU's collective trade shield that limits American economic retaliation. The March 2026 base-access denial is the defining episode, but it is the culmination of structural dynamics traceable to the 1953 Pact of Madrid.

Spain's relationship with American power began as a Cold War marriage of convenience with an authoritarian regime. The **1953 Pact of Madrid** — three "executive agreements" (not treaties, to avoid US Senate ratification, which was politically impossible given congressional opposition to Franco) — permitted US construction of air and naval bases at Rota, Morón, Torrejón, and Zaragoza in exchange for approximately $1.4 billion in military and economic aid over ten years.[^18] Spain became, in the academic formulation, a "strategic satellite rather than a formal ally," ending post-1945 isolation and enabling UN admission in December 1955. The arrangement embedded anti-Americanism into Spanish progressive politics from the outset: the US was associated with propping up dictatorship.

**NATO accession** (May 30, 1982) was partly motivated by strengthening civilian control after 23-F. The 1986 referendum — approved by 56.9% on 59.4% turnout — carried three binding conditions: no integration into NATO's military command structure, prohibition on nuclear weapons on Spanish soil, and gradual reduction of US military presence.[^19] PM Felipe González had campaigned "No to NATO" in opposition before reversing course in government — a betrayal narrative that still resonates on the Spanish left. Spain remained outside NATO's integrated military command until 1996, when parliament authorized negotiation under PM Aznar, and Javier Solana was simultaneously serving as NATO Secretary General.

The **1988 Agreement on Defense Cooperation (ADC)** replaced the Franco-era framework. Amended by three protocols (most recently in 2023 to expand Rota from four to six Aegis destroyers), it establishes Spanish sovereignty over bases; US commanders exercise command and control over US equipment and premises; permanently deployed US aircraft enjoy freedom of movement, but all others require prior authorization.[^20] This legal architecture — specifically the sovereignty and consultation provisions — is what Spain invoked in March 2026.

The **March 2026 base-access crisis** represents the most consequential test of the US-Spain relationship in decades and the most significant defiance of American military operations by a NATO ally since France's withdrawal from integrated military command in 1966. When the US and Israel launched "Operation Epic Fury" against Iran on February 28, 2026, Sánchez refused access to Rota and Morón for offensive operations. On March 2, FM Albares formalized the denial, stating operations must comply with the bilateral agreement and UN Charter; Defense Minister Robles said Madrid had "absolutely" not been consulted.[^21] The US relocated at least 15 aircraft (including KC-135 tankers) to Ramstein. Trump, meeting Chancellor Merz on March 3, threatened to "cut off all trade with Spain" and stated: "We could use their base if we want. We could just fly in and use it. Nobody's going to tell us not to use it."[^22] On March 4, Sánchez delivered a televised address — "No a la guerra" — consciously echoing the 2003 Iraq War slogan. The White House claimed on March 5 that Spain had "agreed to cooperate"; Spain "categorically" denied this. Polling showed 53.2% of Spaniards backed Sánchez's decision.

Spain's economic exposure to US retaliation proved manageable: US exports to Spain totaled $26.1 billion versus imports of $21.3 billion (a US surplus of $4.8 billion), and only 4–5% of Spanish exports go to the US. EU collective trade policy makes a unilateral embargo effectively impossible. The IBEX 35 actually rose during the crisis.[^23] Most remarkably, Iran reportedly granted Spanish-flagged ships free transit through the Strait of Hormuz — alongside China, Russia, India, Iraq, and Pakistan — as a reward for Spain's opposition to the war.[^24] This created a tangible economic benefit from defiance, a dynamic without precedent in NATO's history.

However, Spain did not completely disengage from allied operations. A Spanish Patriot battery deployed to Turkey under NATO Active Fence provided data that reportedly enabled US forces to intercept an Iranian ballistic missile. Spain also deployed a frigate to Cyprus for defensive NATO support. The pattern was selective defection from offensive operations while maintaining defensive alliance commitments — a distinction the Sánchez government drew explicitly.

**Spain's relationship with France** is the most structurally consequential bilateral dynamic after the EU itself. France is Spain's sole land gateway to continental Europe, giving Paris outsized influence over energy and trade connectivity. The shelving of the **MidCat gas pipeline** in 2019 — with France citing environmental and profitability concerns but widely seen as protecting French nuclear energy priorities against Iberian renewable competition — exemplified this leverage. The replacement **H2Med/BarMar** undersea pipeline (Barcelona to Marseille, operational target 2030) and the **Bay of Biscay electricity interconnection** (EIB-backed at €1.6 billion, nearly doubling capacity to 5 GW, operational target 2028) represent the most important infrastructure projects for Spain's strategic reorientation from energy island to European energy corridor.[^25] The **FCAS** sixth-generation fighter program (estimated €100+ billion, split France 42%, Germany 34%, Spain 24%) is existential for Spain's defense modernization. FCAS collapse would leave Spain without a next-generation combat aircraft path — Spain has ruled out the F-35 and reportedly began exploring Turkey's Kaan fighter as a hedge. France and Spain reaffirmed FCAS commitment in December 2025, but Germany remained conspicuously silent, with mediation between Dassault and Airbus scheduled for mid-April 2026.[^26]

**Spain-Morocco** relations operate on a structural axis of migration leverage and territorial dispute. Morocco's weaponization of migration — allowing 8,000–12,000 migrants into Ceuta in May 2021 in retaliation for Spain hospitalizing Polisario leader Brahim Ghali — forced Sánchez's March 2022 reversal on Western Sahara, endorsing Morocco's autonomy plan as "the most serious, realistic and credible" initiative.[^27] This triggered Algeria's fury (ambassador recalled, Maghreb-Europe pipeline shut), but secured Moroccan migration cooperation: irregular crossings into Ceuta and Melilla dropped 40% in 2023. The XIII High-Level Meeting (December 4, 2025) produced 14 cooperation memoranda and a 119-point communiqué described as the "most ambitious" since the 2022 reset, including fully operational commercial customs posts at Ceuta and Melilla.[^28] Morocco has cooled its sovereignty claims over the enclaves but not abandoned them.

The **Gibraltar** post-Brexit treaty, published February 26, 2026 after four years of negotiations, represents a historic breakthrough: removal of all border checks, demolition of the 1.2 km border fence, customs union between Gibraltar and the EU, and application of Schengen rules — with Spain gaining a new role in border and residency decisions while UK sovereignty and the RAF base are explicitly protected. Provisional application is targeted for April 10, 2026.[^29]

Spain's **Latin American ties** constitute a distinctive asset among European middle powers. The Ibero-American Summit system (22 nations, 30th Summit hosted by Spain in 2026) and SEGIB provide institutional architecture that no other European state possesses. IBEX 35 multinationals — Santander (operations across Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico, Peru, Uruguay, covering 80% of the region's GDP), BBVA (dominant in Mexico), Telefónica, Iberdrola, Repsol — maintain an economic footprint that constitutes a modern echo of the historical empire.[^30] However, the Milei-Sánchez dispute of May 2024 (Argentina's president insulted Sánchez's wife, prompting ambassador withdrawal) illustrates the fragility of soft-power claims when political personalities clash.

Spain's posture toward **China and Russia** has created growing tension with alliance expectations. Russian LNG, while declining from 21.3% of imports in 2024 to 11.4% in 2025, remains structurally embedded through Naturgy's long-term Yamal LNG contract (2.5 mtpa, running until 2038).[^31] Meanwhile, US LNG surged to 30% of Spain's gas supply in 2025, creating a new and potentially weaponizable dependency.[^32] On Chinese technology, Spain is notably permissive: Telefónica renewed 5G core contracts with Huawei; MasOrange (Spain's largest mobile operator) pivoted to Huawei for 5G Advanced; and Spain's Interior Ministry awarded Huawei €12.3 million for judicial wiretap server infrastructure — a security-sensitive contract that drew sharp criticism from CEPA, which described Spain as "one of the EU's most China-friendly governments."[^33]

Disaggregating Spain's relationship to American power across the publication's analytical dimensions:

**Security dependency:** Moderate and declining. Rota and Morón are more valuable to the US (missile defense, Africa power projection) than to Spain. Spain lacks independent power projection but is protected by NATO's collective defense guarantee and the EU's emerging defense architecture. The current US posture is **hostility** — Trump's sovereignty threats and trade retaliation rhetoric following the March 2026 denial.

**Economic dependency:** Low. US trade constitutes 4–5% of Spanish exports; EU collective trade policy shields against unilateral retaliation. However, the surge of US LNG to 30% of gas supply creates a new vulnerability. Current US posture: **hostility** (explicit trade threats).

**Institutional dependency:** Low-to-moderate. Spain's primary institutional framework is the EU, not US-backed institutions. Teresa Ribera's appointment as EU Competition Commissioner gives Spain senior Commission representation. Current US posture: **indifference** to **hostility** (Trump's NATO expulsion suggestion in October 2025).

**Diplomatic dependency:** Low. Spain's Palestinian statehood recognition, ICJ genocide case intervention, and Iran war opposition position it increasingly outside the US-aligned camp. Current US posture: **hostility**.

Spain's void-filling strategy centers on **EU deepening** rather than bilateral substitution. It supports extending qualified majority voting to EU foreign and security policy, joined the Atlantic Group conditioning enlargement on institutional reform, and achieved its highest-ever defense spending (2% GDP, €33.1 billion) under EU and NATO pressure rather than US bilateral coercion.[^34] The March 2026 crisis has, paradoxically, strengthened Spain's intra-European position: the European Council expressed "full solidarity," and Spain's demonstrated willingness to say "no" to Washington enhanced its credibility as a sovereign European actor.

1. Spain's basing agreements (1988 ADC) provide a legal mechanism for denying US operational access that has now been activated — this precedent will constrain future US military planning in the Western Mediterranean and incentivize diversification to less politically constrained facilities. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]
2. Spain's economic insulation from US retaliation (4–5% export exposure, EU collective trade shield) makes it uniquely positioned among NATO allies to absorb the costs of defiance — a structural feature that will persist regardless of government composition. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]
3. Iran's Hormuz transit privileges for Spanish shipping create an unprecedented dynamic where a NATO member receives tangible economic benefits from opposing US military operations — a pattern that could incentivize further selective defection if replicated. [ASSESSED]
4. Spain's defense modernization depends existentially on FCAS: program collapse would leave Spain without a sixth-generation fighter path, having ruled out the F-35, and would represent the most significant defense-industrial crisis since NATO accession. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

## Section 15: CONSTITUTIONAL CRISES & REGIME TRANSITION PATTERNS

Spain has experienced seven constitutions since 1812, exhibiting a pronounced pattern of liberal-authoritarian oscillation — the "Two Spains" cycle — that was broken only by the 1978 settlement.[^35] The current constitutional order is now 47 years old, the longest period of continuous democratic governance in Spanish history. It has been amended only three times (1992 for Maastricht, 2011 for fiscal stability, and 2024 to update disability terminology), making it one of Europe's most rigid constitutions.[^36] This rigidity, once a source of stability, has become a structural constraint: the accumulated stresses of territorial conflict, judicial politicization, and legislative paralysis are being managed through political improvisation rather than institutional adaptation.

The historical cycle is analytically important because it reveals the recurring structural tension that has driven regime change. Spain's constitutions of 1812, 1837, 1845, 1869, 1876, and 1931 each represented an attempt to resolve the central cleavage between liberal-progressive and conservative-authoritarian visions of the state — what Machado captured as the "Two Spains." The pattern: a liberal constitution is established, contested by traditionalist forces, eventually overthrown or hollowed out, replaced by a conservative restoration, which in turn generates progressive backlash. The Primo de Rivera dictatorship (1923), the Second Republic (1931), and the Civil War (1936–39) represent the final, violent iteration of this cycle. Franco's 36-year dictatorship suppressed the cycle by force; the 1978 Constitution attempted to end it by consensus.[^37]

The **1978 Constitution's amendment procedure** is the key structural feature constraining institutional adaptation. Article 167 (ordinary revision) requires a three-fifths majority in each chamber — achievable only with PSOE-PP agreement, which current polarization precludes. Article 168 (aggravated revision) applies to total revision or changes to the Preliminary Title, fundamental rights, or the Crown, and requires: two-thirds approval in each chamber, immediate dissolution of the Cortes, ratification by newly elected chambers also at two-thirds, and a mandatory referendum. Article 168 has **never been invoked** — it is so onerous that it effectively makes core constitutional provisions unamendable.[^38] The territorial model (Title VIII), which generates the most structural friction, falls under the ordinary procedure, but even this requires bipartisan cooperation that has not existed since the 2011 fiscal amendment was rushed through under eurozone crisis pressure.

The **CGPJ blockade** (December 2018 to July 2024) demonstrated how Spain's institutional design can be weaponized through non-cooperation. The CGPJ's 20 members are appointed by a three-fifths parliamentary majority, requiring cross-party agreement. PP refused renewal for 5.5 years, preserving a conservative majority and leaving 20 Supreme Court vacancies (25% of positions) unfilled. The UN Special Rapporteur on judicial independence expressed concern in January 2024. Resolution came only through European Commission mediation: on June 26, 2024, PSOE and PP signed a renewal deal in Brussels, with each party nominating 10 members.[^39] The fundamental tension — parliamentary election of judicial governors creating de facto politicization — was not resolved but merely reset. The CGPJ will face renewal again in approximately 2029, and the same weaponization potential will exist.

The **Constitutional Tribunal** has become a direct extension of partisan competition. Its 6–4 progressive majority, resulting from contested 2022–23 renewals, produced the June 26, 2025 ruling upholding the Amnesty Law's constitutionality.[^40] But the **Supreme Court** — where the conservative majority persisted due to the CGPJ blockade's cascading appointment delays — refused to apply the amnesty to Puigdemont, ruling his embezzlement charges fall outside the law's scope. This inter-court tension — the Constitutional Court declaring a law valid while the Supreme Court declines to apply it to its most prominent intended beneficiary — is unprecedented and represents a structural fracture in the judicial system. Puigdemont remains in exile in southern France (Vallespir) with an active arrest warrant; in November 2025, he appealed to the Constitutional Court to suspend the warrant, arguing "irreparable damage" to his fundamental rights.[^41]

The **Attorney General's conviction** (November 2025) added another unprecedented institutional rupture. Álvaro García Ortiz was convicted by the Supreme Court (5–2) for leaking confidential information about Alberto González Amador (partner of Madrid regional president Isabel Díaz Ayuso) who was under investigation for tax fraud. García Ortiz received a two-year suspension and fines, and resigned on November 24, 2025 — the first Spanish Attorney General to be criminally convicted.[^42]

Spain's current political dynamics exhibit multiple simultaneous stress indicators without crossing into democratic breakdown. Freedom House scores Spain at **90/100** ("Free") for 2025; the Economist Intelligence Unit's Democracy Index rates it **8.13** (rank 21, "Full Democracy") but with a notably weak "functioning of government" sub-score of 7.50.[^43] The system is experiencing: three consecutive years without a budget (2024–2026, operating on extended 2023 allocations); loss of parliamentary majority after Junts's October 2025 withdrawal; the Koldo corruption scandal reaching PSOE's senior ranks (Santos Cerdán detained June 2025, Ábalos facing Supreme Court proceedings); separate investigations into PM Sánchez's wife and brother; and the Attorney General's unprecedented conviction.[^44]

**Decision-making architecture and leadership environment:** Sánchez's governance is characterized by high centralization within the Presidencia del Gobierno (Moncloa). After Iván Redondo's departure as chief of staff in July 2021 — Redondo had accumulated unprecedented authority, running what critics called a "shadow government" with 17 advisory bodies and 75 advisors — the structure was rationalized under Minister of Presidency **Félix Bolaños**, who became the key institutional negotiator (handling the CGPJ renewal in Brussels and amnesty law negotiations with Junts), and chief of staff **Óscar López**.[^45] The Koldo case exemplifies the risks of this centralized model: key gatekeepers (Ábalos, Cerdán) controlled access and information flows, potentially insulating Sánchez from knowledge of corruption within his own operation. Under crisis conditions (COVID, the March 2026 Iran confrontation), the system centralizes further, with Sánchez personally leading national addresses and maintaining tight message control. **[ASSESSED — based on COVID crisis response pattern (2020) and March 2026 Iran crisis communication; two episodes support the centralization-under-stress inference, but the inner circle's information dynamics remain opaque]**

**Succession dynamics:** Constitutionally, the next general election must occur no later than August 2027. Sánchez announced in September 2025 his intention to seek re-election.[^46] However, legislative paralysis (no budget, no major legislation passing) has fueled speculation about early elections in 2026, though Sánchez has historically preferred endurance over dissolution. The constructive no-confidence mechanism protects him: PP cannot assemble 176 votes for Feijóo without partners who will not cooperate.

All polls show PP as frontrunner (~30.3%) with PSOE at ~28.4%. **Vox** has surged to approximately 17.9% nationally, doubling its seats in snap regional elections in Extremadura (December 2025) and Aragón (February 2026), and gaining significant working-class and youth support (28% among 25–34-year-olds).[^47] PP + Vox together poll at 48–50%, projected at 193–200 seats (176 needed for majority). On the left, Sumar's Yolanda Díaz announced in February 2026 she will not run in 2027 and will leave politics at the end of the legislature. Sumar polls at ~6.1%, Podemos at ~4%. The radical left space remains deeply fragmented and electorally weak.[^48]

The most probable succession scenario is a **PP-led government**, potentially requiring Vox support or tolerance. This would represent the most significant alignment shift since the Transition: Vox's published "remigration" program (June 2025) calling for mass deportations, its Patriots for Europe alignment (with Le Pen and Orbán), and its anti-NATO-spending-increase positioning create tensions with both EU institutional commitments and NATO alliance obligations that a PP-Vox government would need to manage.[^49] **[MODERATE CONFIDENCE — polling trends are consistent but Spanish electoral outcomes have repeatedly confounded projections, and PSOE has demonstrated a capacity to recover from apparently terminal positions]**

Democratic erosion in Spain does not proceed through the classic authoritarian playbook of executive aggrandizement. Instead, it operates through **reciprocal institutional degradation**: each party weaponizes the institutions it controls against the other. PP blocked CGPJ renewal to preserve judicial power; PSOE reformed the Constitutional Tribunal's appointment rules and RTVE governance to expand its influence. The Royal Decree-Law of October 2024 expanding the RTVE board from 10 to 15 members and lowering the appointment threshold from two-thirds to absolute majority was condemned by the International Press Institute as enabling partisan control.[^50] The State Media Monitor 2025 found that 8 of 15 regional public broadcasters are now classified as "State Controlled," up from just 1 in 2022 — with most takeovers by PP-led regional administrations. This pattern of reciprocal degradation means that regardless of which party governs, institutional independence erodes incrementally from both directions.

## Section 16: COLLECTIVE MEMORY OF HUMILIATION & GRIEVANCE

Spain's collective memory landscape is structured by three layered traumas — the Civil War and Francoist repression, the 1898 imperial collapse, and the Catalan crisis of 2017 — plus one persistent grievance narrative (Gibraltar) and one reactivated mythological frame (the Reconquista). These narratives are actively contested along partisan lines, and the contest between them functions as a proxy for competing visions of Spanish national identity. The pipeline must understand which narrative is being activated by which actors in response to incoming events, because the same external development will be interpreted in diametrically opposed ways depending on which memory frame dominates.

The **Civil War (1936–1939) and Francoist repression** remain the deepest unprocessed trauma. An estimated 114,000–140,000 civilians were "disappeared" in Francoist killings, and Spain contains an estimated **2,382–3,500 mass graves**, of which approximately 800–850 have been exhumed over two decades — meaning 1,500–2,700 remain unopened. Of the 11,000–17,000 remains recovered, only about 700 have been positively identified via DNA.[^51] The 2022 **Democratic Memory Law** (Ley de Memoria Democrática) made the state assume responsibility for exhumations, created an official Register of Victims, abolished 33 Franco-era noble titles, and mandated teaching about the dictatorship in schools. By November 2025, the government reported enabling recovery of remains of 9,000 people and delivery of 1,614 declarations of recognition to victims. The law's citizenship provisions ("Grandchildren's Law") generated 876,321 applications by July 2025 (40% from Argentina) before key provisions expired on October 22, 2025.[^52] The **Valle de los Caídos** — renamed **Valle de Cuelgamuros** — was rededicated after Franco's exhumation (October 24, 2019) and José Antonio Primo de Rivera's reburial (2023). In November 2025, the government presented the winning redesign project ("The Base and the Cross"): construction bidding is expected in late 2026, with completion projected for 2030 at a cost of €31 million. The basilica still contains approximately 33,800 remains, many Republican victims interred without family consent.[^53]

The partisan contest over memory has escalated to the regional level. PP-Vox coalition governments in six or more autonomous communities (Aragón, Castilla y León, Valencia, Baleares, Cantabria, Extremadura) have replaced regional memory laws with **"Harmony Laws" (Leyes de Concordia)** that equate the dictatorship with democratic periods like the Second Republic. On April 30, 2024, three UN experts condemned these laws as potentially contravening international human rights standards.[^54] PP frames memory legislation as "revenge" and argues "history belongs to historians"; Vox calls for outright repeal of all memory laws. Education implementation has lagged: the Democratic Memory Law mandated that studies of Francoism be part of compulsory secondary education, but education is a regional competency and PP-governed regions have resisted implementation.

The constraint strength of Civil War memory is **genuinely binding on military policy but asymmetric across parties**. For the left and center-left (representing approximately 45–50% of the electorate), the Civil War narrative — fused with the Iraq War experience (see below) — creates deep resistance to military adventurism that any PSOE-led government must respect. For the right, the Civil War memory operates differently: PP's position is that the Transition's consensus to "move forward" should be respected, making memory laws themselves the provocation. Vox has gone further, explicitly rehabilitating Francoist-adjacent narratives — Abascal's 2019 campaign launch at Covadonga, the legendary site of the first Christian victory in 722, declaring "the Reconquista has started in the south," fuses medieval myth with contemporary anti-immigration mobilization.[^53] The test of constraint strength: when Aznar aligned Spain with Bush on Iraq in 2003, defying 91% public opposition (the highest in any country polled), the domestic political cost was regime-ending — his party lost the election three days after the March 11, 2004 Madrid bombings. This precedent means no Spanish government will lightly commit to military operations that echo the Iraq template. Sánchez invoked 2003 explicitly during the March 2026 Iran crisis: "In 2003, a few irresponsible leaders dragged us into an illegal war."[^54]

**Anti-American sentiment** has deep structural roots — not merely episodic reactions to specific policies. The 1953 bases agreement associated the US with sustaining dictatorship. The 1898 Disaster — Spain's loss of Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Philippines, and Guam to the United States — shattered world-power pretensions and generated the Generation of '98's introspective crisis. Iraq was the crystallizing event: 91% opposition, the Azores Summit, the March 11 bombings, and the electoral punishment of the PP. Pew Research's June 2025 Global Attitudes survey provides the most recent quantification: Spain's favorable view of the US dropped **17 percentage points** between 2024 and 2025, to just **31% favorable versus 66% unfavorable**. Confidence in Trump stands at only **19%** (80% have little or no confidence). Critically, Spain was the only country surveyed where views of the US became more negative while views of China simultaneously became more positive. Spaniards now name the US as their top threat about as often as they name Russia.[^55] The March 2026 Iran crisis has reactivated the Iraq template with striking precision — the same slogan ("No a la guerra"), the same political dynamic (conservative party aligned with US, progressive party in defiance), and the same overwhelming public support for defiance (53.2% backed Sánchez).

**The 1898 Disaster** continues to shape Spanish self-perception through a paradoxical mechanism: it drives Spain's enthusiastic Europeanism. The Generation of '98 (Unamuno, Machado, Baroja, Azorín) diagnosed Spain's ills as isolation and backwardness, prescribing "Europeanization" as the cure. EU accession in 1986 fulfilled this prescription; Spain's identity as a "good European" is the positive institutional expression of the 1898 wound. This means that EU institutional weakening or exclusion would be experienced not merely as a policy setback but as a regression to the pre-1986 condition of isolation — triggering deep national anxiety. The constraint is binding: no mainstream Spanish political actor (even Vox, uniquely among European far-right parties) advocates EU withdrawal.[^56]

**Catalan grievance** centers on the **1714 siege of Barcelona** and the subsequent Nueva Planta Decrees that abolished Catalan institutions. September 11 — the **Diada** — commemorates the fall of Barcelona to Bourbon forces and functions as Catalonia's national day. This historical memory was deliberately constructed and maintained through the *Renaixença* cultural movement (19th century), the Catalan government's educational system, and civic organizations like Òmnium Cultural. The 2017 crisis — referendum, independence declaration, Article 155, imprisonment — added a fresh layer of grievance. However, the post-*procés* trajectory suggests the constraint is weakening: independence support has fallen to **38%** (the lowest ever recorded) with opposition at 54% (the highest ever recorded) per the CEO survey of February–March 2025.[^57] The election of PSC's Illa as Catalan president represents a significant shift from confrontation to accommodation. The grievance persists as a mobilizable reserve (~40% of the Catalan electorate) but its constraint on national policy has diminished — evidenced by the fact that Sánchez successfully absorbed the political costs of the Amnesty Law without Catalan independence sentiment surging.

**Basque memory** centers on ETA's violence — over 850 killed, 2,600 wounded over nearly 60 years — and its aftermath. Covite documents at least **358 unsolved ETA murders**; only 16 cold cases have been solved, none with ETA member cooperation. No truth and reconciliation commission has been established, partly because it would require investigating **state crimes**: the GAL (Grupos Antiterroristas de Liberación) death squads killed 27–28 people under Felipe González's government (1983–1987), and the prisoner dispersal policy remains contentious.[^58] Victims' associations — particularly the **AVT** (Asociación de Víctimas del Terrorismo, ~4,400 members, aligned with PP) and **Covite** — maintain strong political influence and have fiercely opposed EH Bildu's normalization as a parliamentary partner. In 2023, 44 convicted former ETA members appeared on EH Bildu municipal election lists, provoking outrage from victims' groups. Arnaldo Otegi has said ETA's violence "should never have happened" and expressed "lament" for victim suffering, but EH Bildu has never cooperated with justice in solving pending cases — Covite considers these gestures insufficient without full accountability.[^59] PP and Vox routinely describe Sánchez's parliamentary reliance on Bildu as governing "with the heirs of terrorists." The constraint binds differentially: for PP voters, association with ETA's political heirs is a bright line; for PSOE, the calculation is that Bildu's democratic participation represents the Basque peace process's success.

**The Reconquista narrative** has been reactivated as a mobilization frame by the far right, with real-world consequences. Vox's invocation of medieval Christian reconquest — Abascal at Covadonga in 2019, his February 2025 Madrid rally with Viktor Orbán vowing to "reconquer Europe," and the June 2025 "remigration" program — is not merely rhetorical. The **Torre-Pacheco riots** (July 2025, Murcia) demonstrated the narrative's capacity to generate violence: after the beating of a pensioner (three North Africans arrested), a fabricated video circulated on social media, sparking three nights of clashes between far-right groups and North African migrants. Approximately 300 people were investigated. The Interior Minister blamed Vox and far-right groups; Abascal fanned the flames, claiming Spain suffers a "brutal migratory invasion."[^60] This represents the most significant far-right-linked racial violence in recent Spanish history and illustrates how the Reconquista frame converts immigration anxiety into organized confrontation.

**Western Sahara** constitutes a suppressed decolonization grievance. Spain colonized the territory from approximately 1884 to 1976; the November 1975 Madrid Accords ceded responsibility to Morocco and Mauritania despite a UN mission finding "overwhelming consensus" for Sahrawi independence and an ICJ ruling supporting self-determination. Spain is technically still the administering power under international law, having never formally completed decolonization. The 50th anniversary of withdrawal (February 2026) has prompted renewed attention. Sánchez's March 2022 endorsement of Morocco's autonomy plan — reversing decades of official neutrality — generated opposition from Sumar (Yolanda Díaz boycotted the December 2025 Morocco summit, posting that "not one centimeter of Sahrawi territory" should be ceded) and Sahrawi solidarity organizations in Spain. The estimated 3,000–12,000 Sahrawis in Spain maintain advocacy for self-determination, but the grievance's constraint strength is weak: it affects Spain-Morocco and Spain-Algeria relations but does not significantly constrain broader foreign policy.[^61]

**Gibraltar** functions as a low-salience but persistent grievance. Ceded to Britain under the Treaty of Utrecht (1713), it represents the last European territorial dispute on sovereign soil. The February 2026 post-Brexit treaty breakthrough has partially defused this: Spain gains a role in border and residency decisions while accepting continued UK sovereignty. The grievance's constraint strength is weak — no Spanish government would risk a significant diplomatic crisis over Gibraltar alone — but it provides a permanent irritant that can be activated when UK-Spain relations deteriorate for other reasons.[^59]

The **Leyenda Negra** (Black Legend) — 16th-century Protestant propaganda portraying the Spanish Empire as uniquely cruel — shapes diplomatic reflexes more than domestic politics. Julián Juderías systematized the concept in 1914, in the acute vulnerability after 1898. María Elvira Roca Barea's *Imperiofobia y Leyenda Negra* (2016) became a bestseller (17+ editions) arguing Spain's colonial legacy has been unfairly maligned. Mexico's 2019 demand for a colonial-era apology provoked intense backlash. Spain's sensitivity to external criticism of its colonial record shapes its framing of *Hispanidad* as a cultural bridge rather than imperial project, and produces defensive reactions when Latin American leaders challenge this framing.

Where Western analytical frameworks systematically misread Spanish behavior: (a) **Anti-war sentiment** is typically interpreted as pacifism or weakness; the corrective frame is that it represents a deeply embedded institutional constraint rooted in Civil War trauma and Iraq War experience, making military adventurism politically suicidal regardless of strategic logic. (b) **Europeanism** is typically interpreted as ideological commitment to liberal internationalism; the corrective frame is that it is equally a compensatory mechanism for the 1898 wound, making Spain's EU commitment deeper and more structurally embedded than ideological preference alone would produce. (c) **Catalan independence** is typically interpreted through self-determination frameworks; the corrective frame is that it operates through fiscal grievance mechanisms (Section 4) as much as identity dynamics, meaning economic conditions are as relevant as cultural politics to its trajectory.

## Section 17: CROSS-FACET INTERSECTION ANALYSIS

**Intersection 1: Water scarcity (Section 8) × Electoral system mechanics (Section 15) × España vaciada demographics (Section 5)**

Spain's water crisis does not distribute evenly across the electorate. The Tagus-Segura transfer — sustaining €2.4 billion in agricultural GDP in the southeast — is being eliminated by a combination of climate change (70–79% reduction in transferable flows projected) and a May 2025 Supreme Court ruling enforcing ecological flows. The affected regions (Murcia, southern Valencia, eastern Andalusia) are represented through provinces that receive a minimum two-seat allocation in the D'Hondt system, over-representing rural agricultural constituencies. Meanwhile, the donor basin (Castilla-La Mancha) is governed by PSOE's Emiliano García-Page, who has declared the transfer "history." The intersection creates a structural political trap: water reallocation away from irrigated agriculture will disproportionately affect electorally over-represented rural constituencies that already trend toward PP and Vox, converting a hydrological crisis into a territorial-electoral crisis. When the pipeline encounters reporting on water policy disputes, it should assess whether the dispute is operating as a proxy for the center-periphery electoral geography described in Section 4 rather than interpreting it at face value as an environmental policy disagreement. The distinguishing indicator is whether affected agricultural interests frame their opposition through territorial-identity language ("Madrid is stealing our water") rather than technical-economic language.

**Intersection 2: Francoist institutional residue (Section 3) × Judicial politicization (Section 15) × Reciprocal institutional degradation**

The CGPJ's appointment mechanism — parliamentary election requiring three-fifths majority — was designed during the Transition as a democratic check on a judiciary inherited wholesale from the Franco regime. González admitted: "the state apparatus was retained, in its entirety, from the dictatorship." The mechanism was meant to ensure democratic accountability over a judiciary that had served authoritarian power. Four decades later, the judiciary has been thoroughly democratized in personnel but the accountability mechanism has been weaponized: PP's 5.5-year blockade of CGPJ renewal exploited the very tool designed to prevent Francoist judicial persistence, using it instead to preserve partisan advantage. The intersection reveals that Transition-era institutional designs, created to solve one problem (authoritarian residue), have generated a structurally different problem (partisan capture). When the pipeline encounters reporting on judicial reform proposals, it should assess whether the proposal addresses the original Transition-era concern (democratic accountability) or the current concern (partisan weaponization) — these require opposite solutions, and proposals that address one will typically worsen the other. The distinguishing indicator is whether the reform proposes to increase or decrease parliamentary involvement in judicial appointments.

**Intersection 3: Tourism dependency (Section 6) × Housing crisis (Section 13 civil society mobilization) × Demographic transformation (Section 5)**

Spain's tourism sector (12.6–15.6% of GDP, 96.8 million visitors in 2025) intersects with its housing crisis through a specific mechanism: short-term rental platforms (Airbnb, Booking.com) convert residential housing stock into tourist accommodation, reducing supply and driving up rents in precisely the urban areas where the immigrant workforce (20.3% of population, concentrated in service sectors) needs to live. The April 2025 housing protests — the largest since 15-M — and the anti-tourism protests across Barcelona, Mallorca, and the Canary Islands are frequently reported as separate phenomena, but they share a common structural driver: the commodification of urban housing stock by tourism capital. The intersection produces a paradox: Spain's demographic survival depends on immigration (negative natural population balance since 2017), but the economic model that attracts immigrants (tourism-driven services) simultaneously makes housing unaffordable for them. When the pipeline encounters reporting on either housing protests or anti-tourism protests, it should assess whether the intersection dynamic is at work — tourist accommodation conversion reducing immigrant housing supply — rather than interpreting them as separate issues. The distinguishing indicator is whether protest demands target both tourism regulation and housing affordability simultaneously, indicating awareness of the structural linkage.

**Intersection 4: Energy island infrastructure (Section 7) × France as geographic gatekeeper (Section 2) × Defense industrial dependency (Section 14, FCAS)**

Spain's dependence on France operates across three nominally separate domains — energy interconnection, geographic access, and defense industrial cooperation — but these create a compound dependency that no single-domain analysis captures. France's resistance to electricity interconnection expansion (2.8 GW, congested 68% of the time) keeps Spain an energy island; France's position as sole land gateway constrains trade and transport; and France's Dassault dominates the FCAS program on which Spain's sixth-generation fighter depends. This compound dependency means France holds leverage over Spain across energy, trade, and defense simultaneously. A bilateral dispute in any one domain can cascade into others — and France has demonstrated willingness to use this leverage (shelving MidCat in 2019, dominating FCAS workshare negotiations). When the pipeline encounters reporting on Franco-Spanish friction in any single domain (energy, transport, defense), it should assess whether the friction is isolated or part of a cross-domain leverage play. The distinguishing indicator is whether French resistance in one domain coincides with demands or concessions sought in another domain.

**Intersection 5: Collective memory of anti-Americanism (Section 16) × US LNG energy dependency (Section 7/14) × Iran Hormuz transit privileges (Section 14)**

The March 2026 base-access denial was interpreted through the Iraq War template — and indeed Sánchez explicitly invoked 2003. But the structural consequences extend beyond memory politics into energy geography. US LNG now constitutes 30% of Spain's gas supply (up from 16.6% in 2024), creating a dependency that did not exist during the Iraq confrontation. Simultaneously, Iran's reward of Hormuz transit privileges for Spanish shipping creates an economic incentive structure that reinforces the anti-war political consensus. The intersection produces a novel dynamic: Spain's anti-American collective memory, which previously operated purely as a domestic political constraint, now has material energy-geography consequences. A Spanish government seeking reconciliation with Washington would face not only the 53.2% domestic majority backing defiance but also the concrete economic risk of disrupted LNG supply (if the US weaponizes energy) and loss of Hormuz transit privileges (if Iran revokes them). When the pipeline encounters reporting on US-Spain diplomatic repair efforts, it should assess whether the energy dependency dimension constrains the reconciliation space rather than interpreting the dispute as purely political. The distinguishing indicator is whether Spanish energy procurement decisions (LNG contract diversification, alternative supplier negotiations) accompany or precede diplomatic signaling.

**Intersection 6: Basque fiscal autonomy (Section 3) × Pension sustainability crisis (Section 5) × Immigration-dependent demographics (Section 5)**

The Basque Concierto Económico — under which foral provinces collect virtually all taxes and pay a negotiated *cupo* to Madrid — creates a fiscal asymmetry that intersects with the pension crisis through a specific mechanism: the common-regime communities that bear the pension system's full weight (since foral communities manage their own fiscal transfers) are also the communities most dependent on immigration to maintain their worker-to-retiree ratios. As the pension spending gap widens (projected to rise from 12.7% to 16.1–16.3% of GDP by 2050), pressure will intensify on common-regime communities to increase immigration integration while the foral communities — insulated by fiscal autonomy — face less acute demographic pressure. This intersection means the pension crisis will exacerbate territorial fiscal resentment, with common-regime communities (particularly Catalonia and Valencia) pointing to the Basque fiscal privilege as an inequity that worsens their burden. When the pipeline encounters reporting on pension reform or territorial financing negotiations, it should assess whether the Basque Concierto is operating as a grievance amplifier rather than interpreting pension reform and territorial financing as separate policy domains. The distinguishing indicator is whether non-foral communities explicitly link pension burden-sharing to Concierto reform demands.

STRUCTURAL CLAIMS:
1. Water reallocation from irrigated agriculture will function as a territorial-electoral crisis because affected constituencies are structurally over-represented in the D'Hondt electoral system, converting hydrological policy into center-periphery political conflict. [ASSESSED]
2. France holds compound leverage over Spain across energy, transport, and defense domains simultaneously — a cross-domain dependency that makes bilateral friction in any single domain potentially cascading. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]
3. The March 2026 Iran crisis created a novel intersection between collective memory (anti-Americanism) and energy geography (US LNG dependency, Iranian Hormuz privileges) that materially constrains future US-Spain reconciliation beyond the purely political dimension. [ASSESSED]
4. Transition-era institutional designs (CGPJ appointment mechanism) created to solve authoritarian-residue problems have generated structurally different partisan-capture problems — reform proposals addressing one concern will typically worsen the other. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]
5. The pension sustainability crisis will amplify territorial fiscal resentment against the Basque Concierto Económico as common-regime communities bear disproportionate pension burden without equivalent fiscal autonomy. [MODERATE CONFIDENCE]

## Section 18: KEY ANALYTICAL JUDGMENTS

**Judgment 1:** Spain will not meet NATO's 5% GDP defense spending target by 2035, and will struggle to sustain even the 2.1% cap it negotiated at the Hague Summit beyond the current political cycle. Defense spending reached 2% only through a one-time 43.1% surge in 2025; structural fiscal constraints (pension spending rising to 16.1% of GDP by 2050, debt above 100% of GDP, no new budget since 2023) and deep public resistance to military expenditure make sustained high defense spending politically unsustainable regardless of which party governs. **This judgment would be falsified by:** Spain maintaining defense spending above 2.5% of GDP for three consecutive fiscal years, or a governing coalition explicitly adopting a higher NATO target with parliamentary approval. **[HIGH CONFIDENCE]** — based on converging evidence from Section 5 (pension fiscal cliff), Section 6 (Eurozone fiscal constraints), Section 12 (chronic defense underfunding history), and Section 16 (anti-militarist collective memory).

**Judgment 2:** Spain's next government (by August 2027 at the latest) will be PP-led, likely requiring Vox support or tolerance. This transition will generate immediate friction between Vox's sovereigntist/anti-immigration positioning and Spain's EU institutional commitments, but will not produce an existential crisis because Spain's EU lock-in costs (Section 11) are prohibitively high and no significant political actor — including Vox — advocates EU withdrawal. The most likely outcome is selective non-compliance on specific EU policies (migration quotas, climate targets) rather than systemic confrontation. **This judgment would be falsified by:** PSOE winning the next general election or forming a government, or PP governing without Vox support or tolerance. **[MODERATE CONFIDENCE]** — polling is consistent (PP ~30.3%, PSOE ~28.4%, Vox ~17.9%) but Spanish elections have repeatedly produced unexpected outcomes, and PSOE has demonstrated recovery capacity from apparently terminal positions.

**Judgment 3:** The Catalan independence question will not return to crisis levels within the next political cycle (through approximately 2031), barring a severe economic shock or a PP-Vox government that deliberately re-escalates confrontation. Independence support at 38% (lowest recorded) and the PSC's governance of the Generalitat under Illa represent a structural de-escalation. However, the ~40% independence floor constitutes a permanent mobilizable reserve, and the unresolved constitutional question (no mechanism for legal self-determination) means the issue is contained, not resolved. **This judgment would be falsified by:** independence support returning above 50% in CEO surveys, a pro-independence majority being restored in the Catalan parliament, or mass mobilization exceeding 500,000 participants at any single event. **[MODERATE CONFIDENCE]** — the de-escalation trajectory is well-documented, but the absence of a permanent constitutional resolution leaves the issue vulnerable to reactivation by external shocks.

**Judgment 4:** Water scarcity will become Spain's most politically disruptive domestic issue within the next decade, surpassing immigration and housing in its capacity to generate territorial conflict, economic disruption, and institutional stress. The Tagus-Segura transfer's decline (70–79% flow reduction projected), the agricultural sector's 80% water consumption at 2.3% GDP contribution, and the Supreme Court's ecological flow enforcement create an irreversible structural reallocation that will pit agricultural regions against urban centers, southern basins against northern ones, and tourism against farming. **This judgment would be falsified by:** a sustained multi-year recovery in precipitation patterns returning to pre-2020 averages, or a technological breakthrough (cost-effective desalination at scale) that removes the zero-sum allocation dynamic. **[HIGH CONFIDENCE]** — based on converging evidence from Section 2 (geographic water asymmetry), Section 8 (climate projections showing 20–40% precipitation decline), and Section 17 (water-electoral intersection).

**Judgment 5:** Spain will emerge from the current period of US retrenchment with a structurally reduced security dependence on Washington but a paradoxically increased energy dependence (US LNG at 30% of gas supply). The March 2026 crisis demonstrated that Spain can absorb the political costs of defying the US on military operations, but the economic dependencies created by energy market restructuring since 2022 (replacing Algerian pipeline gas with US LNG) have created a new vulnerability that did not exist during the Iraq confrontation. **This judgment would be falsified by:** US LNG share of Spanish gas supply falling below 15% for two consecutive years, or Spain establishing alternative supply contracts that provide comparable volume and price. **[HIGH CONFIDENCE]** — based on Section 7 (LNG infrastructure), Section 14 (US energy dependency analysis), and the documented trajectory of supply diversification since 2022.

**Judgment 6:** FCAS program survival through to a prototype demonstrator (currently targeted ~2029) is necessary for Spain's defense-industrial sovereignty but faces at least a 40% probability of program collapse or fundamental restructuring due to Franco-German industrial disputes. Spain is the most vulnerable partner — lacking industrial capacity for an independent sixth-generation fighter and having ruled out the F-35. Program failure would force Spain toward either the Turkish Kaan fighter (already being explored as a hedge) or acceptance of permanent technological dependency on a non-European platform. **This judgment would be falsified by:** successful Phase 2 launch and demonstrator flight within two years of current target, or Spain securing a credible alternative sixth-generation program partnership. **[ASSESSED]** — the probability estimate reflects the severity of Dassault-Airbus disputes (Section 12, Section 14) but alternative resolution pathways remain plausible; Germany's silence is the key uncertainty variable.

### Event Significance Thresholds

**Structural Continuity (baseline priority):**
- Cabinet reshuffles that preserve the left-right factional balance and maintain the PSOE-Sumar coalition structure. A reshuffle where Montero departs for Andalusia or Díaz steps back from front-line politics is expected and represents continuity.
- Quarterly defense spending reports showing Spain at or near 2% GDP. The 2025 surge was a structural adjustment, not a policy revolution; spending hovering between 1.8–2.2% reflects the expected fiscal constraint dynamic.
- Catalan political developments where independence support remains in the 35–42% range and PSC continues governing the Generalitat. The post-*procés* containment is the structural baseline.
- Spanish opposition to NATO spending increases above 2.1% or abstention from specific alliance operations. Spain's selective-participation posture is the structural norm, not a deviation.
- Anti-tourism or housing protests at the scale seen in 2024–2025. These represent activated structural grievances (Section 13) operating through established channels.

**Genuine Deviation (elevated priority):**
- A cabinet reshuffle that concentrates key portfolios (Interior, Defense, Economy) in a single political faction or introduces figures from outside the PSOE-Sumar coalition framework — this would signal a governance model change.
- Defense spending rising above 2.5% or falling below 1.5% of GDP — either direction represents a break from the structural equilibrium and indicates either genuine rearmament commitment or fiscal capitulation.
- Catalan independence support surging above 48% in CEO surveys, combined with Diada mobilizations exceeding 500,000 — this would indicate the post-*procés* containment is failing.
- Spain joining any new bilateral defense agreements with non-NATO/non-EU partners (Turkey, Gulf states, or any BRICS-adjacent framework) that go beyond arms procurement — this would signal genuine multi-vector realignment rather than selective defection within the alliance.
- A Spanish government formally requesting renegotiation of the 1988 ADC or requesting US base drawdown — this would signal a structural relationship change, not an episodic confrontation.

### Common Misreading Patterns

**Misreading 1: Interpreting Spain's base-access denial as a "rupture" with NATO.** Spain denied operational access for offensive Iran strikes while maintaining defensive alliance commitments (Patriot battery support, Baltic Air Policing, UNIFIL). The correct analytical frame is **selective defection within the alliance** — a posture enabled by the legal architecture of the 1988 ADC and the EU's collective trade shield — not alliance dissolution. Spain remains firmly embedded in NATO and EU institutional structures; the defection capacity exists precisely because the alliance commitment is otherwise deep enough to absorb it.

**Misreading 2: Treating Spanish domestic corruption scandals as indicators of institutional decay.** Both major parties have been consumed by corruption scandals spanning decades (Gürtel for PP, ERE for PSOE, Koldo currently). These are structural features of Spain's political economy (Section 9), not signs of recent deterioration. The pipeline should assess each corruption revelation against the baseline expectation of endemic party-financing irregularities and personal enrichment, and elevate only cases that reach beyond the established pattern — specifically, cases that compromise institutional independence (the Attorney General conviction), implicate the head of government directly (not just associates), or involve foreign state actors.

**Misreading 3 (LLM-specific — recency bias): Over-weighting the March 2026 Iran crisis as a turning point.** When processing US-Spain relations reporting, the pipeline should resist treating the Iran crisis as a rupture and instead assess it against the cyclical pattern described in Section 16: the 1953 bases association, 1986 NATO referendum conditions, 2003 Iraq War opposition, and 2026 Iran denial form a consistent pattern of Spanish resistance to US military operations perceived as illegitimate. The structural pattern predicts periodic confrontation followed by pragmatic accommodation on non-military dimensions. The distinguishing indicator for genuine structural change (versus cyclical friction) would be Spanish action to renegotiate the 1988 ADC or request base drawdown — absent this, treat the crisis as within the established oscillation range.

**Misreading 4 (LLM-specific — Western-framework imposition): Analyzing Spain through a standard "Southern European fiscal irresponsibility" frame.** Spain's simultaneous triple credit-rating upgrade (September 2025), risk premium falling below France's, and GDP growth double the Eurozone average represent a structural reputational inflection. The old frame — PIIGS, bailout recipient, austerity subject — no longer maps onto Spain's fiscal reality (debt declining toward 100% GDP, deficit at 2.5%, employment at record highs). The pipeline should apply an updated frame: Spain as a fiscal outperformer constrained by pension obligations and defense spending pressure, not as a crisis-state on permanent probation.

**Misreading 5 (LLM-specific — false equivalence): "Both sides" framing of the Supreme Court–Constitutional Court Amnesty Law conflict.** The structural reality involves an asymmetry: the Constitutional Court (the institution constitutionally empowered to determine the constitutionality of laws) upheld the Amnesty Law at 6–4; the Supreme Court (which is not empowered to override constitutional rulings) is declining to apply the law to Puigdemont on narrow grounds. Framing this as a "disagreement between courts" obscures the institutional hierarchy — the Constitutional Court's ruling is constitutionally supreme, and the Supreme Court's resistance represents institutional defiance, not legitimate legal disagreement. The pipeline should frame this as the Supreme Court testing the limits of Constitutional Court authority, not as an evenly balanced interpretive dispute.

### Cross-Leader Connection Points

**Spain-France (Sánchez-Macron/successor):**
**Structural driver:** France is Spain's sole land gateway to continental Europe, dominant FCAS partner, and gatekeeper of energy interconnection. **Void-filling dynamic:** The US previously served as the ultimate security backstop that made Franco-Spanish bilateral friction manageable within NATO. US retrenchment has elevated the bilateral relationship's load: Spain and France must now coordinate on European defense (FCAS, EU strategic autonomy), energy security (H2Med, Bay of Biscay interconnection), and Mediterranean migration without US mediation. The relationship has deepened but also become more fraught. **Routine vs. significant:** Joint ministerial councils and FCAS working-group meetings are routine. A French announcement blocking or substantially delaying the Bay of Biscay interconnection, or German FCAS withdrawal leaving France and Spain as sole partners, would be significant. **Tension points:** FCAS workshare (Dassault dominance), energy interconnection pace, agricultural trade competition, migration burden-sharing.

**Spain-Italy (Sánchez-Meloni):**
**Structural driver:** Co-recipients of the largest NGEU allocations; shared Mediterranean migration exposure; aligned FCAS-outsider interest (Italy in GCAP, Spain in FCAS but both needing European defense-industrial options). **Void-filling dynamic:** Limited. Italy and Spain previously operated in parallel within US-anchored NATO; the relationship has not fundamentally changed character under US retrenchment, though both now have greater incentive to coordinate on Mediterranean security and EU southern-tier fiscal interests. **Routine vs. significant:** Bilateral summits and Mediterranean cooperation agreements are routine. An Italy-Spain joint defense procurement initiative outside existing NATO/EU frameworks, or coordinated opposition to northern-tier EU fiscal austerity, would be significant. **Tension points:** Divergent migration policies (Meloni's restrictive approach vs. Sánchez's regularization program), different Russia postures (Meloni's Atlantic alignment vs. Spain's selective defection).

**Spain-Germany (Sánchez-Merz):**
**Structural driver:** FCAS trilateral partner; Germany is Spain's second-largest EU trading partner; Airbus supply chain integration. **Void-filling dynamic:** Moderate. Germany's silence on FCAS (December 2025) and exploration of GCAP alternatives threaten Spain's defense modernization. The relationship has become more consequential as FCAS survival depends partly on German commitment. **Routine vs. significant:** Trade consultations and Airbus coordination are routine. German FCAS withdrawal announcement or German-Spanish joint Taurus cruise missile development (signed August 2025) deepening into broader bilateral defense partnership would be significant. **Tension points:** FCAS survival, EU fiscal rules enforcement, defense spending expectations.

**Spain-Poland (Sánchez-Tusk):**
**Structural driver:** Both are post-democratic-erosion-scare middle powers with strong EU commitment; aligned on EU institutional reform; complementary security profiles (Poland eastern flank, Spain southern flank). **Void-filling dynamic:** Significant. Poland's security anxiety about Russia and Spain's Mediterranean/Atlantic orientation create potential for a structural partnership distributing EU defense burdens geographically. Both countries' EU institutional weight (combined ~16% of EU population) makes their alignment consequential for qualified majority votes. **Routine vs. significant:** EU Council coordination and bilateral meetings are routine. A formal Spain-Poland security cooperation agreement or coordinated EU defense-spending initiative would be significant. **Tension points:** Migration policy (Poland's eastern border experience vs. Spain's southern exposure), defense spending levels (Poland at ~4% GDP vs. Spain at ~2%), Russia posture intensity.

**Spain-Morocco (Sánchez-Mohammed VI):**
**Structural driver:** Migration control leverage, Ceuta/Melilla territorial claims, Western Sahara, energy transit (Medgaz pipeline). **Void-filling dynamic:** Limited direct US role in this bilateral; the relationship's dynamics are primarily regional. However, US retrenchment from the Middle East and North Africa reduces the external constraint on Moroccan assertiveness, potentially increasing migration leverage pressure. **Routine vs. significant:** High-level meetings and migration cooperation are routine (December 2025 summit with 14 memoranda is the new baseline). Moroccan resumption of migration pressure (relaxing border enforcement at Ceuta/Melilla), formal sovereignty claims over the enclaves, or Spain reversing the Western Sahara position would be significant. **Tension points:** Western Sahara (domestic opposition from Sumar and Polisario supporters), Ceuta/Melilla sovereignty, fisheries agreements, Tanger Med port competition with Algeciras.

**Spain-UK (Sánchez-Starmer):**
**Structural driver:** Gibraltar post-Brexit treaty (February 2026), defense cooperation, intelligence sharing. **Void-filling dynamic:** Moderate. Brexit severed the UK's EU institutional relationship, making bilateral frameworks more important. The Gibraltar treaty represents the most significant UK-Spain institutional innovation in decades. **Routine vs. significant:** Gibraltar treaty implementation milestones are the key monitoring events. Provisional application commencing (target April 10, 2026) is routine if on schedule; failure to commence by July 2026 would be significant. **Tension points:** Gibraltar sovereignty sensitivities, fisheries, post-Brexit trade friction, divergent climate policy ambition.

**Spain-Turkey (Sánchez-Erdoğan):**
**Structural driver:** NATO allies with Mediterranean exposure; emerging defense-industrial link (Kaan fighter exploration, 30 Hürjet trainers ordered December 2025). **Void-filling dynamic:** Potentially significant and growing. Turkey's positioning as an alternative defense-industrial partner for European states seeking options outside Franco-German dominance intersects with Spain's FCAS vulnerability. If FCAS collapses, Turkey becomes a structurally important defense partner. **Routine vs. significant:** Bilateral defense procurement contracts are routine. Spanish formal participation in a Turkish-led fighter program or Turkish Kaan acquisition would be highly significant — signaling both FCAS collapse and a major alignment reorientation. **Tension points:** Turkey's Russia relationship, Erdoğan's democratic erosion record, EU-Turkey friction on migration and human rights.
