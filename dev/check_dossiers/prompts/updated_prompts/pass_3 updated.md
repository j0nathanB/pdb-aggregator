For each relationship, assess: (a) what role did the US previously play in mediating, anchoring, or backstopping this bilateral dynamic (if any); (b) how has the relationship adapted to the absence of that role — has it deepened, become more fraught, or remained largely unaffected; and (c) what new functions (security coordination, institutional coalition-building, economic partnership, diplomatic alignment) has this bilateral relationship taken on that it did not previously perform? Relationships where the void-filling dynamic is negligible should be noted as such — not every bilateral connection is shaped by US absence.
  - **Routine vs. significant threshold:** What kinds of interactions between these two leaders represent routine institutional behavior (and can be processed at baseline priority), and what kinds of interactions would represent a significant shift (and should trigger elevated analytical attention)? Be specific.
  - **Tension points:** Where do the structural interests of these two countries diverge, and what issues are most likely to produce friction? This helps the pipeline interpret bilateral disagreements in structural context rather than as personality-driven disputes.

### Stylistic Instructions

- Write in analytical prose. No bullet points within sections except in Sections 0, 18, 19, and 20.
- Favor causal explanation over description. "X happened" is less valuable than "X happened *because* Y, which means Z for today."
- Be specific. Name dates, figures, percentages, and treaty names where they strengthen the argument. Avoid vague hedging when you have evidence.
- Assume the consuming LLM has no prior regional expertise loaded in context. Provide enough context that references are self-contained — a name, date, or event mentioned without explanation is useless.
- When the historical record is contested or the analytical inference is uncertain, say so explicitly rather than presenting interpretation as settled fact.
- Do not editorialize about whether the country's behavior is good or bad. Explain the logic of its behavior from its own structural position.
- Where a concept has a direct parallel in another country the reader might know better, a brief comparative aside (one sentence) can accelerate understanding — but do not overuse this technique.
- Every section must conclude with a `STRUCTURAL CLAIMS` block of numbered, discrete, falsifiable assertions.
- Target length: 8,000–12,000 words for the initial seed dossier.

### Source Citation Instructions

Cite sources using Markdown footnote syntax. Every substantive factual claim, statistic, date, treaty reference, or institutional detail that is not common knowledge should carry a footnote citation. Analytical inferences drawn from cited facts do not need separate citations, but the underlying facts they rest on do.

Use numbered footnotes in sequential order: `[^1]`, `[^2]`, etc. Place all footnote definitions at the end of your output in a `## Sources` section. Format each footnote as:

```
[^N]: Author/Organization, "Title," Publication, Date. URL (if available).
```

If citing from web search results, include the URL. If citing from training knowledge, provide the most specific attribution you can (e.g., "World Bank, World Development Indicators, 2023" or "Constitution of [Country], Article X").

Prioritize primary and institutional sources: government publications, central bank reports, treaty texts, constitutional documents, international organization datasets, and established academic works. Use journalistic sources for recent events and institutional dynamics that primary sources do not cover.

Aim for a minimum of 15–25 citations per pass. Density should be highest in sections with specific empirical claims (Sections 0, 5, 6, 7, 11, 12) and can be lighter in primarily analytical sections (17, 18).
