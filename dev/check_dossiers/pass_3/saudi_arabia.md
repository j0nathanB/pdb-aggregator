## SECTION 13: History of Dissent and Civil Society Infrastructure

Saudi Arabia under Mohammed bin Salman has achieved the most comprehensive elimination of organized opposition infrastructure in the kingdom's modern history. Through a three-pronged strategy of decapitating opposition networks, deploying world-class digital surveillance, and offering social liberalization as a pressure valve, MBS has created a system where no domestic entity capable of independent mobilization exists outside state control. **Freedom House scores Saudi Arabia 7 out of 100 (Not Free)**, and the kingdom currently has **near-zero organizational capacity for domestic mobilization** in a crisis scenario.

### The Sahwa movement rose and fell as the kingdom's only mass opposition

The Islamic Awakening (Sahwa) movement emerged from the 1950s–60s cross-pollination between exiled Muslim Brotherhood members fleeing Egypt and Syria and Saudi Arabia's native Wahhabi-Salafi tradition. The intellectual godfather was Muhammad Qutb (brother of Sayyid Qutb), who taught at Umm al-Qura University in Mecca for thirty years. Syrian scholar Muhammad Surur Zayn al-Abidin gave rise to the "Sururist" branch. The movement spread through the education system, gaining mass following during the 1980s Afghan jihad era.

The 1990–91 Gulf War catalyzed the Sahwa into overt political opposition. The deployment of **500,000 U.S. troops** on Saudi soil provoked fury among religious constituencies. In 1991, Sahwa-affiliated scholars presented the "Letter of Demands" (Khitab al-Matalib) to King Fahd, followed by the more expansive 1992 "Memorandum of Advice." Key figures **Salman al-Awda** and **Safar al-Hawali** became household names through cassette-tape sermons distributed across the kingdom. The state responded with mass arrests in 1994–95, imprisoning hundreds. Al-Awda and al-Hawali were released in 1999 as part of a rapprochement, and were partially co-opted during the post-9/11 counter-terrorism era.

The 2003 reform petitions ("Vision for the Present and Future of the Homeland") represented an intellectual evolution toward constitutional-monarchist demands, and the Saudi Civil and Political Rights Association (ACPRA) became the most prominent domestic rights organization before being banned. Its founder Abdullah al-Hamid died in custody in 2020; co-founder Mohammed al-Qahtani was tried in the anti-terror court.

The 2011 Arab Spring tested Saudi Arabia's stability. On March 11, a planned "Day of Rage" attracted 26,000+ Facebook followers, but the state deployed overwhelming police presence, including helicopter overflights of major cities. King Abdullah announced **$130 billion in assistance and job creation**, the Council of Higher Ulama issued a fatwa against protests, and the regime successfully sectarianized the discourse. Protests materialized primarily in the Shia Eastern Province—in Qatif, Hofuf, and Awamiyah—and continued through 2012, representing the "largest and longest protest movement in Saudi modern history" while remaining geographically and sectarianly confined.

MBS's September 2017 crackdown effectively destroyed the Sahwa as an organized force. Al-Awda, Awad al-Qarni, and Ali al-Omari were arrested; al-Hawali was re-arrested in July 2018 after publishing a 3,000-page book criticizing the royal family. The Muslim Brotherhood was designated a terrorist organization in 2014. The movement has, in the words of Saudi analysts, "ceased to exist" domestically.

### Prominent dissidents face decades in prison for tweets

The scale and severity of repression under MBS represents a qualitative escalation from previous eras:

**Loujain al-Hathloul**, the women's rights activist who campaigned for driving rights and an end to male guardianship, was arrested in May 2018—weeks before the driving ban was lifted. She was tortured with electric shocks, waterboarding, and sexual harassment. Sentenced to 5 years and 8 months by the Specialized Criminal Court in December 2020, she was released in February 2021 but remains under a travel ban extending to approximately 2026 and cannot leave Saudi Arabia.

**Salman al-Awda**, the Sahwa cleric with 14 million Twitter followers, has been detained since September 2017 and faces the death penalty for charges including "stirring public discord"—reportedly stemming from a tweet that appeared supportive of Qatar reconciliation.

**Mohammed al-Ghamdi**, a retired teacher aged 54, was sentenced to death in July 2023 for tweets from anonymous accounts with a combined total of **10 followers**. His sentence was commuted on appeal to **30 years**. His brother Asaad received 20 years for similar online criticisms.

**Salma al-Shehab**, a Leeds University PhD student, was sentenced to **27 years plus a 27-year travel ban** for tweets supporting women's rights. **Nourah al-Qahtani** received **45 years plus a 45-year travel ban** for social media posts. **Manahel al-Otaibi**, a fitness instructor, received **11 years** for Snapchat photos without an abaya. Executions reached a record **338 in 2024**, doubling from 170 in 2023.

Exile organizations including **ALQST** (London, founded ~2013), **DAWN** (Washington, founded by Jamal Khashoggi before his murder), and **SANAD** (UK-based) provide documentation and advocacy but have, per Carnegie's assessment, "negligible influence on actual developments within the kingdom." Saudi asylum seekers tripled between 2012 and 2017.

### Digital surveillance has neutralized online dissent

Saudi Arabia is a confirmed NSO Group Pegasus client. Documented targets include ALQST founder Yahya Assiri (targeted 2018 and 2020), Omar Abdulaziz (Canadian-based dissident and Khashoggi collaborator), Ghanem al-Masarir (UK-based dissident, also physically attacked near Harrods), and al-Hathloul herself. The UK High Court in 2025 ordered Saudi Arabia to pay **£3,025,663** in damages for Pegasus hacking and a directed physical attack on al-Masarir.

MBS's former adviser **Saud al-Qahtani** organized troll farm operations employing hundreds of young men at approximately $3,000/month, with daily tweet quotas, target lists for intimidation, and systematic mass-reporting of critical content. Twitter employee **Ali Alzabarah** was recruited by Saudi intelligence to spy on dissident accounts—a successful penetration of a major social media platform. Self-censorship is now "pervasive," and prosecution for social media posts with as few as 10 followers demonstrates that even minimal online expression carries existential risk.

### The women's rights paradox and the absence of labor organizing

The central tension in Saudi women's rights is that the state implements reforms activists fought for while imprisoning the activists. The driving ban was lifted in June 2018 while al-Hathloul sat in prison. The March 2022 Personal Status Law, despite introducing a minimum marriage age, **codified male guardianship** in marriage, divorce, custody, and inheritance. Female labor participation reached **35% in 2024**, exceeding the Vision 2030 target of 30%, but this represents state-directed economic policy, not a rights-based achievement.

Saudi Arabia has had no independent trade unions since the 1950s, when ARAMCO labor actions were crushed. Among the **13.4 million migrant workers** (41.6% of the population), the kafala sponsorship system was formally abolished in June 2025, but enforcement across 1.2 million employers remains, in HRW's assessment, a "monumental task." Domestic workers (approximately 3.6 million people) were excluded from earlier reforms. Human Rights Watch documented Saudi border guards killing **hundreds of Ethiopian migrants** at the Yemen border in 2022–2023.

## SECTION 14: Patron-Client History and Alliance Genealogy

Saudi Arabia is executing the most significant recalibration of its foreign policy since the 1945 Roosevelt–Ibn Saud compact. Under MBS, the kingdom has shifted from mono-alignment with Washington to a sophisticated multi-vector hedging strategy—maintaining the United States as its primary security partner while building optionality across Chinese, Russian, Pakistani, Turkish, and other relationships. The key structural fact remains: **Saudi Arabia's most advanced military platforms require continuous American support**, creating a dependency that cannot be replaced in the near term, but each diversification move gives Riyadh more leverage and resilience.

### The US-Saudi relationship from the Quincy to the F-35

The foundational moment was President Roosevelt's February 14, 1945 meeting with King Abdulaziz aboard USS Quincy, establishing the implicit "oil for security" bargain. The relationship deepened through the ARAMCO concession (nationalized by 1980), survived the 1973 oil embargo through the petrodollar arrangement (oil priced in dollars, revenues recycled into U.S. Treasuries), and was formalized strategically through the Carter Doctrine (1980), Reagan-era intelligence cooperation, and the Gulf War alliance (Saudi Arabia contributed **$36 billion**).

Post-9/11 strain (15 of 19 hijackers were Saudi) created the deepest crisis, but operational counter-terrorism cooperation deepened. Obama-era tensions over the Iran nuclear deal and Arab Spring responses shook Saudi confidence. Trump 1.0's May 2017 Riyadh visit announced a $110 billion defense deal, though only approximately **$14.5 billion** materialized. Biden called Saudi Arabia a "pariah," then executed an embarrassing retreat with the July 2022 "fist bump" visit, followed by fury over the October 2022 OPEC+ production cut of 2 million barrels per day.

**Trump 2.0 has dramatically reset the relationship.** The May 13, 2025 Riyadh visit produced a **$142 billion defense sales agreement**—described as "the largest in history"—alongside a **$600 billion Saudi investment commitment** to the United States. Verified formal FMS notifications to Congress through March 2026 total approximately **$16.6 billion**, including $9 billion for 730 PAC-3 MSE missiles, $3.5 billion for 1,000 AMRAAMs, and $3 billion for F-15 sustainment. Analysts note the $142 billion figure is likely aspirational, consistent with the 2017 pattern.

MBS's November 18, 2025 White House visit—his first to Washington since the Khashoggi murder—produced three landmark outcomes. First, Trump approved **F-35 sales** to Saudi Arabia, limited to two squadrons with less advanced configurations than Israel's F-35I, and critically **not conditioned on Israel normalization**. Delivery is years away and faces congressional review. Second, Saudi Arabia received **Major Non-NATO Ally (MNNA) designation**, the 20th country so designated. Third, a **Joint Declaration on Civil Nuclear Cooperation** was announced, completing negotiations for a Section 123 agreement that, per the Arms Control Association, **does not include traditional non-proliferation safeguards** and leaves room for Saudi enrichment. MBS has publicly stated: "If Iran gets a nuclear weapon, we would have to get one."

### Saudi-China: hardware from America, software from Beijing

Xi Jinping's December 7–9, 2022 state visit to Riyadh was the most consequential non-American summit in Saudi history. The leaders signed a **Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Agreement**, with 34 investment deals worth approximately $30–50 billion across green energy, IT, transport, and construction. A harmonization plan aligned Vision 2030 with China's Belt and Road Initiative.

China is Saudi Arabia's **largest trading partner** (bilateral trade hit $87.3 billion in 2021), and Saudi Arabia is China's largest crude oil supplier (~1.77 million barrels per day). In November 2023, the PBOC and SAMA signed their first currency swap agreement covering **RMB 50 billion (~$6.93 billion)**. Bank of China opened its first Riyadh branch in September 2023. Saudi Arabia joined the **mBridge** cross-border CBDC platform with China, Hong Kong, Thailand, and the UAE—potentially enabling oil transactions outside SWIFT. However, no confirmed large-scale yuan-denominated oil sales have been verified. The structural logic of accumulating RMB creates demand for Chinese goods and BRI participation, but the riyal's dollar peg and yuan's limited international use constrain the pace of change.

Military cooperation runs deeper than commonly acknowledged. It began with a **1986 deal for ~50 CSS-2 (DF-3) intermediate-range ballistic missiles**—conducted before formal diplomatic relations. Saudi Arabia subsequently acquired **DF-21 ballistic missiles** (neither country acknowledged publicly). Drone cooperation has accelerated dramatically: a 2017 order for Wing Loong II drones was described as China's largest-ever arms export, and a reported **$5 billion Wing Loong-3 deal** in 2025 includes a Jeddah-based assembly line. **Huawei deployed Saudi Arabia's first end-to-end 5G network** with STC in 2018 and provides the core infrastructure for NEOM's 5G network. **Six of the ten largest contractors** by contract value in Saudi Arabia are Chinese firms.

Saudi Arabia became an SCO dialogue partner in 2023. Regarding BRICS, its status remains **deliberately ambiguous**—invited in August 2023 but has not formally joined. MBS did not attend the October 2024 Kazan summit; Foreign Minister Prince Faisal arrived only for the final day. Carnegie describes Saudi Arabia as "hedging its commitment to a group that itself constitutes a hedging club."

### Saudi-Russia, Saudi-Iran, and the Beijing-brokered rapprochement

The Saudi-Russia relationship is anchored in OPEC+ coordination since 2016. The October 2022 production cut of 2 million barrels per day—the largest joint action—was explicitly against Washington's protests. Putin visited Riyadh in December 2023, declaring "nothing can prevent the development of our friendly relations." MBS facilitated multiple Ukraine-Russia prisoner exchanges and hosted U.S.-Russia peace talks in Riyadh in February 2025.

The March 10, 2023 Beijing-brokered Saudi-Iran agreement committed both sides to resume diplomatic relations, reopen embassies within two months, and reactivate dormant cooperation agreements. Implementation has proceeded: embassies reopened on schedule, multiple high-level visits occurred, and the third session of the Saudi-Iran-China trilateral committee met in Tehran in December 2025. Direct Hajj flights from Iran resumed in May 2025 for the first time in a decade. However, the relationship remains "détente, not rapprochement." The sectarian competition, Iran's network of non-state actors, and divergent U.S. relationships constitute structural limits. Notably, when the U.S. and Israel struck Iran in 2025, **Saudi Arabia refused to allow its territory or airspace to be used** and publicly condemned the strikes—a remarkable posture given decades of hostility.

### The Pakistan defense pact and the nuclear question

The **Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement** signed September 17, 2025 between MBS and Pakistani PM Shehbaz Sharif stipulates that "any aggression against either country shall be considered an aggression against both." This is the **first mutual defense pact between an Arab Gulf state and a nuclear-armed state**. Signed eight days after Israel's strike on Doha, the pact maintains deliberate nuclear ambiguity—a Saudi official stated it "encompasses all military means" while analysts debate whether it constitutes an extended deterrent. The Belfer Center assesses it as "primarily a political signal of solidarity" rather than an unconditional war guarantee, but the signal itself is consequential.

### Israel normalization is off the table

The October 7, 2023 Hamas attack froze normalization prospects that had appeared imminent weeks earlier. A Washington Institute survey (August 2025) found **99% of Saudi respondents** viewed normalization negatively. MBS reportedly told U.S. congressional members his normalization efforts "put his life at risk." Saudi Arabia's conditions have hardened: establishment of a Palestinian state (not merely a "pathway"), cessation of hostilities in Gaza, a U.S. defense pact, and civil nuclear cooperation. INSS assessed in February 2026 that "Saudi-Israeli normalization is currently off the table," and Prince Turki bin Faisal stated in December 2025: "Saudi Arabia is not considering a normalization deal with Israel."

### Multi-vector hedging as the new Saudi doctrine

Saudi Arabia's foreign policy is best understood as "transactional realism" rather than alignment shift. The kingdom maintains **American hardware for defense** (78% of arms imports), **Chinese infrastructure for economic diversification** (6 of 10 largest contractors), **Russian coordination for oil price management** (OPEC+), **Turkish drones for capability gaps** ($3 billion Bayraktar Akinci deal), and **Pakistani strategic depth** (the SMDA). The riyal remains pegged to the dollar; oil is still overwhelmingly priced in dollars. Selective yuan transactions serve as leverage and insurance, not replacement.

**UK and France** remain significant defense partners. BAE Systems has a 60-year relationship with Saudi Arabia, maintaining **6,500 personnel** in-kingdom. A deal for 48 additional Typhoons (estimated $13 billion) stalled after Germany's post-Khashoggi arms embargo but advanced after Germany lifted its block in January 2024. France, Saudi Arabia's third-largest arms supplier (~9% share), is negotiating a potential **54 Rafale** sale, while Macron has cultivated a personal relationship with MBS. Saudi-Egypt relations are increasingly transactional, with Saudi Arabia shifting to conditional aid after providing tens of billions since 2013. Saudi-Turkey relations have normalized following Erdogan's April 2022 transfer of the Khashoggi trial to Saudi jurisdiction.

## SECTION 15: Constitutional Crises and Regime Transition Patterns

MBS has executed the most thorough consolidation of power in modern Saudi history through a deliberate sequence: economics first, then military, then political, then constitutional. The absence of a deputy crown prince is simultaneously his greatest vulnerability and a deliberate choice to prevent the emergence of a rival. **The functional transition from King Salman to MBS is already complete**; the formal succession is assessed as inevitable.

### Lateral succession sustained the kingdom for six decades

From Ibn Saud's death in 1953 through 2015, succession passed laterally among his sons—a loose form of agnatic seniority rather than strict primogeniture. Certain sons were bypassed for character, health, or maternal lineage. The Sudairi Seven (sons of favored wife Hassa bint Ahmad al-Sudairi) dominated, producing three consecutive kings: Fahd, Abdullah (not Sudairi, but the interregnum), then Salman.

The 1964 removal of King Saud established critical precedents. Saud's financial mismanagement (approximately $450 million in debt), failed foreign policy, and power-grabbing prompted senior princes and the ulema to act. The process was remarkably institutionalized for a monarchy: Crown Prince Faisal was appointed regent in March 1964; when Saud ordered the Royal Guard to resist, Faisal deployed the National Guard to surround Saud's palace. On November 2, 1964, Grand Mufti Muhammad ibn Ibrahim issued a fatwa calling for abdication, and the family formally deposed Saud. The episode demonstrated that the ulema hold legitimating power, the royal family collectively can remove a king, and **the National Guard is the decisive military force in internal disputes**.

### MBS's consolidation followed a precise sequence

MBS's rise from special advisor to his father to effective ruler took seven years:

- **January 23, 2015**: Appointed Defense Minister upon Salman becoming king
- **April 29, 2015**: Elevated to Deputy Crown Prince (Crown Prince Muqrin deposed)
- **March 2015**: Launched Operation Decisive Storm in Yemen
- **June 21, 2017**: Appointed Crown Prince after Mohammed bin Nayef deposed
- **November 4, 2017**: Ritz-Carlton anti-corruption crackdown
- **September 27, 2022**: Appointed Prime Minister
- **March 2020**: Arrested Prince Ahmed bin Abdulaziz and MbN on treason charges

The June 2017 removal of Crown Prince Mohammed bin Nayef was engineered through reported coercion—MbN was summoned, denied sleep and access to medication, and pressured to swear allegiance. The Allegiance Council voted 31 to 3 in favor, but was presented with a fait accompli. MbN's March 2020 formal arrest on treason charges eliminated the last credible rival.

The **November 2017 Ritz-Carlton crackdown** detained up to 500 people, including 11 princes, 4 ministers, and dozens of businessmen. Prince Miteb bin Abdullah (National Guard Minister, son of King Abdullah) was the principal target—he reportedly paid over **$1 billion** in settlement. The government recovered approximately **$106.6 billion** through settlements with 87 individuals. Reports of torture emerged, and one detainee, Major General Ali Al Qahtani, died in custody. The operation simultaneously eliminated rival power centers and generated revenue.

### Decision-making is hypercentralized around one person

MBS holds direct or delegated authority over defense (via brother Khalid bin Salman), economic planning (CEDA chair), sovereign wealth (PIF chairman, **$925 billion** AUM), intelligence oversight, religious affairs, entertainment, and mega-project development. His inner circle includes **Khalid bin Salman** (Defense Minister, full brother, former F-15 pilot and U.S. ambassador), **Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman** (Energy Minister, half-brother, decades of OPEC experience), **Khalid al-Humaidan** (GIP intelligence chief, retained professional), and **Saud al-Qahtani** (former "troll master," formally fired after Khashoggi but reportedly maintaining contact).

The **Allegiance Council**, created by King Abdullah in 2006 with 34 members, has never overruled a sitting king's nomination. It has functioned as a rubber stamp: ratifying MbN's deputy crown prince appointment in 2015, voting to depose Muqrin in April 2015, and voting 31-3 to elevate MBS in June 2017 under conditions described as coercive. At least two dissenting members were subsequently detained.

### The succession vulnerability: no deputy, no plan

The deputy crown prince position has been **vacant since June 2017**. Two reasons explain this: MBS's relative youth (now 40) made the vacancy seem low-risk, and designating a deputy would create a potential rival during the consolidation phase. A 2017 constitutional amendment reportedly bars the king and crown prince from belonging to the same family branch, potentially complicating the candidacy of **Khalid bin Salman**—the most commonly cited backup. MBS has five children (three sons, two daughters), with the eldest approximately 15–16 years old, all far too young for political roles.

King Salman, born December 31, 1935, is **90 years old** with reported cognitive decline, multiple lung infections requiring hospitalization in 2024, and has not made a public speech since October 9, 2022. The functional transition is complete; MBS chairs all substantive decision-making bodies. Reports indicate King Salman has made a video announcing MBS as king. The orderly formal succession is assessed as the highest-probability near-term scenario.

### MBS's crisis behavior follows a consistent pattern

Across Yemen (2015), the Qatar blockade (2017), the Khashoggi murder (2018), and the oil price war (2020), MBS demonstrates a recurring template: **maximalist opening position**, willingness to absorb significant costs rather than appear weak, narrative control through state media, sacrifice of subordinates to deflect blame, eventual pragmatic adjustment never framed as retreat, and opportunistic use of crises for internal consolidation. The March 2020 arrest of Prince Ahmed and MbN coincided with COVID chaos and the oil crash, suggesting deliberate use of external distraction as cover for internal power moves.

## SECTION 16: Collective Memory of Humiliation and Grievance

Saudi national mythology is undergoing the most radical rewriting since the kingdom's founding. MBS is simultaneously pre-dating the state's origin to erase Wahhabi co-founding, embracing pre-Islamic heritage to create territorial nationalism, liberalizing social norms, and centralizing all political authority. The historical pattern—from the Ikhwan to Juhayman to the Sahwa—is the dynasty's willingness to instrumentalize and then discard ideological partners when they threaten Al Saud primacy.

### Diriyah 1818 and the exile narrative as foundational trauma

The destruction of Diriyah by Ibrahim Pasha's Ottoman-Egyptian forces in September 1818 functions as the primal wound of Saudi identity. After a six-month siege by approximately 30,000 troops, Imam Abdullah bin Saud surrendered and was sent to Istanbul for beheading. Over 250 members of the Saudi family were exiled. The city was razed and burned. MBS has transformed Diriyah's ruins into a multi-billion-dollar heritage destination (UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2010), positioning the destruction as an enduring grievance against foreign intervention—the emotional substrate for Saudi sovereignty sensitivity.

The 1891–1902 exile in Kuwait and Abdulaziz's dramatic recapture of Riyadh with approximately 40–60 men on January 15, 1902—seizing Masmak Fort in a nighttime raid—constitute the regime's heroic founding epic. MBS's establishment of **Founding Day (February 22)** in 2022, commemorating 1727 (when Muhammad bin Saud came to power in Diriyah), rather than the traditional 1744 date of the Saudi-Wahhabi pact, is a deliberate rewrite. As Hoover Institution scholar Cole Bunzel analyzed: this "practically writes Wahhabism out of the narrative altogether." The five official Founding Day symbols—flag, palm tree, falcon, horse, marketplace—contain **nothing religious**.

### 1929 and 1979 established the template for religious-state relations

The Ikhwan rebellion provides the template for the Saudi state's relationship with religious zealots. The Ikhwan (Wahhabi-Bedouin warriors settled in 120 agricultural colonies by 1929) served as Ibn Saud's conquest force but rebelled when he refused to continue expansion into British protectorates. At the **Battle of Sabilla (March 29–31, 1929)**—the last major battle where one side rode camels—Ibn Saud's British-equipped forces with machine guns and RAF air support crushed the Ikhwan. The remnants were incorporated into what became the **Saudi Arabian National Guard**. The message: the Al Saud always prioritize dynastic survival over ideological purity.

The November 20, 1979 Grand Mosque seizure directly echoed this pattern. **Juhayman al-Otaybi**—whose grandfather was one of the three Ikhwan rebel leaders at Sabilla—led approximately 300–400 militants in seizing Islam's holiest site. The two-week siege, resolved with French GIGN assistance and Pakistani military advisers, ended with 63 rebels publicly beheaded across eight cities. The aftermath was counterintuitive: rather than cracking down on religious extremism, King Khalid **empowered the religious establishment further**—banning cinemas, expanding religious police authority, intensifying gender segregation. This conservative compact lasted 37 years until MBS began dismantling it in 2016–2017. MBS himself frames the post-1979 period as an aberration, claiming Saudi Arabia was "moderate before 1979."

### Shia, Hejazi, and regional grievances persist beneath the surface

Saudi Shia (2–3 million, 10–15% of population) concentrated in the **Eastern Province**—which contains the majority of Saudi oil production—face systemic discrimination: restricted religious practice, exclusion from senior positions, school textbooks teaching Shia practices constitute "polytheism." The January 2, 2016 execution of Sheikh **Nimr al-Nimr** alongside 46 others triggered the severance of Saudi-Iranian diplomatic ties and protests across the Shia world. The 2017 demolition of the 400-year-old al-Musawara neighborhood in Awamiyah (Nimr's hometown) was perceived as collective punishment. The state frames all Shia dissent through the Iranian threat lens, but independent analysis consistently finds grievances are overwhelmingly domestic in origin.

**Hejazi identity** represents a latent alternative pole—cosmopolitan, pluralistic, historically independent, with Shafi'i/Maliki jurisprudence rather than Hanbali/Wahhabi. The Hashemite Kingdom of Hejaz was conquered in 1924–25; Ibn Saud co-opted merchant elites rather than crushing them. The "Najdification" of Saudi identity treats "Saudi origins" as synonymous with Najdi tribal identity, leaving Hejazis with "less solid socially accepted claims to being 'original' Saudis."

**Najran's Ismaili community** (700,000–1,000,000 Sulaimani Ismailis, a majority in the province) faces acute marginalization: only 1 of 35 provincial department heads is Ismaili; approximately 400 Ismaili officials were purged after a 2000 confrontation; Saudi Arabia's highest judge declared in 2006 that Ismailis "outwardly appear Islamic, but inwardly, they are infidels."

### MBS is replacing Wahhabism with civilizational nationalism

The identity rewrite operates across multiple dimensions. **Pre-Islamic heritage** has been rehabilitated: Hegra (Mada'in Saleh), where a fatwa by Grand Mufti Ibn Baz had previously warned against visiting, is now a major tourist destination. Six UNESCO World Heritage Sites—five predating Islam—have been opened free of charge. New textbooks convey a positive image of the pre-Islamic era with "seamless continuity" to Islamic and modern periods. Controversy erupted in 2023 when Saudi social media promoted ancient Arabian goddesses (Al-Lat, Manat, Al-Uzza) as "our ancient history."

The entertainment revolution—concerts, cinemas, mixed-gender events, the MDL Beast festival, Formula 1, the 2034 FIFA World Cup bid—serves as both economic diversification and identity transformation. **NEOM** ($500 billion mega-project) represents the most radical expression: a techno-futurist identity untethered from both Wahhabi and tribal heritage. Yet the **"Custodian of the Two Holy Mosques"** identity (formally adopted by King Fahd in 1986, replacing "His Majesty") remains the irreducible core of Saudi soft power and the foundation of global Islamic standing. This creates an inherent tension: MBS secularizes domestic life while depending on religious legitimacy for international status.

### The Iran threat narrative has been muted but remains available

Since 1979, Saudi Arabia framed Iran as an existential sectarian enemy—serving to delegitimize Shia dissent, justify security expenditure, and rally Sunni solidarity. The 2023 rapprochement has significantly muted this narrative domestically as part of broader de-Wahhabification. But the competitive relationship remains fundamentally intact: competition for regional status, influence over Iraq, Syria, and Yemen, and Islamic leadership persists. **Sectarian rhetoric is "much better understood as a manipulable political instrument than an absolute fate"**—available for reactivation if strategic circumstances demand it.

## SECTION 17: Structural Intersections and Cross-Facet Analysis

### The oil-diversification paradox drives everything

The Saudi system's fundamental structural tension is that Vision 2030's economic diversification depends on oil revenues that may be insufficient. The IMF estimates Saudi Arabia needs approximately **$90+ per barrel** to balance its budget, yet oil traded at $63–70 through much of 2025. The 2025 fiscal deficit reached **SR 276.6 billion ($73.8 billion), approximately 5.5% of GDP**—the largest in five years. Oil still constitutes ~55% of fiscal revenues and ~73% of exports. The PIF (~$925 billion) is redirecting from 30% to 18–20% international investments, concentrating risk domestically. The feedback loop is self-defeating: Vision 2030 requires sustained oil revenue to fund the transition away from oil dependence.

### Hyper-centralization enables speed but eliminates resilience

The absence of a deputy crown prince, the suppression of all independent institutions, and the concentration of decision-making in one person creates a system optimized for rapid reform but fragile under stress. No independent media, no civil society watchdogs, and no public policy debate means policy failures go unreported internally until they become crises. CSIS's Jon Alterman identifies the operational consequence: "For all that Western commentary sees Saudi reform efforts as a tightly orchestrated top-down process, in practice it is a chaotic jumble. Ministries undermine each other in pursuit of their own key performance indicators."

### The social contract renegotiation depends on job creation

The old contract (oil wealth → patronage → political quiescence) is being replaced by (entertainment + employment + national pride → quiescence). With **70% of the population under 35**, youth unemployment at approximately 15%, and skills gaps costing an estimated **SAR 62 billion ($16.5 billion) annually**, this contract requires continuous job creation. Female labor force participation surged to **36.3% (Q1 2025)** and overall Saudi unemployment hit a record low of **6.3%**, but displaced workers remain jobless for an average of 11.3 months. If Vision 2030 megaprojects stall, the youth bulge becomes destabilizing.

### Alliance hedging intersects with domestic political economy

AI and compute are becoming the new oil in U.S.-Gulf relations. Saudi Arabia offers abundant energy for data centers while requiring U.S. high-end semiconductor technology, creating new asymmetric interdependence. Engagement with Huawei and Chinese tech serves as leverage—Saudi Arabia's $100 billion AI fund reportedly "would divest from China tech if US asked." The BRICS ambiguity functions similarly: participation without commitment maximizes negotiating leverage with Washington.

### National identity rewriting carries legitimacy risks

MBS scores lowest on "Tribal and Domestic Support" in structural assessments, reflecting alienation of conservative Najdi constituencies and Sahwa-influenced populations. The new nationalism may struggle to bind the kingdom's regional identities (Hejaz, Eastern Province, Asir, Najran) absent the religious glue Wahhabism previously provided. Surveillance technology and repressive capacity suppress organized opposition, but a legitimacy deficit could surface during a succession crisis or economic downturn when patronage diminishes.

## SECTION 18: Falsifiable Analytical Judgments

**J1: MBS will formally accede to the throne before end of 2028.** King Salman's functional incapacity is increasingly evident; the transition is operationally complete. Capital Economics, Washington Institute, and multiple analysts converge on this assessment. (Confidence: High)

**J2: No significant elite challenge to MBS's rule will emerge through 2030.** All rival branches have been systematically excluded from senior positions. The Royal Guard and Presidency of State Security are under direct MBS control. The sole disruption pathway is an assassination or health crisis. (Confidence: High)

**J3: Saudi non-oil GDP will exceed 55% of total GDP by 2030 but fall short of the 65%+ targets implied by Vision 2030.** Non-oil GDP already reached ~52% by Q1 2025; the trajectory is positive but decelerating. Oil's fiscal dominance will persist. (Confidence: Medium-High)

**J4: NEOM will not achieve more than 500,000 residents by 2035.** The Line was scaled back 97% to 300,000 residents; only 2.4 km of the planned 170 km was completed before suspension in September 2025. The project is pivoting toward data center use. (Confidence: High)

## SECTION 20: Source Reliability, Common Errors, and Middle-Power Relationships

### Source reliability matrix

**Saudi state media** (Arab News, Saudi Gazette, Al Arabiya) is owned by members of the royal family through SRMG or MBC Group. Media Bias/Fact Check rates all three as low credibility for independent analysis. Useful exclusively for understanding official messaging and economic data releases; unreliable for human rights, political dynamics, or opposition coverage. **Asharq Al-Awsat** (London-based, SRMG-owned) is more sophisticated, providing serious commentary within Saudi-acceptable parameters, but maintains documented bias on Yemen, Qatar, and Iran.

**Al Jazeera** (Qatar-owned) provides critical Saudi coverage but with consistent anti-Saudi editorial slant, particularly on Yemen, OPEC, and regional rivalry. **Middle East Eye** (London, alleged but denied Qatari funding) rates as mostly factual with high credibility per Media Bias/Fact Check; important for scoops but adjust for editorial perspective. **Al Monitor** is the **highest reliability tier** among non-academic sources—read by the White House, NATO, and foreign ministries, winner of the International Press Institute Free Media Pioneer Award.

Among think tanks, **CSIS** (Jon Alterman), **International Crisis Group**, and **Chatham House** produce the highest-quality Saudi analysis. **AGSIW** provides excellent Gulf-focused work but was seed-funded by Saudi Arabia and the UAE, with corporate members including Aramco, Raytheon, and Lockheed Martin—adjust for pro-Gulf framing. **IMF Article IV consultations** are essential for authoritative economic data.

### Seven common analytical errors about Saudi Arabia

**1. Mirror-imaging liberalization expectations.** Western analysts assume economic modernization will produce political liberalization. The Saudi model explicitly rejects this linkage—political freedom scores have regressed even as economic freedom improved.

**2. Overestimating regime fragility.** The "bearish case against Saudi Arabia writes itself" but has been repeatedly premature—predicted during the 2014–16 oil crash, the 2019 Aramco attacks, and the pandemic. The dynasty has survived destruction (1818), exile (1891), rebellion (1929), seizure (1979), and insurgency (2003–09).

**3. Treating Vision 2030 as a coherent plan.** In practice it is "a chaotic jumble" where ministries undermine each other pursuing competing KPIs—one boosting foreign investment while another raises taxes and a third mandates Saudization.

**4. Conflating social reform with political intent.** Entertainment and women's driving serve economic diversification and population management, not rights-based principles. The 27-year sentence for Salma al-Shehab during the entertainment boom demonstrates the distinction.

**5. Overestimating U.S. leverage.** Saudi willingness to cooperate with Russia on OPEC+ against U.S. wishes and to use China as mediator with Iran demonstrates genuine multi-alignment, not rhetorical hedging.

**6. Underestimating MBS's domestic popularity.** Arab Barometer data confirms strong support among under-35 Saudis. Western media disproportionately amplifies elite opposition and diaspora critics.

**7. Applying binary U.S.-China framing.** Saudi Arabia seeks to maximize options across all relationships simultaneously. BRICS ambiguity is a feature of strategic design, not indecision.

### Key middle-power bilateral relationships

**Saudi-India**: India's third-largest crude supplier (33.35 MMT, 14.3% of Indian crude imports). Over 2.6 million Indian workers in Saudi Arabia. Modi's April 2025 state visit expanded the Strategic Partnership Council. India is one of eight designated Vision 2030 strategic partners.

**Saudi-Japan**: Bilateral trade approximately $39 billion. The Petro Rabigh joint venture (Aramco-Sumitomo) anchors the relationship. Saudi-Japan Vision 2030 bilateral framework signed 2017.

**Saudi-South Korea**: Middle East accounted for over 50% of Korean overseas construction contracts ($444 billion of $845 billion, 2002–2020). South Korea secured a strong position in the Saudi nuclear reactor program (bidding began late 2025).

**Saudi-UAE**: The most consequential and volatile bilateral relationship. December 2025 saw the first direct Saudi military action against UAE-linked assets in Yemen. OPEC+ friction intensifies as Saudi Arabia's fiscal breakeven (~$90/barrel) far exceeds the UAE's (~$50/barrel). Competing corporate headquarters strategies, AI hub ambitions, and divergent regional postures define a relationship trending toward "prolonged competitive divergence."

**Saudi-Germany**: Arms exports restricted since the post-Khashoggi 2018 embargo. Human rights tensions persist as a barrier to full normalization. Economic relationships continue in non-military sectors.

**Saudi-Brazil**: BRICS dynamics link the two, with Saudi maintaining ambiguity at BRICS summits. Agricultural trade is significant but the relationship lacks strategic depth.

## Conclusion: structural dynamics shaping Saudi Arabia's trajectory

Five dynamics will determine Saudi Arabia's path. First, the **oil-diversification race against time**: Vision 2030's success depends on sustained high oil prices to fund the transition away from oil, a paradox that generates persistent fiscal pressure. Second, **MBS's hyper-centralization** enables rapid reform but has created a system with no institutional resilience, no succession plan, and no feedback mechanisms for policy correction. Third, the **social contract renegotiation** depends on continuous job creation for a massive youth demographic—if megaprojects stall, the implicit bargain of entertainment and employment for political quiescence dissolves. Fourth, the **Saudi-UAE structural divergence** has replaced Gulf solidarity with competitive rivalry across OPEC+, corporate positioning, and regional influence. Fifth, the **great power hedging equilibrium** is sustainable only as long as U.S.-China competition remains below the threshold that forces a binary choice.

The most probable trajectory (assessed at ~45%) is **constrained muddling through**: oil prices fluctuating between $55–75, fiscal deficits persisting at 3–6% of GDP, megaprojects progressively scaled back, diversification real but slower than planned. MBS accedes to the throne but faces growing fiscal constraints. The managed-transformation scenario (~25%) requires sustained oil above $75 and regional de-escalation. The crisis scenarios—oil price shock (20%) or succession disruption (10%)—remain lower probability but carry catastrophic consequences given the system's dependence on a single decision-maker and a single commodity.
