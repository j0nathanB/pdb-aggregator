## 13. Civil society and dissent infrastructure

### The Singing Revolution bequeathed durable institutional DNA

Latvia's modern civil society traces directly to the 1986–91 national awakening. The **Environmental Protection Club (VAK)**, founded **28 February 1987**, channeled opposition to Soviet megaprojects — the Daugava hydroelectric dam and Riga metro — into the first mass political mobilization. Helsinki-86, founded the same year, organized the landmark **14 June 1987 flower-laying at the Freedom Monument**, widely considered the spark of the Third Awakening. The **Latvian Popular Front (LTF)**, established summer 1988 with **250,000 members**, provided the organizational architecture for independence. The **Baltic Way** of 23 August 1989 — two million people forming a 600 km human chain — remains the defining image.

The **Barricades of January 1991** cemented civil society's role in statebuilding. After Soviet OMON attacked the Lithuanian TV tower on 13 January, LTF leader **Dainis Īvāns** broadcast a 4:45 AM radio call to defend strategic buildings. Up to **700,000 people** (one-third of the population) participated. Six people died, including filmmaker Andris Slapiņš. Over **32,000** Barricade Defenders received commemorative medals. The 35th anniversary in January 2026 featured bonfire-lighting at the Saeima and Īvāns himself speaking at events, demonstrating the living continuity between independence-era mobilization and current democratic identity.

### Current landscape: vibrant journalism, underfunded NGOs

Latvia's civil society in 2026 features **25,600 registered NGOs**, though only roughly half are active and most have no employees. The sector's financial activity is declining, creating a structural vulnerability beneath surface-level organizational density.

The think tank ecosystem punches above its weight. **Centre for Public Policy PROVIDUS** (est. 2002) leads on governance and anti-corruption research, funded by the German Embassy, European Commission, OECD, and Open Society Institute. The **Latvian Institute of International Affairs (LIIA)**, directed by **Dr. Karlis Bukovskis** since 2022, publishes the annual *Foreign and Security Policy Yearbook* and hosts the Riga Dialogue and Riga Security Forum. LIIA receives project funding from NED, Freedom House, the German Marshall Fund, and Konrad Adenauer Foundation. The **NATO Strategic Communications Centre of Excellence**, headquartered in Riga, provides an additional layer of analytical capacity.

Investigative journalism is Latvia's civil society crown jewel. **Re:Baltica** (founded 2011 by Inga Spriņģe and Kristīna Rizga, managed by Sanita Jemberga) operates as a cross-Baltic investigative hub and IFCN-verified fact-checker through **Re:Check**. Its investigations into Russian sabotage packages routed through Riga and sanctioned oligarch Pyotr Aven's visitors to Latvia have had direct policy impact. **Ir magazine** provides sustained political analysis. The public broadcaster **LSM**'s "De Facto" program serves as the primary broadcast investigative vehicle. Re:Baltica's 2025 innovation — providing content to regional media to address news deserts ahead of June 2025 municipal elections — signals awareness of the information-gap threat in a country where media is heavily Riga-concentrated.

Labor organization remains weak. The **Free Trade Union Confederation (LBAS)** has approximately **100,000 members** (down from 275,000 in 1995), organized across 20 industry affiliates. The largest, **LIZDA** (education), accounts for 30,220 members. Only ~7% of members are under 35. The **Employers' Confederation (LDDK)**, led by president **Andris Bite**, represents firms employing 44% of the workforce. Both participate in the **National Tripartite Cooperation Council (NTSP)**, but labor's political leverage is modest.

### Funding gap and foreign support architecture

A critical funding gap emerged in 2024–2025 when the **Active Citizens Fund** (EEA/Norway Grants) ended. The new €9.88 million Civil Society Support Fund under the 2021–2028 EEA/Norway Grants agreement (signed 12 September 2024) will not distribute until 2025 at earliest. The state **National NGO Fund** provides only ~€2.5 million annually, and Civic Alliance–Latvia has called for at least €4.1 million in additional state funding.

German political foundations maintain significant presence. **Konrad Adenauer Foundation** has operated a Baltic regional office from Riga since 1993 and co-organizes the annual Riga Conference. **Friedrich Ebert Foundation** co-publishes LIIA's yearbook. **Heinrich Böll Foundation** supports Riga Conference events. The **Open Society Foundations** fund PROVIDUS and Re:Baltica projects. The **Sigrid Rausing Trust** (UK) funds Re:Baltica. NED funding is primarily indirect, channeled through think tanks rather than direct democracy programming — appropriate given Latvia's consolidated democratic status. The Trump administration's 2025 USAID cuts have broader regional implications but affect Latvia less directly than non-EU states.

Latvia **has not adopted a "foreign agent" law**. Discussions on an NGO transparency register are ongoing (DELNA/Transparency International Latvia monitors this), but parliament has shown awareness of how such laws have been misused in Russia and Georgia. At the EU level, the European Commission's proposed Directive on transparency of foreign influence initially generated CSO opposition.

### Religious institutions: the Orthodox autocephaly revolution

The most consequential church-state development since independence was the **8 September 2022** Saeima vote mandating full autocephaly for the **Latvian Orthodox Church (LOC)**, severing ties with the Moscow Patriarchate. The law took effect **1 January 2023**. Metropolitan **Aleksandrs (Kudryashov)**, head since 1990, accepted the changes and amended the LOC charter, though he reportedly still commemorates Patriarch Kirill during liturgy. The Latvian government published records showing Aleksandrs was recruited as a KGB agent in the 1980s. The law requires the LOC to notify the President's Chancellery of hierarchical appointments — implying national security vetting. Moscow called it "false autocephaly" and "blatant state interference" but has no operational lever to reverse it.

The **Evangelical Lutheran Church of Latvia (LELB)**, with approximately **250,000 members**, is the largest Protestant denomination. Theologically conservative (affiliated with LCMS/Missouri Synod rather than LWF mainstream), the LELB positions itself as a moral arbiter, particularly on social issues. Its conservative stance influenced the decades-long delay in passing civil unions (finally enacted 2023). The **Catholic Church** is strongest in Latgale but less politically prominent than the Lutheran and Orthodox institutions.

### Russian-speaking civil society: accelerating marginalization

The post-2022 period has dramatically reshaped the Russian-speaking community's civic space. **Moscow House** in Riga — headquarters of Russia's "compatriot policy" — was transferred to state ownership by Saeima vote on 11 January 2024. The education transition to Latvian-only instruction proceeded on schedule: preschool from September 2023, general education from September 2025, with Russian as a second foreign language ending from 2026. From 2026, public media funding is allocated exclusively to Latvian and "languages belonging to the European cultural space," effectively ending Russian-language public broadcasting.

Approximately **180,000 residents** held non-citizen status as of January 2024 (65.69% ethnic Russian). New immigration rules require Russian citizens to secure EU long-term residence, demonstrate A2 Latvian proficiency, and pass security screening by June 2025. Of ~25,000 Russian citizens affected, ~16,000 secured permanent residence, ~2,600 left voluntarily, and **841 received formal deportation notices** in October 2025. A recent study found pervasive "apathy, disbelief that their voice matters" among Russian-speakers. A protest against school language transition in August 2024 drew only 30 people — a stark marker of political disengagement.

### Istanbul Convention protests mark civil society's largest mobilization since independence

The **October 2025 Istanbul Convention crisis** produced the largest protest action in Latvia in years. When the Saeima voted **56–32** on 31 October to withdraw from the Convention (ratified only in November 2023), over **10,000 people** gathered in Riga and other cities — with solidarity protests in the US and Australia. CSOs working on violence prevention faced intense disinformation campaigns. President Rinkēvičs exercised his suspensive veto on 3 November, and parliament agreed to postpone reconsideration until after the October 2026 elections. This mobilization demonstrated that Latvia's civil society retains significant rapid-response capacity on progressive social issues, even as conservative backlash on "gender ideology" intensifies.

**Jaunsardze** (Young Guard), the state-funded military youth cadet body under the Ministry of Defence, has grown dramatically post-2022. Open to ages 10–21, it has trained an estimated **26,000 young people** by 2025, targeting 34,000. Activities now include drone (UAV) operator training. The program has signed cooperation agreements with **UK Army Cadet Forces** and **Poland's Central Military Recruitment Center**. On the progressive end, the youth organization **"Protests"** (affiliated with the Federation of Young European Greens) organizes climate marches, LGBTQ+ rights actions, and housing affordability protests.

## 14. Alliance genealogy and patron-client relationships

### From failed interwar neutrality to NATO's third-highest spender

Latvia's alliance posture is entirely explicable through its 20th-century experience. The interwar republic's attempts at neutrality between Germany and the Soviet Union ended catastrophically with the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact's secret protocol assigning Latvia to Moscow's sphere. The lesson — that small-state neutrality invites predation — is the foundational axiom of post-1991 foreign policy. Latvia joined NATO's Partnership for Peace in 1994, received a Membership Action Plan in 1999, and acceded on **29 March 2004**.

The defense spending trajectory tells the strategic story. From **0.94% GDP in 2014** (with only 7.6% on equipment), Latvia climbed to the NATO 2% minimum by 2018 and then accelerated dramatically after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine: **3.3% GDP (€1.36 billion)** in 2024, **3.73–3.80% GDP (€1.59 billion)** in 2025, and a Saeima-approved **4.91% GDP (€2.16 billion)** for 2026. This makes Latvia the **third-highest NATO spender** by GDP share, behind only Poland (4.48%) and Lithuania (4.0%). Equipment procurement has risen from 7.6% to **37%** of the defense budget. The €2.16 billion 2026 budget allocates 53% (€1.14 billion) to capability enhancement, with major line items including **€303 million** for fighting vehicles, **€200 million** for air defense, **€181 million** for munitions, and **€159 million** for indirect fire systems.

### NATO's forward posture: from tripwire to brigade

The NATO force presence in Latvia has undergone qualitative transformation. What began as an **Enhanced Forward Presence battlegroup** (2017) under Canadian framework nation leadership became the **Multinational Brigade Latvia (MNB-LVA)**, formally established **3 July 2024** with transfer of authority on **17 October 2024**. The brigade comprises approximately **3,000 personnel from 14 NATO nations**: Albania, Canada, Czechia, Denmark, Iceland, Italy, Latvia, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Poland, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, and Sweden. **Exercise RESOLUTE WARRIOR 25** (1–10 November 2025) tested the brigade's first deployment beyond Camp Ādaži, with ~2,400 troops operating across eastern Latvia with live-fire demonstrations.

Major procurement transforms Latvia's capability profile. The **$179.8 million HIMARS contract** (signed 20 December 2023) delivers six launchers plus **ATACMS tactical ballistic missiles** (300 km range), with deliveries beginning 2027. Coordinated with Estonia (six HIMARS, delivered April 2025) and Lithuania (eight HIMARS, ~$495 million), this creates approximately **20 HIMARS launchers across the Baltics** under a joint capability agreement signed in Riga, January 2024. The **IRIS-T SLM** air defense system (~€600 million, from Germany's Diehl Defence, delivery 2026) was jointly procured with Estonia. The **Naval Strike Missile** coastal defense ($105 million, Raytheon/Kongsberg, delivery 2026–2027), **84 ASCOD infantry fighting vehicles** (two batches of 42, €387 million each, with 30% local content), and **six Bayraktar TB2 drones** round out the transformation.

### The United States: aligned messaging, underlying anxiety

Latvia has navigated the Trump 2.0 era with deliberate strategic messaging. On **24 January 2025**, Secretary Rubio's first round of allied consultations included FM **Baiba Braže**, and both sides agreed to expand cooperation. Latvia has adopted Trump's "peace through strength" language and aligned with US energy priorities — over one-third of gas imports now come from US LNG sources. Braže stated in February 2025: "We are interested in strengthening energy cooperation with our strategic partner, the United States, including increasing LNG imports."

The **Baltic Security Initiative** provides the institutional backbone. US Congress approved **$228 million** for Baltic defense capabilities in 2024, and the BSI was enshrined in the NDAA in December 2025 with bipartisan support (312 of 435 House members). Combined FY2023–2024 DoD security assistance to the three Baltic states exceeded **$450 million**. Active US Foreign Military Sales cases with Latvia total approximately **$339 million**. Latvia receives ~$3.5 million annually in International Military Education and Training (IMET), with 150 Baltic students at US military academies.

The underlying anxiety is real but managed. Trump's ambiguous stance on Article 5 and temporary suspension of Ukraine military aid generated concern. Latvia hedges by accelerating European defense cooperation while maintaining maximum alignment with Washington to preserve the US commitment. No direct bilateral friction has been reported.

### Canada: the indispensable framework partner

**Canada's commitment to Latvia is its largest overseas military mission** and arguably the most consequential component of Canadian defense policy outside North America. Approximately **2,000 Canadian troops** serve under Operation REASSURANCE, with a target of **2,200 by 2026**. PM **Mark Carney** visited Latvia on **26 August 2025**, renewing Operation REASSURANCE for three years and building on PM Trudeau's **$2.6 billion commitment** (July 2023). The **$230 million CAD infrastructure investment** includes a **$58 million** 2nd Line Maintenance Facility at Ādaži (17,000 sq m, groundbreaking 31 October 2025) — Canada's largest infrastructure project abroad in decades. Equipment deployed includes **15 Leopard 2A4M tanks**, Griffon and Chinook helicopters, and Spike anti-armor missiles. In December 2025, reports emerged that the Canadian Armed Forces is studying establishing a **permanent base in Latvia**.

### The United Kingdom: drone coalition and JEF

The Latvia-UK relationship centers on two frameworks. The **International Drone Coalition**, co-led by Latvia and the UK, was formally established **14 February 2024** during a Ramstein meeting. It has grown to **18 member states**, delivered **€1.8 billion** in drone support to Ukraine in 2024, and committed **€2.75 billion** for 2025. Latvia contributed €20 million for drone purchases plus €10 million for joint Latvian-Ukrainian industry projects. The **Drone Summit 2025** in Riga (28 May) drew 1,000+ participants and ~100 companies. Latvia opened an **Autonomous Systems Competence Center** on 3 September 2025. This co-leadership role gives Latvia outsized alliance influence for a country of 1.8 million.

Latvia is a founding member of the UK-led **Joint Expeditionary Force (JEF)** (2014 Letter of Intent), a 10-nation framework providing sub-Article 5 response options. JEF's **Baltic Sentry** enhanced vigilance activity, launched January 2025 after suspected sabotage of the Estlink 2 cable, demonstrates the framework's operational relevance.

### EU dependency and Nordic-Baltic convergence

Latvia receives **€4.6 billion in EU Cohesion Policy funding** for 2021–2027 (€5.4 billion with national co-financing), representing roughly **50% of total public investment**. Classified as a "less developed" region (GDP per capita below 75% EU average), Latvia qualifies for maximum co-financing rates up to 85%. This creates a deep structural dependency on EU transfers that shapes Latvia's Council voting behavior: consistently pro-Atlanticist, pro-Ukraine, pro-sanctions, and supportive of Ukraine's EU membership path.

The **Nordic-Baltic Eight (NB8)** has transformed from a talk-shop into a credible security actor. With Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) joining NATO, all eight members are now allies. The Baltic Sea is colloquially a "NATO lake." In November 2025, NB8 countries contributed a joint **~$500 million** to the Prioritized Ukraine Requirements List. The **NB8+Weimar Triangle** format (adding Poland, Germany, France) held its first FM-level meeting in Denmark in 2025, signaling the format's expanding ambition.

### Zero hedging: firm Western commitment without alternatives

Latvia exhibits **zero multi-vector hedging** toward non-Western powers. It withdrew from China's 16+1 format on **11 August 2022** alongside Estonia, citing incompatibility with strategic objectives. Latvia has no relationship with BRICS or SCO. The only Global South outreach signal was NB8 foreign ministers' joint visit to New Delhi in February 2024. Latvia's strategy is entirely within the Western alliance framework, but it is **diversifying within the West** — deepening European, Nordic, and Canadian ties as insurance against potential US disengagement while maintaining maximum alignment with Washington. This is classic small-state bandwagoning with intra-alliance diversification, not geopolitical hedging.

The most exposed US dependency channels, in order: (1) military hardware supply chain (HIMARS, Black Hawks, ATACMS, $339 million in active FMS cases); (2) intelligence and situational awareness capabilities; (3) extended deterrence and Article 5 psychological credibility; (4) BSI congressional funding ($228 million annually); and (5) the nuclear umbrella, for which no European replacement exists.

## 15. Constitutional crises and regime transition patterns

### Five regimes in a century: the pattern of external imposition and internal fragility

Latvia's regime history is defined by the interplay of external great-power predation and internal institutional weakness. The **1918 republic** suffered extreme parliamentary fragmentation (22–24 parties in a 100-seat Saeima), producing 13 governments and nine prime ministers in 12 years. This instability enabled PM **Kārlis Ulmanis** to stage a bloodless coup on **15–16 May 1934**, dissolving the Saeima, banning all political parties (including his own), and merging the presidency with the premiership in 1936. The authoritarian regime was relatively benign by contemporary European standards, focused on "Latvianizing" the economy through state corporations.

External imposition then erased sovereignty entirely. The **Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact** (23 August 1939) assigned Latvia to the Soviet sphere. Soviet occupation began 17 June 1940, followed by sham elections and forced incorporation by 5 August 1940. The "Year of Terror" saw ~35,000 deportees. Nazi occupation (1941–44) brought the Holocaust and Reichskommissariat administration. The second Soviet occupation (1944–91) imposed collectivization, Russification, and mass immigration that reduced ethnic Latvians to barely a majority.

The independence restoration of **4 May 1990** (transitional declaration) and **21 August 1991** (full restoration) was constitutionally framed as continuity, not creation. The 1922 Satversme was reaffirmed and fully reinstated for the June 1993 elections, with citizenship restricted to pre-1940 citizens and descendants.

### Seventeen prime ministers in 33 years: coalition instability as structural feature

Post-1991 Latvia has had **17 prime ministers** from 1990 to the present, with average government duration of roughly 1.5–2 years. No party has ever won more than ~33% of the vote. Coalitions typically comprise 3–4 parties spanning centre-right to right. Key figures include **Valdis Dombrovskis** (longest tenure, ~5 years, 2009–2014), **Krišjānis Kariņš** (~4.5 years across two cabinets, 2019–2023), and current PM **Evika Siliņa** (September 2023–present). Typical collapse triggers include budget disagreements, corruption scandals, personality clashes, and oligarch influence. The ethnic Russian-majority party Harmony was systematically excluded from government throughout its period as the largest party (2011–2018) — an informal cordon sanitaire.

### The 2011 dissolution: Latvia's anti-oligarch watershed

The only lawful Saeima dissolution in Latvian history occurred on **28 May 2011** when President **Valdis Zatlers** invoked Articles 48–50 after parliament voted to protect corruption-suspected MP **Ainārs Šlesers** from immunity-lifting. Zatlers declared parliament "feels comfortable in its atmosphere of lies." Under the constitution, dissolution required referendum confirmation — with the president automatically removed if voters rejected it. The Saeima retaliated by electing ZZS-linked **Andris Bērziņš** as Zatlers's replacement on 2 June. In the **23 July referendum**, **94.3%** voted for dissolution (turnout ~44%). The September 2011 snap elections eliminated the oligarch parties of Šlesers and Šķēle from parliament entirely, producing landmark anti-corruption reforms including public party financing and stricter donation rules.

The irony of 2026 politics is that **Šlesers has returned** via his **Latvia First (LPV)** party, founded August 2021, now polling at **~14.8%** — second place. His Christian-fundamentalist, EU-skeptic, right-wing populist positioning represents the resilience of informal power networks despite institutional reform.

### The Siliņa government: ideologically heterogeneous, politically fragile

PM **Evika Siliņa** formed Latvia's 42nd cabinet on **15 September 2023**, winning confidence 53–39 with an unusual coalition: **New Unity (JV)** (centre-right, PM + 7 ministries), **ZZS** (agrarian/centre-right, 4 ministries + Speaker Daiga Mieriņa), and **The Progressives** (centre-left, 3 ministries). Key ministers include Defense Minister **Andris Sprūds**, FM **Baiba Braže** (who replaced Kariņš after his March 2024 resignation over a charter-flight scandal), and Finance Minister **Arvils Ašeradens**.

The **Istanbul Convention crisis** of October 2025 exposed the coalition's central fissure. ZZS MPs broke ranks to vote **with the opposition** for Convention withdrawal, producing the **56–32** vote of 31 October that would have made Latvia the first EU country to withdraw. President Rinkēvičs's suspensive veto and parliament's agreement to postpone until after the 2026 elections saved the coalition, which survived to pass the 2026 budget on 4 December (52–42). Despite predictions at formation that the government was unlikely to survive the parliamentary term, the coalition has endured approximately 2.5 years — relatively long by Latvian standards.

### October 2026 election: seven parties, knife-edge arithmetic

The **3 October 2026** election projects to return seven parties above the 5% threshold. Based on SKDS polling data (November 2025 trend):

- **New Unity (JV)**: ~16.5% (~22 seats) — governing, centre-right
- **Latvia First (LPV)**: ~14.8% (~20 seats) — Šlesers's right-wing populist vehicle
- **Progressives**: ~11.8% (~16 seats) — governing, centre-left
- **National Alliance**: ~11.1% (~15 seats) — right/nationalist, opposition
- **ZZS**: ~8.1% (~11 seats) — governing, agrarian
- **Sovereign Power (SV)**: ~6.8% (~9 seats) — right-populist, EU-skeptic
- **For Stability! (S!)**: ~5.2% (~7 seats) — replacing Harmony as main Russian-speaker party

The current JV+ZZS+Progressives coalition would hold ~49 seats — below the 51-seat majority, requiring a fourth partner. The opposition bloc (LPV+NA+S!+SV) would hold ~51 seats but is ideologically incoherent. A JV+LPV+NA combination (57 seats) is arithmetically viable but would require JV to partner with the very oligarch interests the 2011 dissolution targeted. Campaign issues center on defense spending, the Istanbul Convention, economic stagnation, and corruption.

### Democratic consolidation: solid but not impregnable

Latvia's democratic indicators are strong but reveal specific weaknesses. **Freedom House** scored Latvia **89/100** (Free) in 2025, up from 88 in 2024 partly due to the civil unions law taking effect. The **Corruption Perceptions Index** score of **59/100** (rank 38th) is the worst among the Baltic states (Estonia: 76, Lithuania: 63) and fell short of Latvia's own target of 64. The EU Rule of Law Report flagged two persistent concerns: inadequate safeguards against political influence in **Supreme Court judge appointments** (no progress in 2024 or 2025) and the absence of a long-term anti-corruption planning document (not expected until 2028). The Judicial Academy became operational 1 January 2025, and the specialized Economic Court resolves cases in 6.9 months versus 30 months in the general judiciary. The **Constitutional Court** (Satversmes tiesa), led by President **Irēna Kucina** since November 2024, has adjudicated over 574 cases and upheld the education language transition in July 2024.

### Presidential power: Rinkēvičs as activist diplomat-president

President **Edgars Rinkēvičs** (sworn in 8 July 2023) brings unprecedented foreign-policy depth to a constitutionally constrained office. As Latvia's longest-serving Foreign Minister (2011–2023) and a US National Defense University graduate, he has actively used his powers: nominating PM Siliņa, exercising his suspensive veto on the Istanbul Convention withdrawal, and chairing a reinvigorated **National Security Council**. His most consequential achievement is securing Latvia's **election to the UN Security Council** in June 2025 with 178 of 188 votes — the country's first-ever UNSC seat, with a presidency scheduled for **November 2026** (coinciding with the election campaign). Approval ratings have fluctuated: +45 net in February 2025, declining 7.7 points by June 2025, with the lowest support in Latgale (under 40%).

The NSC, chaired by the president and including the Speaker, PM, foreign, defense, and interior ministers, plus parliamentary committee chairs, makes **advisory/recommendatory decisions only** — except for binding recommendations on the Constitution Protection Bureau director. A new **National Crisis Management Center** under direct PM authority, approved early 2025, represents significant centralization of crisis coordination.

## 16. Collective memory, grievance, and sacred narratives

### The occupation thesis as constitutional bedrock

Latvia's foundational political narrative is the **State Continuity Doctrine**: the Republic of Latvia proclaimed on 18 November 1918 never ceased to exist as a legal entity during the Soviet (1940–41, 1944–91) and Nazi (1941–44) occupations. The 1990 declaration was explicitly a "restoration," not a creation, of statehood. International legitimacy rests on the **Welles Declaration** (23 July 1940), by which the US refused to recognize Soviet annexation, and on consistent Western maintenance of Baltic diplomatic missions throughout the occupation period. The **European Court of Human Rights** upheld the doctrine in **Savickis and Others v. Latvia** (Grand Chamber, June 2022) and accepted Latvia's "constitutional identity" argument as justification for differential treatment of non-citizens in 2023.

The doctrine's policy consequences are far-reaching. Only pre-1940 citizens and descendants received automatic citizenship, creating the **non-citizen (nepilsoņi)** status that applied to ~300,000 people at independence. Latvia's government concluded in 2016 that the Soviet occupation inflicted **€185 billion** in economic damages. The **2014 Constitutional Preamble** enshrines the narrative in constitutional text: "The people of Latvia did not recognise the occupation regimes, resisted them and regained their freedom."

The **Museum of the Occupation of Latvia** (founded 1993, privately funded, **75,000+ items** including 2,300+ video testimonials) serves as the primary institutional transmission mechanism. Its location at Riflemen Square in the former Soviet-era Museum of the Red Latvian Riflemen is itself a spatial reclamation. The museum covers the Holocaust, though some observers note it may underemphasize the extent of Latvian participation in killings. Leading scholar on the doctrine is **Dr. Ineta Ziemele**, former Constitutional Court president and former ECHR judge.

### Deportation memory as living politics

The **June 14, 1941** deportation removed **15,443 Latvians** (~0.80% of population) in a single night, with families separated — men to Gulag camps, women and children to Siberian settlements. Mortality rates were extreme, estimated at ~60%. The **March 25, 1949** Operation Priboi was larger: approximately **42,000 people** deported, over 70% women or children under 16, with the explicit purpose of breaking Forest Brothers resistance and forcing collectivization (93% of farms collectivized by year's end). Latvia lost approximately **20% of its population** during WWII from combined Soviet and Nazi occupations.

Both dates are observed as **Commemoration Day for the Victims of Communist Genocide** — the terminological choice "communist genocide" deliberately maps onto the Double Genocide framework. Processions from the Occupation Museum to the Freedom Monument are attended by the President, Speaker, and PM, with black-ribboned Latvian flags. In 2021 (80th anniversary of the first deportation), over **15,000 names** of deportees were read aloud across all 119 municipalities. The first public commemoration on 14 June 1987, organized by Helsinki-86, is considered a foundational spark of the national awakening — connecting deportation memory directly to independence.

### Holocaust memory: recognition under persistent tension

The Holocaust destroyed approximately **70,000** of Latvia's ~93,000 pre-war Jewish population — over 90% of Latvian Jewry. The **Rumbula massacre** (30 November and 8 December 1941) killed approximately **25,000 Jews** — the second-largest two-day Holocaust atrocity after Babi Yar. It was carried out by Einsatzgruppe A under Friedrich Jeckeln, with direct assistance from the **Arajs Kommando** — a Latvian SD auxiliary unit of 300–500 members that directly killed **at least 26,000 people**, participated in the burning of Riga's Great Choral Synagogue (4 July 1941), and later conducted anti-partisan operations in Belarus.

Latvia's Holocaust recognition architecture is institutionally robust but emotionally shallow. The **Commission of the Historians of Latvia** (established 1998 under presidential authority) published **27+ volumes** across sub-commissions covering both Soviet and Nazi-era crimes. The Rumbula memorial (2002), Bikernieki memorial (2001), **239 memorial plaques** at mass killing sites, recognition of over **400 Righteous Among the Nations** (including Žanis Lipke, whose memorial museum operates in Riga), and observance of international remembrance days all demonstrate formal institutional commitment. However, as Ilya Lensky, director of the Museum "Jews in Latvia," observed: "We recognize collaboration" because "our older brothers in Europe, United States are watching us" — acknowledging the EU/NATO accession conditionality that drove recognition.

The core analytical tension: a far greater proportion of Latvia's population has personal or familial experience with Soviet deportations than with the Holocaust, creating an asymmetry in public empathy. More than **40** Soviet-convicted Arajs Kommando members were **rehabilitated by Latvian courts** after independence despite evidence, a fact that undermines recognition credibility. A 2014 musical celebrating pre-war aviator **Herberts Cukurs** — who was the Arajs Kommando deputy commander assassinated by Mossad in 1965 — illustrated how Latvian public culture can compartmentalize collaboration history.

### March 16: declining but unresolved

The **Latvian Legion** (15th and 19th Waffen Grenadier Divisions of the SS) comprised approximately **87,550 men** by July 1944, of whom **80–85% were conscripts**. The symbolic date — 16 March — commemorates the 1944 Battle of Velikaya River, the only time both divisions fought together under a Latvian commander. The commemoration was briefly designated an official remembrance day by the Saeima in 1998, revoked in 2000 under international pressure. The current status: March 16 is **not an official day**, senior officials do not attend in official capacity, but private commemoration continues. The 2025 procession drew approximately **250 participants** — significantly declined from peaks. The **1950 US Displaced Persons Commission ruling** that "Baltic Waffen SS Units are to be considered as separate and distinct in purpose, ideology, activities and qualifications for membership from the German SS" provides the legal backstop, but the transfer of Arajs Kommando members into the Legion in 1943 creates an indelible historical link between killing units and the conscript force.

### Victory Monument demolition: the cathartic rupture of 2022

The **79-metre Victory Monument** (erected 1985, architect Alexandr Bugaev) — informally called "Moscow's Finger" or *okupeklis* (occupation + monument) — served as the primary symbolic flashpoint between ethnic communities. By 2005, **~260,000 people** gathered there for May 9 Victory Day celebrations. The demolition timeline accelerated after Russia's February 2022 invasion: Saeima suspended the Latvia-Russia memorial preservation agreement on 12 May (68–18), Riga City Council voted to dismantle on 13 May, and the Saeima passed the **Law on Prohibition and Dismantling of Objects Glorifying Soviet and Nazi Regimes** on 16 June with a 15 November deadline.

On **25 August 2022 at 16:42**, the obelisk was toppled without explosives. A June 2022 survey showed 49% supported demolition (**72% of ethnic Latvians vs. 9% of ethnic Russians**); 25% opposed (76% of ethnic Russians vs. 10% of ethnic Latvians). Russia's Investigative Committee launched a criminal case; FM Rinkēvičs quipped that Russia would receive "a dead donkey's ears, not compensation." The UN Human Rights Committee's request to halt removal was ignored. Across Latvia, **more than 100 Soviet monuments** were ultimately dismantled by the November deadline. The Constitutional Court upheld the law's constitutionality in **December 2023**, including the requirement that municipalities partially finance demolition (which amounted to only 0.018% of Daugavpils's budget).

### Language as existential marker and the 2012 trauma

The **18 February 2012 language referendum** — triggered by a pro-Russian NGO collecting 187,378 signatures for Russian as a second state language — produced one of Latvia's highest-ever turnouts (**71.12%**) and a decisive rejection: **74.80% voted no** (821,722 votes). The regional breakdown revealed the ethnic geography: Kurzeme rejected 91.53%, while Latgale — the only region with a Russian-speaker majority — voted 55.80% in favor. President Bērziņš had threatened to resign if the measure passed. The political trauma drove the **2014 Constitutional Preamble** amendment enshrining Latvian as the sole official language. The ECtHR upheld subsequent Latvian-language education reforms in **Valiullina v. Latvia** (November 2023), accepting the constitutional identity argument.

### The Double Genocide controversy and Western misreadings

The "Double Genocide" framework — asserting moral and legal equivalence between Nazi and Soviet crimes — is institutionally embedded in Latvia's commemorative architecture through the "Communist Genocide" terminology for deportations, the Commission of Historians' dual mandate, and Latvia's support for the **Prague Declaration** (June 2008) and **August 23** as the European Day of Remembrance for Victims of Stalinism and Nazism. Critics led by **Dovid Katz** (Defending History) argue this constitutes "Holocaust obfuscation without denying a single death," while **Efraim Zuroff** (Simon Wiesenthal Center) calls the Prague Declaration "sinister." The **Seventy Years Declaration** (2012) was issued as a counter-document. The core analytical criticism: while Soviet deportations were genuine atrocities targeting political/social classes, the Baltic peoples survived as nations — they were not subjected to an annihilation campaign. The Holocaust, by contrast, sought total racial extermination.

Western analytical misreadings typically apply post-colonial or minority-rights frameworks that fail to account for Latvia's occupation experience. The Latvian perspective frames language policy, citizenship restrictions, and monument removal not as ethnic discrimination but as decolonization — restoring a state that was colonized through population transfer and cultural suppression. The **Quincy Institute** (2025) characterized the Baltics' "maximalist anti-Russia stance" as risking "unintended escalation," but this reading underweights the degree to which occupation memory constitutes a genuine, historically grounded threat perception rather than mere hawkishness.

### How memory drives alliance behavior

The connection between collective memory and foreign policy is direct and acknowledged. Latvia's national security adviser under PM Siliņa, **Airis Rikveilis**, stated: "This is not going to be 1940." Latvia's embassy in Berlin organized a February 2026 event titled "Latvia's Bitter Experience of Russian Occupation: Stories from the Past that Warn Europe Today" — explicitly deploying memory as a diplomatic instrument. The UNSC seat is used as a platform for the occupation narrative's contemporary implications. European Parliament President Roberta Metsola acknowledged it took "way too long" for others to listen to the Baltics about the Russian threat. Latvia's 4.91% GDP defense spending, the Baltic Defence Line fortifications, border fencing, and urban shelter signage all reflect a society whose strategic culture is shaped not by abstract threat assessment but by lived intergenerational trauma.

## 17. Current political and security developments (2024–2026)

### Economic recovery insufficient to ease electoral pressure

Latvia's economy stagnated in 2024 (~-0.3% initial estimate, later revised upward) before recovering to **2.5% YoY growth** in Q3 2025 and **2.9%** in Q4. Full-year 2025 growth settled at approximately **1.7%** (Bank of Latvia, revised down from 2.6%). The 2026 forecast ranges from 1.7% (European Commission) to 2.8% (Bank of Latvia), with the government budgeting 2.1%. Inflation fell sharply to 1.4% in 2024, rebounded to ~3.5% in 2025 on food and heating tariffs, and moderated to **2.9% HICP** by January 2026. Unemployment fell to **6.7%** in Q4 2025, with tight labor market conditions driving ~5.8% nominal wage growth. The fiscal position is strained: the budget deficit reaches **3.3% GDP** in 2026 due to defense spending, with public debt rising from 46.6% (2024) toward ~54.6% by 2027. The EU national escape clause was activated to permit fiscal rules exceedance for defense.

### Espionage surge and the Ždanoka case

Latvia has experienced an **espionage surge** with 22 arrests since 2023, including three in early 2025. The most prominent case involves **Tatjana Ždanoka**, former Latvian MEP (2004–2024), whom leaked emails published by The Insider and Re:Baltica in January 2024 revealed as an FSB agent since at least 2004–2005, with two known handlers: Dmitry Gladey (2005–2013) and Sergei Beltyukov (2013–2017). The State Security Service (VDD) opened criminal proceedings in February 2024. The European Parliament imposed sanctions (€1,750) and limited her activities. As of early 2026, the investigation remains complicated by a pending Belgian court ruling on the legality of a search at Ždanoka's Brussels residence. She denies all allegations.

Other espionage cases include a **72-year-old Russian citizen** sentenced to 8 years for gathering intelligence on critical infrastructure near Riga Airport (with explosives found), an Estonian and Ukrainian citizen detained for infrastructure monitoring, and a Latvian man arrested in September 2025 for sharing NATO troop locations and security protocols with Russian contacts via Telegram. VDD identifies Russia as the greatest security threat and notes increasing use of Telegram for intelligence recruitment.

### Energy grid synchronization transforms the strategic landscape

On **8–9 February 2025**, the Baltic states permanently disconnected from the Russian-controlled IPS/UPS (BRELL) electricity grid and synchronized with the Continental European Network — ending approximately 60 years of dependence on Moscow-controlled grid dispatch. The project cost exceeded **€1.2 billion** in EU co-financing through the Connecting Europe Facility. Kaliningrad became an electrical island. A November 2025 **Baltic 3+1 Energy Dialogue** with the US Secretary of Energy discussed LNG imports, critical infrastructure security, and small modular nuclear reactors. Suspected sabotage of the Estlink 2 cable (Christmas Day 2024) and a Latvia-Sweden fiber optic cable (January 2025) prompted NATO's **Baltic Sentry** enhanced vigilance activity.

### Ukraine: unwavering support with per-capita leadership

Latvia's total support to Ukraine since February 2022 exceeds **€859 million** (0.7% of annual GDP for 2022–2024): military aid €540 million, humanitarian/government support €82 million, refugee assistance €225 million, and reform/reconstruction €12 million. In 2024, Latvia exceeded its 0.25% GDP military support pledge, delivering **€170 million** versus €112 million pledged. The 2025 target rose to 0.30% GDP; 2026 returns to 0.25%. Equipment delivered includes **42 Patria 6×6 APCs**, CVR(T) reconnaissance vehicles, self-propelled howitzers, radars, and approximately **5,000 drones** in 2024. Latvia authorized Ukraine to use all provided weapons to strike inside Russian territory.

Latvia's position on peace negotiations is unambiguous: advocacy for a "just, lasting and comprehensive peace" ensuring Ukraine's rights under the UN Charter, promotion of Ukraine's Peace Formula, and **opposition to territorial concessions**. A bilateral security agreement was signed in April 2024 (10-year commitment). Latvia's UNSC seat, beginning January 2026, provides a unique platform; FM Braže warned in March 2026 that US/Israel strikes on Iran must not push Ukraine off the international agenda.

Approximately **32,000 Ukrainian refugees** reside in Latvia with temporary protection. UNHCR's 2025 survey found 62% employment, 41% with effective Latvian communication skills, 80% school enrollment, and 72% intending to remain. However, public support for receiving refugees declined from **73.5%** (December 2022) to **46.8%** (late 2024).

### Russia and Belarus: near-zero diplomatic relations, active hybrid threat

Latvia has virtually no diplomatic relations with Russia — embassy staff drastically reduced, Russia declared a "state sponsor of terrorism." The Belarus border features high fencing, with Latvia restricting pedestrian/non-motorized crossings at three remaining checkpoints in March 2025. The 2026 defense budget allocates **€55 million** for eastern border reinforcement. Persistent DDoS attacks from Russia-linked hacktivists (NoName057, Anonymous Guys) target government and critical infrastructure, surging with each Ukraine aid announcement. Latvia updated its Criminal Law in mid-2024 to criminalize deepfake political manipulation. Approximately **20% of Russian-speaking viewers** still access banned Russian state TV via VPNs.

## 18. Bilateral relationships with monitored middle powers

### Latvia-Estonia: the deepest bilateral partnership

The Latvia-Estonia relationship is the most integrated among Latvia's bilateral ties. A **second joint government session** (27 August 2025, Tartu) produced joint decisions across five domains: security/crisis preparedness, connectivity, EU MFF negotiations, skills/competitiveness, and digital solutions. Joint projects include the **Baltic Defence Line** (Latvia investing €303 million over five years; 280 km fence on the Russia border completed by end-2025), **Baltic Innovation Fund 3** (launched September 2025), the fourth Estonia-Latvia electricity transmission link under construction, and coordinated fuel supply security and export/investment action plans. Military cooperation operates through the Baltic Defence College (Tartu), NATO StratCom CoE (Riga), MND North (Ādaži/Karup), and joint HIMARS and IRIS-T procurement. Friction is minimal — limited to EU MFF negotiation coordination.

### Latvia-Lithuania: Rail Baltica strains an otherwise strong relationship

Security cooperation mirrors the Estonia relationship: Baltic Defence Line, coordinated HIMARS procurement (January 2024 MoU), Baltic Council of Ministers (Latvia chairing 2025), and near-identical Russia/Ukraine positioning. Energy interconnection is strong following grid synchronization, with Klaipėda LNG and the Lithuania-Poland GIPL pipeline ensuring regional gas access.

**Rail Baltica** is the primary friction point. The 870 km high-speed rail project (Tallinn-Riga-Kaunas-Polish border) has suffered severe cost overruns — from ~€6 billion a decade ago to potentially **€15.3 billion** for Phase 1 (€23.8 billion total), with Latvia's share at ~€5.5 billion. Phase 1 single-track completion is now targeted for 2030, pushed from the original 2025 timeline. European Court of Auditors reports note delays are likely to increase further, with no implementation schedule for Phase 2. Latvia's section may not be fully operational before 2035. An EU Commission implementing decision (9 July 2025) set new milestones. A €1.77 billion electrification contract was signed in September 2025. The project's dual-use significance for NATO military mobility (25-ton axle load for tanks, €51 million EU military mobility fund to Latvia in January 2025) keeps political commitment high despite frustrations.

### Latvia-Poland: Suwalki solidarity without bilateral friction

The Suwalki Gap — the ~65 km corridor between Poland and Lithuania connecting the Baltics to the rest of NATO — defines the Latvia-Poland security relationship. Poland's **East Shield** program ($2.5 billion, 700 km of fortifications along Russia/Belarus borders, launched November 2024) directly benefits Baltic security. Polish troops contribute an armored company (Leopard 2PL tanks) to MNB-LVA. Both countries are members of the **Bucharest Nine**, **Three Seas Initiative**, and **European Sky Shield Initiative**. Political alignment on Russia, Ukraine, and defense spending is near-total. No bilateral friction points are identified.

### Latvia-Sweden: a landmark first NATO deployment

Sweden's first major NATO deployment since its **7 March 2024** accession is to Latvia. The Swedish Parliament approved long-term battalion rotations on 12 December 2024, with **550 troops** from the 71st Mechanized Infantry Battalion arriving 18 January 2025 with CV90 infantry fighting vehicles, APC360 APCs, and Stridsvagn 122 (Leopard 2) tanks. The transfer of authority ceremony (7 February 2025) was attended by PM Kristersson, Defence Minister Jonsson, FM Malmer Stenergard, and Chief of Defence Claesson — underscoring the political significance. **King Carl XVI Gustaf** visited Camp Valdemar at Ādaži. Sweden and Denmark now alternate battalion responsibility on ~6-month rotations. A second Swedish deployment with enhanced air defense systems (CV90 LVKV 90) is planned for 2026. Both countries participate in the **CAVS Framework** for Patria 6×6 procurement (Finland-Sweden-Latvia core, expanded to include Germany, Denmark, UK, and Norway by September 2025).

### Latvia-Denmark: structural defense integration through MND North

Denmark is co-framework nation with Latvia and Estonia for **Multinational Division North**, with HQ elements at Ādaži and Karup. Denmark contributes a rotating battalion to MNB-LVA (peak deployment 850 troops in 2024) and alternates with Sweden. A **state visit by King Frederick X and Queen Mary** (28–29 October 2025) produced a letter of intent to expand defense cooperation. Denmark joined the CAVS Framework (April 2025) and is a Drone Coalition member and contributor to the common fund.

### Latvia-Canada: the defining bilateral defense relationship

Canada's role as framework nation for MNB-LVA, with ~2,000 troops, $2.6 billion total commitment, $230 million CAD infrastructure investment, and potential permanent basing, makes this Latvia's **single most important bilateral defense relationship**. PM Carney's August 2025 visit renewing Operation REASSURANCE for three years and the December 2025 study on permanent basing signal deepening commitment. Canada deployed 15 Leopard 2A4M tanks (end 2023), Griffon and Chinook helicopters, and serves as the organizational hub for 14 contributing nations.

### Latvia-UK: co-leadership and sub-threshold response

Beyond the Drone Coalition co-leadership (€4.5 billion cumulative commitments, 18 member states) and JEF founding membership, the UK-Latvia relationship operates through the 3+3 framework nation format (UK leads the Estonian battlegroup, providing indirect regional security). The UK joined the CAVS Framework in September 2025 and contributes CVR(T) vehicles to Ukraine via Latvia. The UK-Norway December 2025 frigate deal (£10 billion) enhances Baltic Sea naval security.

### Latvia-Finland: defense industry integration

Finland's **Patria** opened a 6×6 armored vehicle production facility in **Valmiera** in 2024 — significant Finnish FDI creating an operational defense-industrial link. Finnish FM **Elina Valtonen** visited Riga on 28 January 2025, meeting FM Braže and addressing the diplomatic corps on Nordic-Baltic security. Both are founding ESSI members and CAVS Framework participants. The NB8 format and joint PURL contribution (~$500 million NB8-wide) provide multilateral reinforcement.

### Latvia-Ukraine: patron relationship, not peer partnership

Latvia's relationship with Ukraine has evolved beyond traditional bilateral ties into what functions as a patron-client dynamic in the security domain — with Latvia as patron despite its far smaller size. Latvia's 0.25–0.30% GDP annual military commitment, co-leadership of the Drone Coalition, training of **4,000+ Ukrainian soldiers** since 2022 (with 4,000 planned for 2025), and participation in training the **NB8 Nordic-Baltic Brigade** (3,000–5,000 Ukrainian personnel) give Latvia influence disproportionate to its population. The €15 million reconstruction commitment focused on Chernihiv region, Jaunsardze's August 2025 camp for 52 children of killed or wounded Ukrainian soldiers, and authorization to strike inside Russian territory with Latvian-provided weapons all signal deep political solidarity.

## 19. Cross-cutting strategic frameworks

Latvia participates in an unusually dense web of multilateral frameworks for a country of 1.8 million: **NATO** (since 2004), the **EU** (since 2004, eurozone since 2014), **NB8** (increasingly the primary coordination platform), **JEF** (UK-led, 10 nations), **ESSI** (European Sky Shield), **CAVS** (armored vehicle procurement), **Bucharest Nine**, **Three Seas Initiative**, **Baltic Council of Ministers/Baltic Assembly**, and now the **UN Security Council** (2026–2027). This institutional density provides redundancy — if any single framework weakens, multiple alternatives exist to maintain security cooperation, information sharing, and political coordination. The NB8's emergence as a de facto defense coordination mechanism (moving beyond its origins as a political talk-shop) is perhaps the most significant structural development since 2022.

## 20. Conclusion: a small state at the edge of strategic transformation

Latvia in 2026 occupies a paradoxical position. Its external security architecture has never been stronger — NATO brigade-level presence, approaching 5% GDP defense spending, UNSC membership, deep Nordic-Baltic integration, and a Canadian framework partner studying permanent basing. Its internal politics, however, face compounding pressures: coalition fragility, the return of oligarch-linked populism via Šlesers's Latvia First, progressive-conservative polarization over the Istanbul Convention, Russian-speaking community disengagement, and an economy growing too slowly to cushion the fiscal strain of defense investment.

Three dynamics deserve close monitoring through the October 2026 election cycle. First, **the cordon sanitaire's durability**: whether mainstream parties will partner with Latvia First despite Šlesers's history, as arithmetic may demand. Second, **defense spending sustainability**: 4.91% GDP is extraordinary for a country with 59/100 corruption perceptions and a projected 3.3% budget deficit — the social contract supporting this burden depends on perceived threat credibility. Third, **the Russian-speaking community's trajectory**: with 180,000 non-citizens, 841 facing deportation, Russian eliminated from education and media, and documented political apathy, the integration challenge has shifted from active resistance to passive alienation — a different but potentially more durable problem.

Latvia's strategic culture — forged in occupation, deportation, and contested memory — produces a clarity of threat perception and alliance commitment that larger, less historically traumatized states struggle to match. The question for the next decade is whether that clarity can be maintained against the entropy of populism, fiscal strain, and alliance uncertainty.
