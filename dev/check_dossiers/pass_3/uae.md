## Section 13: The emptiest civil society in the modern Middle East

The UAE has achieved something historically unusual — the near-total elimination of independent civil society within a state that simultaneously projects a cosmopolitan, tolerant global brand. **Every single human rights defender active inside the UAE has been imprisoned or driven into exile**, according to the Gulf Centre for Human Rights. The trajectory from limited dissent to total suppression unfolded in identifiable phases.

**The 1994–2011 dissent arc.** Al-Islah (Association for Reform and Guidance), founded in 1974 as a Muslim Brotherhood-affiliated NGO, built influence through mosques, universities, and professional associations during the 1980s — what one ruling family member called a "state within the state." Its Dubai branch was dissolved in 1994 after Egyptian government complaints about alleged Islamic Jihad financing. Members were barred from public office. The organization continued operating informally, particularly in Ras al-Khaimah, until the Arab Spring forced a decisive confrontation.

On March 3, 2011, **133 Emirati academics, judges, lawyers, and activists** signed a petition to the UAE president demanding a fully elected Federal National Council with legislative powers, universal suffrage, and retreat of the security state. The government responded by arresting the "UAE Five" — Ahmed Mansoor, Nasser Bin Ghaith, and three others — in April 2011, simultaneously shutting down the Teachers' Association and Jurists' Association. Seven petition signatories had their citizenship revoked, rendering them stateless. The arrests established the precedent that even constitutionally grounded reform demands would trigger criminal prosecution.

**The mass trials.** The UAE94 trial (verdict July 2, 2013) was the largest political mass trial in UAE history: **56 defendants sentenced to 10 years**, 8 to 15 years in absentia, with 25 acquitted. The International Commission of Jurists found proceedings fell "well below international fair trial standards." Most defendants completed their sentences by 2019–2022 but remain detained in so-called "Munasaha Counselling Centres" — described by rights groups as simply different buildings within the same prison complex. At least 55 dissidents were held beyond their sentence completion as of 2023.

The UAE84 trial, launched December 7, 2023 — timed during COP28 — recycled many UAE94 defendants on new charges of forming a "clandestine terrorist organization" under the 2014 Counter-Terrorism Law. On July 10, 2024, **43 defendants received life sentences** and 10 others received 10–15 years. The Federal Supreme Court rejected all 53 appeals on March 4, 2025. Nine UN experts called the terrorism charges "spurious." The use of retroactive legislation and double jeopardy violated fundamental legal principles.

**Ahmed Mansoor's annihilation.** Mansoor — an engineer, blogger, Martin Ennals Award laureate (2015), and HRW advisory committee member — has been in **solitary confinement since his March 20, 2017 arrest**. Sentenced to 10 years for "insulting the status and prestige of the UAE" via social media, he received an additional 15 years in the UAE84 trial. His conditions have been described by UN experts as potentially constituting torture: denied reading materials, television, radio, eyeglasses (since December 2017), and initially a bed or mattress. His wife last saw him in September 2017. The UN Secretary-General has cited his case in reprisals reports eight times across 2014–2024. He was previously targeted with FinFisher spyware (2013–2014), Hacking Team spyware, and NSO Group's Pegasus (2016) — the latter involving three iOS zero-day exploits worth an estimated $1 million, earning him the designation "Million Dollar Dissident" from Citizen Lab.

### The legal architecture of suppression

The legal framework operates through layered instruments. **Federal Decree-Law No. 50 of 2023** (replacing Federal Law No. 2 of 2008) requires all associations to register with the Ministry of Community Development, which retains power to approve registrations, inspect finances, and dissolve organizations. **Federal Decree-Law No. 34 of 2021** on cybercrime criminalizes spreading "false information" and imposes life sentences if the state's "existence is challenged" — applying even to private WhatsApp messages. The 2014 Counter-Terrorism Law uses a definition of terrorism so broad that peaceful advocacy qualifies.

There are **no genuinely independent civil society organizations** operating inside the UAE. The state manages a system of government-organized NGOs (GONGOs) — the Emirates Red Crescent Society, the Abu Dhabi Ma'an social incubator program, and various state-backed foundations — that maintain the appearance of civil society without any autonomous function. Professional associations exist but authorities retain "broad powers to interfere in their activities," per the U.S. State Department.

### Labor, online, and academic suppression

Labor organizing is explicitly prohibited by law. The **kafala sponsorship system** gives employers control over workers' immigration status, creating structural dependency reinforced by widespread passport confiscation and recruitment fee debt bondage. Notable suppression episodes include the 2013 Arabtec strike (dozens deported), Saadiyat Island/Louvre Abu Dhabi construction worker strikes (broken by deportation and possible strikebreaker violence), and the mass deportation of approximately 800 African workers in summer 2021. In July 2024, 57 Bangladeshi nationals received sentences including three life terms for peaceful protest solidarity — pardoned in September 2024 after international pressure. An estimated **132,000 individuals** lived in conditions of modern slavery in the UAE as of 2021.

The surveillance apparatus is among the world's most sophisticated. The UAE contracted both NSO Group (Pegasus spyware) and the homegrown DarkMatter/Project Raven operation — the latter employing former NSA officers to target activists, journalists, and even U.S. citizens. Three ex-NSA operatives reached a $1.6 million deferred prosecution agreement. DarkMatter's founder, Faisal Al Bannai, was subsequently appointed by MBZ as Advisor for Strategic Research and Advanced Technology with ministerial rank. Abu Dhabi's "Falcon Eye" and Dubai's "Oyoon" systems access thousands of cameras with facial recognition capabilities.

Academic freedom constraints affect all foreign university branches. NYU Abu Dhabi has seen professors barred from the country (Andrew Ross, 2015; Bazzi and Keshavarzian, 2017), books censored, and a PhD student detained for one week and deported in May 2024 for yelling "free Palestine" at graduation. The AAUP and Middle East Studies Association accused NYU of complicity in UAE repression. Sorbonne Abu Dhabi lecturer Nasser Bin Ghaith was arrested as part of the UAE Five and subsequently sentenced to 10 years.

### Exile opposition and residual channels

Organized exile opposition operates through the Emirates Detainees Advocacy Center (EDAC), run by Hamad Mohammed Al-Shamsi (sentenced in absentia, added to UAE terror list in 2021), and the London-based International Campaign for Freedom in the UAE (ICFUAE, founded 2015). These groups have achieved European Parliament resolutions, regular UN reprisal citations, and media coverage — but no prisoner releases. The UAE has responded by adding exile leaders to terror lists and bringing new charges.

Traditional dissent channels have been fully co-opted. The majlis tradition has been reduced to managed consultation without political power. All Sunni imams are government-appointed and distribute state-approved weekly sermons. Al-Islah's designation as a terrorist organization in 2014 eliminated any remaining autonomous mosque-based organizing.

**Current ratings**: Freedom House 2025 scores the UAE **18/100 ("Not Free")**. RSF ranks it **164th of 180** on press freedom. Freedom on the Net 2025 scores **28/100 ("Not Free")**, declining from 30 in 2024.

## Section 14: An alliance genealogy built on calculated non-dependence

The UAE's patron-client history reveals a state that has internalized a single strategic lesson across five decades: **never depend on one patron**. From British protectorate to American security partner to multi-vector hedger, each phase was driven by the preceding patron's perceived unreliability.

### From British protection to American alignment

The Trucial States system (1820–1971) bound the UAE's predecessor entities to Britain through treaties giving London control over foreign affairs and defense. Britain's January 1968 withdrawal announcement — experienced as abandonment, with warships standing idle while Iran seized Emirati islands — created the founding trauma that drives current hedging doctrine. The 1990 Gulf War provided the catalyst for American alignment: the UAE hosted U.S. forces during Desert Storm and became **the only Arab country to participate in six coalition operations with the United States since 1991**.

The U.S. partnership deepened through the 1994 Defense Cooperation Agreement (replaced by a 15-year accord in May 2019), the post-9/11 establishment of operations at Al Dhafra Air Base (~5,000 U.S. troops, hosting F-22s, F-35s, U-2s, and RQ-4 Global Hawks), and the 2020 Abraham Accords. On **September 23, 2024**, Biden designated the UAE a "Major Defense Partner" during MBZ's first-ever official state visit to the White House — making the UAE only the second country (after India) to receive this designation. A May 2025 Letter of Intent established a structured defense partnership roadmap, and the UAE was welcomed into the U.S. National Guard State Partnership Program through partnership with the Texas National Guard.

Yet **the F-35 deal remains frozen** as of early 2026. The original $23.3 billion package (50 F-35As, 18 MQ-9B Reapers) was announced under Trump in late 2020, paused by Biden, and suspended by the UAE in December 2021 over U.S. demands to remove Huawei 5G equipment and sovereign operational restrictions. A senior UAE official told Reuters in September 2024 there were "no plans to resume talks." Trump's May 2025 visit yielded **$200 billion in commercial deals** — including the Stargate UAE AI campus — but the F-35 was notably absent. Key friction points include UAE's alleged weapons channeling to Sudan's RSF (prompting congressional blocks on arms sales in March 2025) and the UAE's restriction of Al Dhafra base use during the Israel-Hamas war.

### The China vector: trade giant, defense supplier, AI partner

China is the UAE's **largest trading partner**: bilateral trade reached a record **$101.8 billion in 2024**, roughly 800 times the volume when ties were established in 1984. Over 15,000 Chinese firms operate in the UAE. The defense relationship has expanded through procurement of **12 L-15 trainer/light attack aircraft** (with options for 36 more), Wing Loong II drones, and joint "Falcon Shield" exercises in Xinjiang in 2023 and 2024 — the latter raising intelligence concerns because UAE Mirage 2000-9s also serve in Taiwan's air force.

The **G42 saga** epitomizes the hedging dilemma. The Abu Dhabi AI company, chaired by National Security Advisor Tahnoun bin Zayed and run by former DarkMatter CEO Peng Xiao, maintained deep Chinese partnerships (BGI, Huawei infrastructure, ByteDance investments). Under U.S. pressure, G42 divested Chinese investments in February 2024 and accepted a **$1.5 billion Microsoft stake** in April 2024, agreeing to strip ~$1.7–2 billion in Huawei equipment. But Bloomberg reported the Chinese investments were merely transferred to **Lunate**, a new Abu Dhabi vehicle also overseen by Tahnoun that manages $105 billion including Chinese tech stocks. The **Khalifa Port controversy** — where U.S. intelligence detected construction of a suspected Chinese military facility in 2020–2021 — remains unresolved; leaked intelligence documents suggest construction may have resumed in December 2022.

### Russia: economic opportunism, diplomatic hedging

The UAE did not impose sanctions on Russia after February 2022. UAE-Russia trade increased approximately **68% in 2022 to ~$9 billion**. Electronic component exports to Russia grew sevenfold; microchip exports surged from $1.6 million to $24.3 million. At least 38 influential Russians connected to Putin own properties in Dubai collectively valued at **$314 million or more**. Nearly 1,000 gold shipments arrived from Russia between February 2022 and March 2023, totaling 96.4 tons. Almost one-third of all aircraft parts shipments to Russia originated from the UAE.

The UAE joined **BRICS on January 1, 2024**, and has since shifted its UN voting pattern — consistently abstaining on Ukraine-related resolutions after previously voting in favor four times out of ten. MBZ has maintained personal channels with Putin, hosting him in Abu Dhabi in December 2023, while simultaneously brokering **4,641 prisoner exchanges** between Ukraine and Russia.

### France: the European third option

The **€16.6 billion Rafale deal** (December 2021) for 80 F4-variant jets — the largest Rafale export order in history — was signed days before F-35 negotiations collapsed, serving as both genuine capability acquisition and strategic signal. The first UAE Rafale was unveiled in Paris in January 2025; deliveries begin in 2027. France maintains a **permanent military base in Abu Dhabi since 2009** (Air Base 104 at Al Dhafra, naval facilities at Mina Zayed, and a training camp at Zayed Military City) with approximately 700 troops — its first new overseas base in 50 years. Mubadala pledged €8 billion in French investments alongside the Rafale deal, and the Louvre Abu Dhabi license was extended to 2047.

### India, Israel, and the Asian pivot

The UAE-India relationship has accelerated dramatically. The 2022 CEPA drove non-oil bilateral trade to **$100 billion** in fiscal year 2024–2025. On **January 19, 2026**, MBZ visited New Delhi for a working visit that produced a Strategic Defense Partnership Letter of Intent (covering defense industrial collaboration, cyber, counterterrorism, and special operations), a **$3 billion ADNOC LNG deal**, nuclear cooperation on "large reactors," and space infrastructure development. The I2U2 group (India-Israel-UAE-US) remains active but complicated by Gaza dynamics.

The **Abraham Accords** have survived the Gaza war but cooled substantially. Bilateral trade surpassed $3.2 billion annually, and defense cooperation includes Barak air defense systems, SPYDER systems, and Hermes 900 UAV negotiations. However, the UAE barred Israeli defense companies from the October 2025 Dubai Airshow, and in **September 2025 issued its first public warning that Israeli annexation of the West Bank would constitute a "red line" gravely harming the Accords**. Public support for normalization across the Arab world dropped from 31% (2021) to 13% (2024).

### The Saudi rupture

The December 2025 Yemen crisis represents the most serious fracture in the GCC's central bilateral relationship. In early December, UAE-backed **Southern Transitional Council forces launched a surprise offensive seizing Hadramawt and al-Mahra** — nearly half of Yemen's territory, including oil-producing regions bordering Saudi Arabia. When a joint Saudi-Emirati delegation demanded the STC return seized territory, the STC refused. Saudi Arabia responded with airstrikes on STC positions (December 26–27), culminating in a **Royal Saudi Air Force strike on the port of Mukalla on December 30** targeting what Riyadh said was a UAE weapons shipment (two ships, 80+ vehicles and containers of weapons/ammunition).

The internationally recognized Yemeni government cancelled the defense pact with the UAE, issued a 24-hour withdrawal ultimatum, declared a 90-day state of emergency, and imposed a 72-hour blockade. Saudi-backed forces launched a counteroffensive that recaptured Seiyun (January 3), Mukalla (January 4), and Aden (January 7). STC leader Aidarous al-Zubaidi was removed from the Presidential Leadership Council, charged with high treason, and allegedly fled to the UAE via Somaliland. The **STC announced its dissolution on January 9, 2026**.

This confrontation extends beyond Yemen. Saudi Arabia and the UAE back opposing sides in Sudan (UAE allegedly supports RSF; Saudi supports SAF). Economic competition intensifies as both pursue AI/tech hub status, compete for multinational headquarters (Saudi's 2021 decree requiring relocation to Riyadh), and dispute OPEC+ quotas. The CSIS assessment that the MBZ-MBS relationship has degenerated from "mentor-mentee to rivalry" captures the structural nature of the shift. A joint statement from Iran, Saudi Arabia, and China calling for a UN-supervised Yemen settlement signals new alignment geometries.

### Turkey and Iran: rehabilitated rivals

The UAE-Turkey relationship transformed from enmity (divergence over the Muslim Brotherhood, Qatar blockade, Libya, alleged UAE involvement in the 2016 Turkish coup attempt) to rapprochement beginning in 2021. MBZ visited Ankara in November 2021, pledging a **$10 billion investment fund**; Erdogan visited Abu Dhabi in February 2022 with 13 agreements signed. Non-oil trade reached **$40.54 billion in 2024**, up 11.5% annually.

UAE-Iran diplomatic engagement deepened through 2024–2025, with Tahnoun bin Zayed spearheading rapprochement, a July 2024 foreign minister visit to Tehran, and the UAE serving as intermediary delivering a Trump letter to Iran in March 2025. This engagement was reportedly severed on March 1, 2026 following Iranian retaliatory strikes on UAE territory during the escalation following Israeli-U.S. strikes on Iran — though this very recent development requires verification.

The UAE-Egypt relationship, cemented by shared opposition to the Muslim Brotherhood and **Abu Dhabi's $35 billion Ras El-Hikma deal** (February 2024, the largest FDI in Egyptian history), remains the UAE's most asymmetric patron-client relationship. However, divergences over Sudan and Red Sea dynamics are generating new friction.

## Section 15: A succession machine running on dynastic engineering

The UAE's constitutional architecture is formally federal but functionally monarchical, with power concentrated in Abu Dhabi's Al Nahyan family. Historical succession has been violently contested: **from 1793 to 1966, fifteen sheikhs ascended Abu Dhabi's throne, of whom five were overthrown and eight assassinated.** The current system represents MBZ's attempt to engineer a transition that breaks this pattern.

### From bloodshed to bureaucratic succession

The **August 6, 1966 coup** — replacing Shakhbut bin Sultan with his brother Zayed — established the template for managed transition. Shakhbut refused to spend oil revenues on development, opposed modernization, and resisted federation. British officials and Zayed were "equal partners" in the bloodless removal, carried out while Shakhbut was yachting with Prince Rainier of Monaco. Zayed then built the UAE, serving as president from 1971 until his death on November 2, 2004.

Khalifa bin Zayed (eldest son, different mother from MBZ) became ruler in 2004 and was elected president the next day. His **January 2014 stroke** — described as "not his first" — left him greatly incapacitated, with MBZ assuming de facto control as crown prince and deputy supreme commander. This arrangement persisted for eight years until Khalifa's death on May 13, 2022, when MBZ's formal accession was immediately affirmed by all seven rulers. The smooth transition obscured the underlying reality: for nearly a decade, the UAE's formal head of state was unable to govern.

### The March 2023 restructuring: lineal succession declared

On March 29, 2023, MBZ announced the most consequential dynastic restructuring in UAE history. **Sheikh Khaled bin Mohamed bin Zayed** (born January 8, 1982) was named Crown Prince of Abu Dhabi and Chairman of the Abu Dhabi Executive Council — establishing for the first time that succession would follow a **father-to-son (lineal) rather than brother-to-brother (lateral) pattern**. MBZ's brothers received compensatory positions: Mansour became Vice President (alongside Dubai's ruler), Tahnoun and Hazza became Deputy Rulers of Abu Dhabi.

The Soufan Center assessed this as ending "50 years of horizontal power-sharing" among Zayed's sons. The creation of a second vice president was interpreted as reducing Dubai's influence on national security decisions. Critically, Tahnoun bin Zayed — who reportedly competed for the crown prince title — was not placed in the line of succession despite controlling a sovereign wealth portfolio estimated at **$1.3–1.5 trillion**.

### Khaled bin Mohamed: the prepared heir

Khaled's preparation follows MBZ's own template. Educated at Arizona State University (BA, 2004), Georgetown's Walsh School of Foreign Service (MA, 2007), and reportedly the Royal Military Academy Sandhurst, he was appointed head of the State Security Department in 2016, deputy national security adviser in 2017, and chairman of the Abu Dhabi Executive Office in 2019. His current portfolio spans the Abu Dhabi Executive Council, the Advanced Technology Research Council, the UAE Genomics Council, and board positions at ADNOC and ADIA.

A July 2025 Watan analysis argues Khaled is "not a symbolic prince being groomed for a distant future" but "an active Crown Prince exercising powers through three parallel channels: sovereign security apparatus, media/presidential communications, and economic leverage." He has reportedly been "restructuring the security apparatus to ensure vertical loyalty directly to him, bypassing Tahnoun bin Zayed."

### L'IMAD: the $260 billion consolidation signal

The most dramatic succession signal came on **January 30, 2026**, when the Supreme Council for Financial and Economic Affairs consolidated the assets of L'IMAD Holding Company and ADQ under a single structure chaired by Khaled. This transferred approximately **$260 billion in assets** — including TAQA, Etihad Airways, McLaren, AD Ports Group, PureHealth, and Etihad Rail — from Tahnoun's oversight to Khaled's control.

The significance is structural. ADQ was previously "a central pillar of the investment network overseen by Sheikh Tahnoun." L'Orient Today called it "unprecedented in the emirate." The SCFEA now oversees three principal sovereign investment funds: ADIA, Mubadala, and L'IMAD (plus ADNOC). The move mirrors MBZ's own consolidation pattern — he oversaw Mubadala's creation in 2002 (now $330 billion) while consolidating authority during Zayed's final years.

The timing is notable: GSN reported the ADQ transfer occurred "one day before" Tahnoun's role in a $500 million Trump cryptocurrency venture was publicly identified. Tahnoun retains enormous power — ADIA chairmanship ($1 trillion+), G42, MGX ($50 billion AI fund), and the national security adviser role — but the trajectory of asset reallocation points toward deliberate, phased reduction of his autonomous resource base.

### Dubai's parallel transition and federal architecture

Dubai's succession mechanics are also in motion. On July 14, 2024, **Hamdan bin Mohammed Al Maktoum** (Crown Prince of Dubai, born 1982) was appointed Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Defense — the first time the defense portfolio left Mohammed bin Rashid's hands since the UAE's founding. Dubai's ruler, now 76, faces reputational constraints from divorce proceedings and the Princess Latifa controversy, making him "vulnerable to pressure from Abu Dhabi."

The formal federal architecture — the Supreme Council of Rulers (seven hereditary rulers electing the president), the Federal National Council (40 advisory members, no legislative power, political parties banned) — provides constitutional procedure but not genuine constraint. The BTI 2024 report notes "court decisions are subject to review by the political leadership" and there is no real separation of powers. Decision-making flows from MBZ to his inner circle (the Bani Fatima brothers and increasingly Khaled) to institutional bodies that formalize rather than deliberate.

The **northern emirates** (Ajman, Fujairah, Ras al-Khaimah, Umm al-Quwain) collectively account for only **6.6% of UAE GDP** and depend on Abu Dhabi for approximately 90% of federal government funding. Abu Dhabi announced a $1.6 billion infrastructure investment plan for northern emirates after the Arab Spring to preempt unrest. The military draws disproportionately from these poorer emirates. Federal cohesion is maintained through fiscal dependency rather than political consent.

## Section 16: Grievance as strategic infrastructure

The UAE's collective memory architecture serves dual functions — binding a federation of disparate emirates and justifying the foreign policy doctrine of calculated non-dependence. Unlike many postcolonial states, the UAE's founding grievances concern not colonial exploitation but **abandonment, coercion, and territorial violation by ostensible protectors and allies**.

### The islands: sovereignty wound as national glue

Iran's seizure of Greater Tunb, Lesser Tunb, and Abu Musa on November 30, 1971 — the day before British treaty protections expired — is the UAE's primary sovereignty grievance. On Greater Tunb, 20-year-old policeman **Salem Suhail bin Khamis** refused to lower the flag and was shot dead, becoming the UAE's designated first martyr. Four Ras al-Khaimah policemen and three Iranian soldiers died. The approximately 120 Arab civilians were forcibly removed.

**Commemoration Day** (November 30), established in 2015, directly marks the battle. The narrative is taught in schools, embedded in civic education, and functions as the federation's most unifying grievance — giving emirates with otherwise distinct identities a shared sovereignty claim. The dispute also generates internal tension: Ras al-Khaimah advocates tougher measures against Iran, while Dubai's ruler Mohammed bin Rashid has publicly suggested tensions have been "fabricated by the United States." The UAE embassy in Washington maintains a dedicated "Occupied UAE Islands" page offering three resolution paths. The GCC regularly declares support; an October 2024 EU-GCC summit calling on Iran to "end its occupation" triggered sharp Iranian backlash.

### British abandonment as strategic doctrine

Britain's January 1968 withdrawal announcement was experienced as betrayal. Defence Secretary Denis Healey publicly stated he disliked being "a sort of white slaver for Arab sheikhs." Sheikh Zayed and Sheikh Rashid traveled to London to plead for continued protection or offer payment — both were refused. The rushed federation formation saw Bahrain and Qatar opt out, and Ras al-Khaimah initially refuse to join (only entering in February 1972 after Iran seized its islands and it realized it couldn't survive alone). The "constitution was quickly drafted to meet the 2 December deadline."

The fact that **two British warships stood idle** during Iran's island seizure deepened the trauma. This experience directly informs the UAE's multi-patron strategy: hosting bases from the U.S., France, and the UK simultaneously; maintaining economic ties with China and Russia; and never entering exclusive security arrangements. The lesson is explicit in Emirati strategic culture: patron reliability is finite.

### The 1974 Treaty of Jeddah: coerced concession

The 1974 Treaty of Jeddah with Saudi Arabia represents a grievance of forced territorial concession. Saudi Arabia withheld recognition of the newly independent UAE until territorial disputes from the 1952 Buraimi crisis were resolved. King Faisal told the UAE delegation: "Saudi Arabia had been humiliated in Buraimi and it would have to retrieve its rights."

Saudi Arabia gained a **15-mile coastline** east from Khor al-Udaid (establishing the only Saudi-Qatari land border, severing any UAE-Qatar land connection) and **all income from the 14-billion-barrel Shaybah oil field**. Scholar Richard Schofield called it "the most bizarre international boundary agreement ever signed between two states." Saudi Arabia ratified the treaty in 1993; **the UAE has never ratified it**, claiming discrepancies between the oral agreement and written text that were not noticed until 1975 "due to the absence of lawyers, technicians, and geographers" on the UAE negotiation team. The treaty was publicly reopened in 2004 after Zayed's death. In 2009, Saudi authorities prevented Emirati nationals from entering with ID cards showing maps that included Saudi-administered territory. The December 2025 Yemen confrontation adds a new layer to this unresolved grievance.

### Dubai 2009 and the Houthi attacks: modern traumas

Dubai World's $26 billion debt restructuring announcement on November 25, 2009 — with total state-linked debt reaching ~$80 billion — required an Abu Dhabi bailout of approximately **$25 billion**. The renaming of "Burj Dubai" to **"Burj Khalifa"** at its January 4, 2010 opening — honoring Abu Dhabi's ruler — is the most visible global symbol of Abu Dhabi's dominance. The crisis permanently reshaped intra-federation power dynamics, giving Abu Dhabi substantially more influence over Dubai's policy direction.

The **January 17, 2022 Houthi attacks** — drones and ballistic missiles striking an ADNOC fuel storage facility in Musaffah and a construction site near Abu Dhabi International Airport, killing three — shattered the assumption that the Yemen war would not reach Emirati soil. A follow-up attack on January 24 targeting Al Dhafra Air Base was intercepted by the U.S. THAAD system in its **first-ever operational combat use**. The attacks accelerated defense procurement (including a $3.5 billion South Korean missile deal the same week) and contributed to the UAE's diplomatic pivot toward de-escalation with Iran and the Houthis.

### Identity construction against demographic submersion

The demographic reality — **88.5% expatriates** (~10–10.24 million) versus **11.5% citizens** (~1.31–1.44 million) — creates an ongoing identity crisis managed through the citizenship wall, cultural preservation programs, Emiratization mandates, and compulsory national military service (since 2014). The 2018 "Year of Zayed" centenary was the most intensive identity construction campaign, positioning founding father Sheikh Zayed as the embodiment of tolerance, generosity, and wisdom. The Zayed narrative legitimizes Al Nahyan rule while the "Eyal Zayed" (Sons of Zayed) formulation extends national identity through patrilineal connection to the founder.

Post-Abraham Accords positioning on Palestine has required careful navigation. The UAE provided **$2.27 billion in humanitarian assistance to Gaza** while maintaining diplomatic relations with Israel. Public support for normalization dropped from 31% to 13% across the Arab world between 2021 and 2024. Senior Emirati figures including Dubai's deputy police chief have publicly criticized Israel. The September 2025 "red line" warning on West Bank annexation signals the limits of normalization's domestic sustainability.

## Sections 17–20: Structural interconnections and analytical assessment

### How the citizenship wall feeds the surveillance state

The **89% non-citizen population** creates a unique dual surveillance dynamic. Academic research confirms surveillance technologies "penetrate differentially, depending upon existing racialised categories." The state monitors citizens for political dissent and non-citizens for immigration status, labor compliance, and security threats simultaneously. The kafala system functions as distributed surveillance — employers become co-opted agents of state control, with the power to revoke workers' legal status. The "Falcon Eye" (Abu Dhabi) and "Oyoon" (Dubai) systems access thousands of cameras with facial recognition. The BTI 2024 report confirms the UAE revokes citizenship of nationals engaged in "illegal political activities" while foreigners are "swiftly deported." The citizenship wall thus serves as both identity preservation and surveillance force multiplier — non-citizens have no political stake and can be expelled without process, while citizens face an exit threat (denaturalization) that few other states deploy so routinely.

### The hydrocarbon-climate paradox

The UAE hosts COP28, led a coalition to triple nuclear energy by 2050, set a 47% emissions reduction target by 2035, and operates the Barakah nuclear plant (producing 40 TWh annually, **25% of electricity**). Simultaneously, ADNOC plans capacity expansion. Climate projections show average temperature increases of ~2.5°C by 2050, with four months annually "not conducive to traditional agriculture." The UAE's ecological deficit is "the highest in the world." Approximately **90% of food is imported**, at ~$14 billion annually, with less than 1% of land arable. Water dependency on desalination is existential — a 2010 CIA assessment warned disruption would have "more serious consequences than the loss of any other industry or commodity." During the March 2026 Iran conflict, Bahrain reported a drone strike on a desalination plant, illustrating the vulnerability in real time.

### Zayed mythology meets succession fragility

The Zayed narrative legitimizes Al Nahyan rule but creates a dilemma for the post-Zayed generation. MBZ governed for nearly a decade before formal accession, centralizing power beyond anything Zayed practiced. The 2023 shift to lineal succession breaks the lateral pattern that characterized Zayed's era. Khaled bin Mohamed must eventually derive legitimacy from something other than being "Zayed's grandson" — the L'IMAD consolidation suggests he is building an economic power base analogous to MBZ's Mubadala creation. But the Zayed-era promise of consultation (however nominal) has given way to what Christopher Davidson calls "a marked and rapid intensification of autocratic-authoritarianism." The performance-based legitimacy bargain — generous subsidies in exchange for political quiescence — holds only as long as economic delivery continues.

### Think tank consensus and where Western analysis fails

Major think tank assessments converge on several points. CSIS frames the UAE as "a hyper-connected power leveraging capital and statecraft to hedge globally, disrupt regionally, and reshape ties" with major powers. Carnegie chronicles the pivot from "robust military intervention" toward "dialogue and diplomacy." MEI argues the U.S. must "engage the Gulf as it actually is, rather than as it wishes the region to be." ECFR identifies the UAE as "an influential middle power" in Africa and frames the Saudi-UAE rivalry as reshuffling Horn of Africa alliances into a potential "UAE-Israel-Ethiopia axis" versus "Saudi-Egypt-Turkey" alignment.

Western analytical errors cluster in identifiable patterns:

- **Treating the UAE as a U.S. client state.** The Major Defense Partner designation coexists with comprehensive China engagement, Russian money flows, and BRICS membership. The UAE views itself as what CSIS describes as "a global entrepôt that had earned the right to flourish outside of political blocs."
- **Assuming Saudi-UAE unity.** Multiple analysts now identify this as perhaps the most consequential Western failure. The December 2025 confrontation demolished this assumption, but the underlying structural competition — over OPEC+ quotas, corporate headquarters, AI investment, and regional influence from Yemen to Sudan — predated it by years.
- **Overemphasizing oil rents as the sole explanatory variable.** Academic research argues that focusing on oil rents alone "obscures the length to which the state cooperates with other private and international actors." The surveillance-industrial complex, defense diversification, and AI ambitions all represent autonomous power sources.
- **Describing Gulf stability as "managed illusion."** Emirati policymakers frame security through "stability, economic transformation, and institutional capacity." Multi-directional diplomacy is understood locally as strategic risk diversification, not indecision.

### The EDGE Group as autonomy proxy

EDGE Group, consolidating 25+ defense entities since 2019, ranks 22nd globally in defense firms (per SIPRI) with 160 platforms and 70 autonomous systems. Its acquisitions span Estonia (Milrem Robotics), Switzerland (ANAVIA), Brazil (SIATT), and Poland (FLARIS). This defense-industrial base directly affects alliance flexibility — the F-35 suspension led to the Rafale purchase, demonstrating how supply chain diversification from U.S. dependency translates into foreign policy autonomy. Yet the UAE "cannot achieve and project sustainable autonomous hard power" due to population constraints, technological dependency, and geography. The shift toward autonomous systems and AI-driven warfare is partly a response to the demographic constraint of fielding forces from a citizen population of ~1.4 million.

### Middle power relationships beyond the great powers

The UAE's **deepest middle-power relationship is with France** — spanning the Rafale deal, permanent military basing, the Louvre Abu Dhabi cultural agreement, and nuclear cooperation. **South Korea** represents the second-deepest engagement, anchored in the **$20 billion Barakah nuclear plant** (all four APR-1400 reactors now operational, built on time and budget) and expanding through the KF-21 Boramae fighter program and a **$35 billion industrial defense deal** (March 2026). The **UK** relationship retains institutional depth but carries reputational complications from the Princess Latifa and Princess Haya cases. **Brazil** connects through BRICS, defense-industrial acquisitions (SIATT), and the COP28-29-30 troika. **Germany** maintains significant trade but periodic human rights tensions — philosopher Jürgen Habermas rejected the Sheikh Zayed Book Award in 2021, citing connections to "a dictatorship."

### Trajectory risks crystallizing in 2026

The convergence of the Saudi-UAE rupture, the Iran conflict escalation (with Iranian missiles and drones reportedly targeting UAE infrastructure in March 2026), and the succession engineering underway through L'IMAD creates an unusually concentrated risk environment. The UAE's water, food, and energy infrastructure faces direct military threat while its most important regional relationship has fractured. The AI mega-investments (Stargate UAE, MGX, G42) represent both a hedge against hydrocarbon decline and new high-value targets. The fundamental question CSIS poses — whether the UAE can maintain dual hedging as U.S.-China competition intensifies — has been joined by a more immediate one: whether the multi-vector strategy can survive simultaneous crises with Saudi Arabia, Iran, and the succession transition.

The UAE's structural intelligence profile reveals a state of extraordinary adaptive capacity operating within genuine constraints — demographic thinness, geographic exposure, patron unreliability, and a social contract that substitutes performance for participation.
