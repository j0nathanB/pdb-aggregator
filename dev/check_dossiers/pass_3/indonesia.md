## SECTION 13: HISTORY OF DISSENT AND CIVIL SOCIETY INFRASTRUCTURE

### 13.1 — Civil society faces a double squeeze unprecedented since Reformasi

Indonesia's civil society, once the most vibrant in Southeast Asia, operates under a **dual constriction**: domestic repression escalating under Prabowo and collapse of international funding following the elimination of USAID. The operational space for independent organizations has narrowed faster in 2025–2026 than at any point since the late New Order period. V-Dem's 2025 democracy report places Indonesia in the "grey zone," no longer meeting minimum standards of electoral democracy. Amnesty International documented **104 attacks against human rights defenders** in the first half of 2025 alone, spanning criminalization, intimidation, physical violence, and arbitrary arrest.

The major civil society organizations remain operational but under severe pressure. **KontraS** (Commission for the Disappeared and Victims of Violence), under coordinator Dimas Bagus Arya, documented 10 deaths, 451 injured civilians, and 4,145 arbitrary arrests during the August 2025 protests and their aftermath. KontraS's February 2026 Fact-Finding Commission (KPF) report, produced with YLBHI and LBH Jakarta, traced events across 8 provinces and 18 cities, identifying **6,719 total arrests** — described as "the biggest hunt for activists since 1998." KontraS itself faces systematic intimidation: in March 2025, unidentified motorcyclists surveilled their office, members received calls linked to intelligence officials, and Indonesian Army vehicles were documented on CCTV driving past or stopping at KontraS headquarters on four occasions.

**YLBHI/LBH** (Legal Aid Foundation) continues providing legal defense for detained protesters through the Tim Advokasi untuk Demokrasi (TAUD). LBH Jakarta's TikTok account was permanently banned during the August 2025 protests. **ICW** (Indonesia Corruption Watch) maintains anti-corruption monitoring from Jakarta but faces harassment from pro-government groups. **Walhi** (Friends of the Earth Indonesia), under Executive Director Zenzi Suhadi, filed a Constitutional Court challenge on World Environment Day 2025 against 13 articles of the Job Creation Law's environmental provisions.

The legal architecture constraining civil society has multiple layers. The **Ormas Law** (2013, amended 2017) allows the government to dissolve organizations without judicial oversight — a single ministerial written warning can trigger dissolution within seven working days. The revised **TNI Law** (March 2025) enables military crackdowns on activists under "military operations other than war." The **ITE Law** criminalizes online expression. Most ominously, a proposed **Foreign Propaganda and Disinformation Bill**, confirmed by Coordinating Minister Yusril Ihza Mahendra as a direct presidential initiative, includes a 67-page academic memorandum proposing "foreign capital restrictions" targeting organizations receiving foreign grants. Though not yet on the 2026 legislative priority list, presidential pressure could accelerate its passage.

Prabowo's rhetoric reinforces this framework. At a July 2025 PSI Congress, he accused the Indonesia Gelap protests and #KaburAjaDulu movement of being "fabricated" and "paid for by corruptors." After the August 2025 protests, he publicly used the phrase *"Hai, antek-antek asing"* (Hey, foreign lackeys) — a direct echo of New Order-era delegitimization of dissent through allegations of foreign manipulation.

### 13.2 — The student movement showed surprising resilience but structural fragmentation

The 2025 protest cycle produced the most sustained mass mobilization since 1998, unfolding in three distinct waves. The **"Indonesia Gelap" (Dark Indonesia) protests** erupted on February 17, 2025, triggered by Presidential Instruction No. 1/2025 cutting Rp 306.7 trillion ($19 billion) from education and health budgets to fund the Free Nutritious Meal program. BEM SI (All-Indonesian Students' Union) coordinated simultaneous protests in Jakarta, Bandung, Surabaya, Semarang, Bali, and Yogyakarta, with an estimated 5,000 student participants and additional civil society mobilization. Nine demands were presented, including reviewing budget cuts, rejecting TNI dual-function, and ratifying an Asset Confiscation Law. The movement adopted a punk aesthetic and was accompanied by the viral hashtag **#KaburAjaDulu** (Just Run Away First), expressing youth disillusionment. A protest song by duo Sukatani went viral before police intimidated the artists into retracting it.

The **second wave** followed passage of the revised TNI Law on March 20, 2025, themed "Indonesia Gelap Jilid 2." Police opened fire during Jakarta protests, injuring five university students. Over 5,000 joint security personnel deployed against protesters.

The **August 2025 mass protests** represented a qualitative escalation. Triggered by a proposed Rp 50 million ($3,057) monthly housing allowance for 580 House members — ten times Jakarta's minimum wage — amid mass layoffs exceeding 70,000 workers in the first four months of 2025. Beginning August 25 outside the DPR building, protests spread to nearly **50 cities nationwide**. The August 28 killing of **Affan Kurniawan**, a 21-year-old Gojek driver run over by a Brimob police tactical vehicle, went viral and catalyzed dramatic escalation. Government buildings were torched in Makassar and Mataram. MPs' homes were looted. The Straw Hat Pirates' Jolly Roger flag from *One Piece* became a prominent opposition symbol. KontraS documented **10 deaths, 451 injured civilians, and 6,719 arrests** — including 196 minors detained on the first night alone. Forty-four people were reported missing; two remained unaccounted for as of late 2025. Post-protest mass arrests continued through October, with 1,623 detained between September 1 and October 1. Key detained activists face up to 12 years in prison, and a Political Prisoners' Union was formed inside detention.

Key universities active throughout: UI (University of Indonesia), UGM (Gadjah Mada), ITB (Bandung Institute of Technology), Diponegoro, Airlangga, and Mulawarman. BEM SI served as the central coordinating body, developing a "17+8 People's Demands" platform. However, the movement differs structurally from 1998. East Asia Forum analysis notes it "shows early signs of expanding beyond spontaneous anger towards more organised and durable action," but remains more fragmented and horizontal than the organized student-civil society-mass organization networks of Reformasi. Social media influencers have "overshadowed NGO activists and grassroots vocational students." Indonesian diaspora communities mobilized far more actively than in 1998 or 2019.

### 13.3 — Labor is divided between co-optation and opposition

The labor movement reflects a deep structural split. **KSPI** (Confederation of Indonesian Trade Unions), under President Said Iqbal, maintains a dual strategy of supporting Prabowo's government while pressing economic demands. On May Day 2025, KSPI joined the pro-government rally at the National Monument, with Prabowo as featured speaker. Yet KSPI rallied an estimated 10,000 workers on August 28, 2025, and organized December 2025 protests against the 2026 minimum wage formula.

The opposition camp centers on **GEBRAK** (Gerakan Buruh Bersama Rakyat), a coalition of 35 critical civil society groups, with **KASBI** (Indonesian Trade Union Congress Alliance) as its largest member. GEBRAK's demands include repeal of the TNI Law. **KSBSI**, under President Elly Rosita, maintains independence from both camps, declining to participate in KSPI's December 2025 actions.

Key labor pressure points: 70,000+ layoffs in the first four months of 2025; Jakarta's 2026 minimum wage set at Rp 5.73 million ($341), below the Decent Living Needs threshold; Government Regulation 49/2025 on wages rejected by KSPI as suppressing wages; and an ongoing 2026 Workforce Law revision included in the National Legislative Program that could prove decisive for workers' rights.

### 13.4 — Religious mass organizations have been neutralized as democratic counterweights

The co-optation of **Nahdlatul Ulama** and **Muhammadiyah** — with a combined membership base exceeding **100 million** — represents perhaps the most consequential structural shift in Indonesia's civil society landscape. Per ISEAS FULCRUM (February 2026), Prabowo uses "both carrots and sticks" to secure "complete loyalty," resulting in "a deepening co-option of the two Islamic organisations that have long been considered a bastion of 'civil Islam.'"

NU secured 5 ministerial seats, while Muhammadiyah received 7 ministerial/deputy ministerial seats. Both organizations received **mining concessions** — NU's East Kalimantan concession was pressured to switch its business partner from a Garibaldi Thohir-linked company to one connected to Hashim Djojohadikusumo, Prabowo's brother. Simultaneously, KPK investigations target relatives of NU and Muhammadiyah leaders — NU chairman Yahya Cholil Staquf's brother Yaqut was charged in a hajj corruption scandal, while Muhammadiyah executives face corruption investigations. Prabowo pardoned a convicted Muhammadiyah executive. Both organizations endorsed Prabowo's controversial decision to join Trump's "Board of Peace" in February 2026.

The **212 movement** and its successor organizations, including hardline groups like Wahdah Islamiyah, now have friendly relations with Prabowo — a marked contrast from Jokowi's suppression of the FPI. The Islamist mobilization capacity that produced massive 2016–2019 protests has been effectively absorbed. As RSIS confirmed: "By 2025, major Islamic organisations and parties... all have become members of Prabowo's large political coalition."

### 13.5 — Digital civil society operates in an expanding surveillance environment

With over **167 million active social media users** and TikTok and Instagram each exceeding 100 million accounts, digital platforms have become the primary terrain of political contestation. Key hashtag campaigns — #IndonesiaGelap, #KaburAjaDulu, #TolakRUUTNI — organized and documented the 2025 protest cycle. TikTok became critical for protest documentation through live-streaming; 80% of TikTok Shop Indonesia transactions occur through live format.

Government response has been aggressive. TikTok Live was suspended during the August protests, and TikTok's operating license was temporarily suspended in October 2025 for refusing to provide user data on protest activity — restored only after the company complied. Meta and TikTok were summoned by the Ministry of Communication for content removal discussions. SAFEnet documented criminalization of online expression, doxxing, gender-based online violence, internet disruptions, and suspected sabotage of server optic cables. A student was arrested in May 2025 for posting a fabricated meme; a transgender TikTok user was sentenced to 34 months for a satirical video. The government employs paid political "buzzers" to manipulate online narratives.

A rare positive development: the Constitutional Court's April 2025 ruling restricted ITE Law defamation provisions to natural persons only, excluding government agencies and corporations, and narrowed "public unrest" provisions to exclude online spaces.

### 13.6 — International funding collapse creates existential threat to independent media and CSOs

The elimination of USAID — which provided **$153.52 million to Indonesia in 2024** and had invested approximately $800 million in welfare programs — combined with the suspension of NED's worldwide sponsorship of approximately 2,000 democracy groups, has created what amounts to a funding crisis for Indonesian civil society. Specific casualties include Remotivi (media NGO, lost USAID-linked grants), New Naratif (lost NED funding), and the $38.5 million ERAT governance program for anti-corruption and transparency. Independent media outlets are accelerating diversification toward membership fees, social entrepreneurship, and European donors. The proposed Foreign Propaganda Bill would further restrict foreign funding channels. Freedom House's Freedom on the Net 2025 scored Indonesia **48/100** (Partly Free, declining).

## SECTION 14: PATRON-CLIENT HISTORY AND ALLIANCE GENEALOGY

### 14.1 — Indonesia's alliance structure reflects seven decades of strategic hedging

Indonesia's external relationships have followed a consistent structural logic since independence: maximizing autonomy through diversified dependencies while avoiding formal alliance commitments. The *bebas dan aktif* (free and active) doctrine, articulated by Vice President Hatta in 1948, has been reinterpreted by each successive leader but never abandoned. Under Prabowo, it has mutated into what he calls **"omnidirectional diplomacy"** — a more transactional, leader-driven variant that simultaneously deepens engagement with the United States, China, Russia, and emerging middle powers.

**Cold War positioning** established the template. Sukarno's Indonesia hosted the Bandung Conference (1955), co-founded the Non-Aligned Movement, and pursued confrontation with Western-backed Malaysia. The PRRI/Permesta rebellion (1957–58), involving covert CIA support for regional separatists, left deep institutional distrust of American intentions. After 1965, Suharto's Western tilt embedded Indonesia in the IGGI/CGI aid architecture, with Japan as its largest ODA donor and the IMF/World Bank playing central roles. Post-1998, Western arms embargoes following the East Timor crisis forced defense diversification — the structural origin of Indonesia's current multi-sourcing approach.

### 14.2 — The US relationship is stagnating as Trump-era transactionalism replaces strategic investment

The US-Indonesia defense relationship, symbolized by **Super Garuda Shield** (the largest iteration in 2025 with 6,500+ personnel from 13 nations), retains institutional depth but lacks strategic momentum. The most significant development is negative: Boeing confirmed at the **Singapore Airshow 2026** that the **F-15EX campaign is "no longer active."** The $13.9 billion package — 24 aircraft with weapons, logistics, and training — never progressed beyond MoU stage. The deal's collapse reflects both fiscal mismatch (the package exceeded Indonesia's entire ~$9.5 billion defense budget in 2025) and competing procurement from France and Turkey.

Trump's "Liberation Day" tariffs hit Indonesia with a **32% country-specific tariff** in April 2025. Prabowo traveled to Mar-a-Lago to negotiate directly, agreeing to purchase $15 billion in US energy, $4.5 billion in agriculture, and 50 Boeing aircraft, bringing the tariff down to **19%**. The full Agreement on Reciprocal Trade was signed February 19, 2026, with Indonesia eliminating tariffs on 99% of US products, removing restrictions on critical mineral exports, and adopting forced labor import bans. Trump demanded Indonesia publicly announce a US surveillance drone purchase; Indonesia declined. Analyst assessment from The Diplomat (January 2026): "US-Indonesia Relations Stagnated in 2025" — the USAID closure gutted American Indonesia expertise and institutional relationships.

### 14.3 — China is Indonesia's indispensable economic partner and primary strategic dilemma

Bilateral trade reached a record **$135.15 billion in 2024**, making China Indonesia's largest trading partner by a wide margin. **Chinese firms control approximately 75% of Indonesia's nickel refining capacity**, with Tsingshan Holding Group and Jiangsu Delong alone accounting for over 70%. An estimated $30 billion in Chinese investment has flowed into downstream nickel, while Chinese state-led development finance to Indonesia totaled **$70 billion from 2000–2023**, over 90% as debt at market rates, with an additional $94.1 billion in private Chinese FDI.

The Natuna Sea remains the friction point. In October 2024, China sent coast guard vessels to disrupt Indonesian exploratory drilling. Prabowo's controversial November 2024 joint statement with Beijing — calling for "joint maritime development" in areas of "overlapping claims" — was widely interpreted as implicitly recognizing China's nine-dash line, prompting rapid Foreign Ministry clarification. In April 2025, Indonesia became China's first **Comprehensive Strategic Dialogue** partner in a 2+2 format.

Indonesia manages this asymmetry through three mechanisms: imposing tariffs of up to 200% on certain Chinese goods, attempting to restructure nickel deals to position Chinese companies as minority shareholders (to qualify for US IRA tax incentives), and maintaining robust Western defense ties as a counterbalance.

### 14.4 — France has emerged as Indonesia's most consequential new defense partner

The France-Indonesia defense partnership represents the most substantial structural shift in Indonesia's alliance map. **Three Rafale aircraft were delivered on January 27, 2026**, the first of an eventual 42 contracted in phased tranches worth approximately **$8.1 billion**. During Macron's May 28, 2025 state visit to Jakarta, a Letter of Intent was signed for an additional 12–24 Rafale jets, plus Scorpène submarines and light frigates. Two **Scorpène Evolved submarines** (valued at approximately $2 billion) are under contract with Naval Group, to be built at PT PAL shipyard in Surabaya with full technology transfer — Indonesia will be the test case for Naval Group's lithium-ion battery system. Naval Group established a subsidiary, PT Naval Group Nusantara, in July 2025. France also participates in Super Garuda Shield and Prabowo attended the Bastille Day parade in July 2025.

### 14.5 — The Australia-Indonesia security treaty marks a structural upgrade with inherent limits

The **Jakarta Treaty 2026**, substantively concluded during Prabowo's first state visit to Australia in November 2025 and formally signed in January 2026, commits both parties to "consult at Leader and Ministerial level on a regular basis about matters affecting their common security." It is explicitly **not** a mutual defense pact — if either country's security is threatened, they will "consult and consider what measures may be taken either individually or jointly." The treaty replaces the 1995 Keating-Soeharto security agreement torn up after Australia's role in East Timor's independence. Analyst assessment from ASPI: Australia should not overestimate closeness — the relationship is "not akin to those it has with close non-allied friends as Japan or Canada."

### 14.6 — Russia and BRICS signal Prabowo's structural reorientation toward multipolarity

Prabowo's **June 2025 visit to St. Petersburg** — where he met Putin, signed a Declaration on Strategic Partnership, and keynoted the SPIEF while skipping the G7 Summit in Canada — represents the clearest signal of reorientation. The partnership covers agriculture, space exploration, energy, and military-technical cooperation. A **€2.29 billion joint investment fund** links Danantara with Russia's RDIF. Prabowo returned to Moscow in December 2025, inviting Putin to visit Indonesia. Reports of Russia seeking airbase access at Biak Island (Papua) were denied by Indonesia but alarmed Australia.

Indonesia became a **full BRICS member on January 6, 2025** — the first Southeast Asian nation admitted. Prabowo simultaneously pursues OECD membership (application submitted June 2025) and CPTPP accession (applied September 2024), embodying his dictum: "Indonesia must be everywhere — in the North, in the South, in all platforms."

### 14.7 — Emerging middle power partnerships reflect defense diversification logic

**Turkey** has emerged as a major new defense partner. At Indo Defence 2025 (June 2025), Indonesia signed for **48 KAAN fifth-generation fighters** (valued at approximately $10 billion) and **60 Bayraktar TB3 drones**, becoming the first international customer for the Khan tactical ballistic missile. The KAAN deal carries a significant caveat: Indonesia stipulated aircraft must be "completely free of US export controls" — challenging given US-supplied engines, with Turkey's domestic replacement not expected before 2036.

**South Korea's** KF-21 Boramae program was restructured in June 2025, reducing Indonesia's cost share from 1.6 trillion won to **600 billion won (~$438 million)** for 48 aircraft, with barter arrangements including commodities. The partnership was strained by a January 2024 incident involving Indonesian engineers caught attempting to steal classified KF-21 technical data.

**India** confirmed a **BrahMos missile deal** in March 2026 — three coastal batteries valued at approximately $300–450 million, making Indonesia the second foreign BrahMos buyer after the Philippines. Prabowo was Chief Guest at India's 75th Republic Day (January 2025).

**Japan** remains Indonesia's largest cumulative aid donor with approximately 2,000 companies employing 7.2 million Indonesians. Security cooperation is expanding through discussions on joint Mogami-class frigate development and Official Security Assistance grants.

### 14.8 — Prabowo's diplomatic style is hyperactive, personalized, and strategically ambiguous

Prabowo conducted **32 foreign visits to 22 countries** in his first year — more than any recent predecessor. His first trip as president was to Beijing (November 2024), followed immediately by Washington — a deliberate signal sequence. He describes foreign leaders as "close friends" and "brothers," employing the Chinese proverb "a thousand friends are too few, one enemy is too many." His worldview centers on realist maxims: "The strong will rule and the weak will suffer." Lowy Institute characterizes the approach as "diplomacy as a search for position rather than purpose... selective alignment, building partnerships issue by issue." Critics describe a foreign policy that is "hyperactive" but lacks coherence — "visibility without vision" — with institutional coordination gaps exposed by joint statement controversies with China and unilateral diplomatic initiatives.

## SECTION 15: CONSTITUTIONAL CRISES AND REGIME TRANSITION PATTERNS

### 15.1 — Indonesia's regime transitions follow a recognizable pattern with a critical variable

Each Indonesian regime transition has featured three consistent elements: **(1)** the military as kingmaker or constraint, **(2)** mass mobilization as trigger, and **(3)** elite bargaining determining outcomes. The variable factor is whether military factions split — when they did (1998), the regime fell; when they held (1965–66), the regime was remade under military control.

The 1999–2002 constitutional amendments fundamentally transformed the system: presidential term limits, direct elections, a Constitutional Court, human rights provisions, and removal of military/police appointed legislative seats. Only 11% of the original constitution survived unchanged. The constitutional order has not been formally amended since 2002, but it is being structurally altered through ordinary legislation — particularly the TNI Law and Danantara SOE framework — that achieves structural change without constitutional amendment.

A critical 2000 bargain embedded in the reformasi settlement separated police from military but secured the military's key demand: no retroactive prosecution for past human rights violations, encoded in Article 28(1). This remains a fundamental constraint on democratic consolidation.

### 15.2 — The TNI Law represents the most significant reversal of post-1998 military reform

The DPR **unanimously passed** amendments to the TNI Law on March 20, 2025, expanding the number of civilian positions open to active-duty military officers from **10 to 14 institutions** — adding the Attorney General's Office, BNPB (disaster agency), BNPT (counterterrorism), Bakamla (maritime security), and BNPP (border management). Article 7 now authorizes TNI to conduct "military operations other than war" — including cyberthreats, infrastructure security, and local government assistance — **without DPR consent**, requiring only presidential authorization. Retirement ages were raised from 53 to 58 for enlisted and 58 to 60 for generals. Deliberations were held at a luxury hotel rather than parliament. Imparsial reported at least **2,569 active-duty officers** already serving in civilian roles before the amendments, often outside legal frameworks. The law normalizes and entrenches what scholars characterize as a de facto return to Suharto-era *dwifungsi*.

### 15.3 — The Constitutional Court crisis destroyed the judiciary's credibility as democratic safeguard

The October 16, 2023 ruling enabling Gibran Rakabuming Raka's vice-presidential candidacy was the most consequential judicial decision since Reformasi. The Court ruled 5–4 to create an exception to the 40-year minimum age requirement for candidates who had held or been elected to public office. Chief Justice **Anwar Usman** — who had married Jokowi's sister in May 2022, making him Gibran's uncle — failed to recuse himself. The Ethics Council found Anwar guilty of "serious ethical violation" of five principles but only dismissed him as Chief Justice while allowing him to remain as ordinary justice. The ruling itself was preserved intact. Just six days after the decision, Prabowo announced Gibran as his running mate.

### 15.4 — The mega-coalition and centralized decision-making eliminate structural checks

The **KIM Plus coalition** holds **470 of 580 DPR seats (81%)**, with only PDI-P's 110 seats outside — and even PDI-P has not adopted formal opposition, with Speaker Puan Maharani leading passage of the TNI Law. This virtual unanimity eliminates legislative oversight.

Prabowo's cabinet of **111+ appointees** — the largest since Sukarno's 132-minister cabinet in 1966 — includes at least **23 retired military/police officers**, with 9 of 16 ex-army appointees having Kopassus backgrounds and personal links to Prabowo. His inner circle consists predominantly of Taruna Nusantara alumni (the elite semi-military high school he helped establish in 1990) and Gerindra cadres. Key figures include State Secretary Prasetyo Hadi, Defense Minister Sjafrie Sjamsoeddin (military academy classmate implicated in 1998 crackdowns), and brother Hashim Djojohadikusumo as primary financier.

Power centralization operates through multiple mechanisms: budget decree authority (the January 2025 freeze redirecting Rp 300+ trillion), **Danantara** (managing $172+ billion in state assets with no independent audit mechanism), fiscal recentralization (regional transfers cut approximately one-third from 2024 levels), and the September 2025 cabinet reshuffle that removed Jokowi loyalists including Finance Minister Sri Mulyani Indrawati.

Vice President **Gibran Rakabuming Raka**, at 37 Indonesia's youngest-ever VP, has been marginalized. By May 2025, he was described as an "invisible VP" excluded from strategic meetings and cabinet reshuffles. Prabowo sent Jokowi, not Gibran, to Pope Francis's funeral — a role normally reserved for the VP. Retired military officers' Forum formally petitioned for Gibran's dismissal, though the DPR took no action.

LP3ES assessed Indonesia is "experiencing a reversal towards authoritarianism throughout 2025" — "gradual, systematic, and legitimized decline." Marcus Mietzner (ANU) identifies a pattern where elite collusion "both enables and limits executive aggrandizement" — elites produce backsliding but set limits (they rejected Jokowi's term-extension bid), protecting from "full collapse" while producing "gradual decline, reducing chances of democratic revitalization."

The evidence supports a characterization of **accelerating democratic erosion approaching — but not yet reaching — authoritarian consolidation**. Constraining factors include: elite competition between Prabowo and Jokowi factions, civil society protest resilience (five major waves in 2025), the Constitutional Court's occasional independence (April 2025 ITE ruling), international scrutiny, and constitutional term limits.

## SECTION 16: COLLECTIVE MEMORY OF HUMILIATION AND GRIEVANCE

### 16.1 — The 1965 massacres are being actively rehabilitated rather than reckoned with

The estimated 500,000–1 million killed in the 1965–66 anti-communist purges represent Indonesia's deepest unresolved trauma. Under Prabowo, the trajectory has shifted from suppression to active **historical rehabilitation of the perpetrators**. On November 10, 2025 (Heroes Day), Prabowo conferred the **national hero title on Suharto** via Presidential Decree — HRW's Usman Hamid called it "utterly absurd" given Suharto's responsibility for "one of history's greatest genocides." Culture Minister Fadli Zon commissioned a **10-volume national history series** that presents the 1965–67 mass killings simply as a "resolution" of the 30 September Movement. Made Supriatma (ISEAS) compared it to Suharto's 1975 state history project. Indonesia at Melbourne assessed that Prabowo has "politicised history to an extent not seen since the New Order."

YPKP 65 has identified **359 mass graves** across Indonesia. Communism/Marxism remain legally banned. The 2004 curriculum revision acknowledging the killings was reversed in 2006, with revised textbooks ordered burned. The International People's Tribunal (2015–16) found Indonesia guilty of crimes against humanity and concluded the massacres "could be categorised as genocide," but the verdict had no legal consequences.

Prabowo's personal stakes are direct: his former father-in-law was Suharto; his military career included Kopassus operations marked by civilian massacres in Papua; and he was dismissed from the military in 1998 for kidnapping student activists. The historian Asvi Warman Adam predicted the textbook rewrite will ensure "the New Order will only be a historical account of the successes in development... this record will also erase the cases involving Prabowo in 1998."

### 16.2 — The colonial narrative provides the ideological substrate for multipolarity

The "350 years of colonization" narrative — historically simplified, as full Dutch control over the entire archipelago was achieved only by the early 20th century — functions as the foundational myth of Indonesian sovereignty. Under Prabowo, this narrative directly informs BRICS membership, resource nationalism, skepticism of Western trade conditions, and multi-alignment strategy. At the 2025 SPIEF, Prabowo explicitly invoked colonialism: "For the past 30 years, we have witnessed the dominance of neoliberal economic philosophy and classical free-market capitalism." His inaugural speech centered "anti-colonialism, sovereignty, and self-sufficiency."

The colonial narrative fuels **resource nationalism** across partisan lines. Eve Warburton (Lowy Institute/Cornell) documented how it connects to "popular notions of economic sovereignty," linking land and subsoil resources to national citizens and making normative claims about who should control and benefit from resources. The EU's deforestation regulations and US tariffs are routinely characterized as neo-colonialism.

Notably absent from collective memory: Japan's WWII occupation. A Springer Nature study found the occupation "is largely absent from Indonesian historical consciousness." Japan enabled independence, trained the military, and established administrative structures — the occupation is remembered instrumentally, not as trauma.

### 16.3 — The May 1998 riots remain a taboo subject with latent activation potential

The May 1998 riots — 1,200+ burned to death, 8,500 buildings destroyed, 130+ documented rapes of Chinese-Indonesian women in Jakarta — have **never been criminally investigated**. No prosecutions, no redress. Weekly "Kamisan" protests by mothers of victims have continued outside the presidential palace for over 17 years. Chinese-Indonesians commemorated the 27th anniversary in May 2025 with vows to "resist forgetting."

The textbook rewrite project is expected to omit Prabowo's direct implication in the 1998 kidnapping of student activists that preceded the riots. Anti-Chinese sentiment was weaponized politically in the 2016–2017 campaign against Jakarta Governor Ahok. The military's documented role in fomenting anti-Chinese violence in 1998 — as a "frame-shifting strategy" to distract from its failure to control student protests — established a dangerous precedent that remains available for future activation during economic crises.

### 16.4 — Papua represents an active colonial war framed as counterinsurgency

The situation in Papua has **dramatically worsened** under Prabowo. Human Rights Monitor reported "an unusually high number of torture cases" in Q1 2025, aerial bombardments near civilian settlements in Intan Jaya, and at least **86,886 internally displaced persons**. TNI General Agus Subiyanto announced a new war doctrine in January 2025 including drones and helicopter gunships for bombing remote highlands. The Merauke Strategic National Project plans to develop 2 million hectares of customary land for agriculture without free, prior, informed consent, with implementation handed to the TNI. West Papua remains closed to international observers, UN agencies, and independent journalists.

Papua's domestic framing serves a dual function: the NKRI (Unitary State) territorial integrity narrative makes any external attention to Papua equivalent to foreign interference, while the resource extraction logic (Freeport gold/copper, Wabu Block gold reserves of 2.3 million kg) drives continued military presence. Prabowo's personal history compounds the issue — he led 1996 military operations in Papua marked by civilian massacres.

### 16.5 — Islamic political grievance has been domesticated but religious intolerance persists

The Jakarta Charter's "seven words" — demanding sharia obligation for Muslim adherents — were removed from the constitution on its first day. This generated a foundational grievance that drove the Darul Islam rebellion and Islamic party politics through the 20th century. By 2024, however, this grievance has been **effectively domesticated**: transactional coalition-building takes priority over ideology, NU issued a 2019 declaration explicitly rejecting the caliphate ideal, and Prabowo absorbed Islamic organizations into his patronage network rather than mobilizing them oppositionally.

### 16.6 — Grievance narratives constrain foreign policy through three mechanisms

**Sovereignty maximalism**: The colonial narrative, Papua framing, and East Timor experience combine to produce reflexive resistance to any external involvement in domestic affairs — constraining human rights cooperation, international monitoring, and accountability mechanisms.

**Anti-Western hedging**: Colonial memory provides domestic legitimacy for BRICS membership, Russia partnership, and multi-alignment, while making exclusive alignment with Western institutions politically costly.

**Resource nationalism**: The "350 years" narrative produces broad bipartisan support for state-led, domestically owned resource exploitation models — constraining the terms on which foreign investment (particularly Chinese investment in nickel) can operate.

## SECTION 17: CROSS-FACET INTERSECTION ANALYSIS

### 17.1 — Nickel dependency creates an environmental-sovereignty-alliance trilemma

Chinese firms control **75% of Indonesia's nickel refining capacity**, creating a structural dependency that constrains three domains simultaneously. Indonesia cannot enforce environmental regulations at Chinese smelters — where 60+ deaths/accidents were linked to Tsingshan operations in 2023 alone — without risking capital flight from the sector that generates $38–40 billion in export revenues. It cannot pivot away from Chinese economic dependence even while hedging militarily through French, Turkish, and Indian procurement. And it cannot comply with emerging Western ESG and supply chain due diligence requirements (EU deforestation regulation, US forced labor import bans from the February 2026 trade deal) without restructuring Chinese-dominated production chains. Government Regulation No. 28/2025 froze new nickel smelter investment and raised royalties from 10% to 14–19% — a late attempt to reassert control. But the February 2026 US trade deal's requirement to remove restrictions on critical mineral exports to the United States directly contradicts the downstreaming policy that was supposed to capture value domestically. **The nickel sector is the point where Indonesia's economic nationalism, environmental governance, and alliance hedging collide irreconcilably.**

### 17.2 — The archipelagic information gap enables selective authoritarian deepening

Indonesia's 17,000-island geography creates structural information asymmetries that Prabowo's centralization exploits. Digital civil society and protest movements concentrate overwhelmingly on Java — BEM SI coordination operates through Java-centric university networks, while TikTok live-streaming and Instagram mobilization require bandwidth that outer island populations lack. When authorities suspended TikTok during August 2025 and implemented internet blackouts, the information impact was asymmetric: Java-based protesters could shift to alternative platforms and media coverage, while peripheral communities lost access entirely. The TNI Law's expansion of military "operations other than war" to include cyberthreats and infrastructure security acquires different meaning in Papua (where West Papua remains closed to independent journalists) than in Jakarta (where international media presence constrains repression). **Democratic erosion proceeds at different speeds across the archipelago, with the most severe repression occurring in the most information-poor zones.**

### 17.3 — The demographic dividend is being militarized rather than realized

With **42% of the population under 25**, real wages declining 1.1% annually over 2018–2024, and 68% of the population living near poverty by revised World Bank standards, Indonesia's youth bulge is becoming a destabilizer rather than an economic dividend. Prabowo's response has been to channel this energy through two parallel structures: the **MBG school meal program** (Rp 335 trillion allocated for 2026, absorbing 8.8% of the budget) as a patronage delivery mechanism reaching 30+ million beneficiaries, and **military expansion** — dozens of new military units, six new regional commands, defense budget rising from $8.8 billion to $11.8 billion — as both employment program and control infrastructure. The 73.3% cut to the Ministry of Public Works and regional transfer reductions shift resources from productive infrastructure to consumption (MBG) and coercion (military), structurally undermining the conditions for the demographic dividend to materialize. **The August 2025 protests — triggered by elite privilege amid mass layoffs — are the leading indicator of this failure.**

### 17.4 — Religious co-optation removes the historical circuit-breaker for authoritarian overreach

In every previous Indonesian regime crisis, religious mass organizations played a critical role in constraining or channeling dissent. NU and Muhammadiyah provided organizational infrastructure for the 1998 movement; they served as negotiation intermediaries between students and the military; their moral authority set limits on state violence. Prabowo's systematic co-optation through ministerial seats, mining concessions, and selective KPK investigations has **eliminated this circuit-breaker**. The organizations are now materially dependent on the regime and actively deployed against civil society — ISEAS describes them as "forced to challenge the progressive forces within civil society." If economic crisis generates mass mobilization on the scale of 1998, the traditional institutional intermediaries that prevented total state breakdown will be absent. **This intersection between religious co-optation and democratic erosion is the single most underappreciated structural risk.**

### 17.5 — The 1965 memory project simultaneously delegitimizes dissent and enables BRICS alignment

The Suharto rehabilitation project and "antek-antek asing" (foreign lackeys) rhetoric against civil society draw directly from the 1965 anti-communist playbook — external enemies corrupting domestic order through domestic proxies. This narrative framework performs double duty: it delegitimizes democratic opposition by associating it with foreign manipulation (reproducing the PKI framing), while simultaneously providing ideological cover for alignment with Russia and China, who share an institutional interest in discrediting Western-funded civil society. The €2.29 billion Danantara-RDIF joint investment fund and the proposed Foreign Propaganda Bill are two faces of the same structural logic: **strengthening non-Western economic ties while closing the space for Western-linked civil society organizations.** The textbook rewrite project literally constructs the historical foundation for this reorientation.

### 17.6 — Colonial land tenure patterns perpetuate the Papua conflict through resource extraction logic

Dutch colonial-era extractive frameworks persist in Papua through direct institutional inheritance. The concession model — external capital extracting resources from indigenous territories under state military protection — maps directly from VOC-era spice extraction through Suharto's Freeport contract to Prabowo's Merauke Strategic National Project. The 2 million hectares of customary Marind Anim land being developed for rice and sugarcane without FPIC, with implementation handed to the TNI, reproduces the colonial structure with different flags. The proposed amnesty for independence fighters (January 2025), conditioned on pledging loyalty to Indonesia, mirrors colonial-era "pacification" offers. **Papua is where Indonesia's anti-colonial identity narrative and its colonial-pattern resource extraction create the sharpest internal contradiction** — one that the sovereignty maximalism of the NKRI framework prevents from being acknowledged, let alone resolved.

## SECTION 18: KEY ANALYTICAL JUDGMENTS

**Judgment 1: Indonesia will not recover "Free" democracy status during Prabowo's presidency.**
*Confidence: HIGH (90%)*

The structural conditions for democratic recovery are absent: the mega-coalition eliminates legislative checks, the Constitutional Court's legitimacy is compromised, religious mass organizations are co-opted, the TNI Law entrenches military civilian roles, and the proposed Foreign Propaganda Bill would further constrict civil society. The EIU, Freedom House, and V-Dem trajectories all show continuous decline over a decade. Elite competition constrains full authoritarian consolidation but does not produce democratic recovery.

*Falsification condition:* Freedom House upgrades Indonesia to "Free" (score above 72) or V-Dem Liberal Democracy Index returns above 0.50 before October 2029.

**Judgment 2: The Prabowo mega-coalition will fragment before the 2029 elections, triggered by distributional conflict over Danantara resources or fiscal stress.**
*Confidence: MODERATE-HIGH (75%)*

The coalition's cohesion depends on patronage distribution. Danantara's $172+ billion in managed assets and mining concessions provide the material basis, but Fitch and Moody's negative outlook revisions (March 2026), the statutory 3% deficit ceiling, and the MBG program consuming 8.8% of the 2026 budget create fiscal pressure that will force distributional choices. The September 2025 removal of Jokowi loyalists demonstrates that coalition management requires periodic exclusion — each exclusion creates a potential defector. If GDP growth falls below 4.5% for two consecutive quarters, the patronage base contracts below what the coalition requires.

*Falsification condition:* KIM Plus maintains >75% DPR seat control through the 2029 election without a major party defection, or Danantara generates sufficient returns to avoid fiscal trade-offs.

**Judgment 3: Chinese share of Indonesian nickel refining will exceed 80% by 2028 absent active industrial policy intervention that Prabowo is structurally unlikely to implement.**
*Confidence: MODERATE (65%)*

Government Regulation 28/2025 freezing new smelter investment and the royalty increase (10% to 14–19%) represent a late effort at reassertion, but 54 operating smelters, 38 under construction, and 45 in planning are predominantly Chinese-financed. Indonesian firms own only approximately 10% of refined capacity. The February 2026 US trade deal's critical mineral export provisions may actually accelerate Chinese processing dominance by increasing demand for Indonesian refined nickel.

*Falsification condition:* Indonesian-owned share of refined nickel capacity rises above 20% by 2028, or government successfully restructures majority-Chinese smelters into minority-Chinese ownership.

**Judgment 4: The civil society funding gap from USAID elimination will not be filled by alternative sources within three years, producing permanent organizational attrition.**
*Confidence: HIGH (85%)*

USAID provided $153.52 million annually; NED sponsored approximately 2,000 democracy groups globally. European donors, while willing, lack the scale and institutional infrastructure to replace these flows. The proposed Foreign Propaganda Bill would further constrain foreign funding channels. Indonesian independent media are attempting diversification to membership and social enterprise models, but these generate a fraction of previous funding. The organizational capacity destroyed — particularly in governance, anti-corruption, and media independence — takes decades to rebuild.

*Falsification condition:* Alternative funding sources (European governments, private philanthropy, membership models) reach 75% of previous USAID/NED levels within 36 months.

**Judgment 5: Prabowo's defense procurement diversification will generate an interoperability crisis by 2028–2030.**
*Confidence: MODERATE-HIGH (75%)*

Simultaneous procurement from France (Rafale, Scorpène), Turkey (KAAN, Bayraktar, Khan), South Korea (KF-21), India (BrahMos), and legacy Russian platforms (Su-27/30) creates a logistics, maintenance, and interoperability challenge that exceeds Indonesia's institutional capacity. There has been no updated Defense White Paper since 2015. Analysts describe the acquisition strategy as "haphazard and fragmented." The KAAN deal's stipulation that aircraft be free of US export controls — impossible before Turkey's domestic engine by 2036 at earliest — suggests some agreements function as diplomatic signaling rather than operational procurement.

*Falsification condition:* Indonesia publishes a Defense White Paper with integrated platform strategy, or successfully operates 4+ fighter types simultaneously at stated readiness levels.

**Judgment 6: A major protest cycle exceeding August 2025 in scale will occur before 2028, triggered by the intersection of economic grievance and governance failure.**
*Confidence: MODERATE-HIGH (70%)*

Five major protest waves in 2025 alone establish a pattern of escalating frequency. The structural triggers persist: fiscal austerity outside the MBG program, mass layoffs in manufacturing (textile sector collapse), real wage decline, elite self-dealing visible through Danantara, and the absence of institutional channels for grievance resolution. The 2025 protests demonstrated that leaderless, social media-coordinated movements can rapidly scale. The removal of religious organization intermediaries (Judgment 4 of Section 17) increases the risk that future protests lack moderation mechanisms.

*Falsification condition:* GDP growth exceeds 6% sustained, real wages increase, and no protest wave exceeding 20 cities occurs before December 2027.

**Judgment 7: Indonesia will attempt to mediate or host a significant international peace initiative by 2027, leveraging BRICS membership, ASEAN chair ambitions, and Prabowo's "everywhere" doctrine.**
*Confidence: MODERATE (60%)*

Prabowo's diplomatic hyperactivity — 32 visits to 22 countries in year one — and his framing of Indonesia as a bridge between Global North and South create institutional pressure for a signature diplomatic achievement. The Gaza evacuation offer (1,000 Palestinians), the Russia strategic partnership, and BRICS membership position Indonesia as a potential mediator. The risk is that "visibility without vision" produces a high-profile initiative that lacks follow-through.

*Falsification condition:* Indonesia does not host, convene, or actively mediate a significant multilateral peace process by December 2027.

**1. Danantara transparency trajectory**
*Observable:* Audit access by BPK/BPKP/KPK; publication of portfolio returns; Patriot Bond subscription rates; credit rating agency commentary on SOE governance.
*Threshold:* If Danantara operates for 18+ months without independent audit, treat as confirming authoritarian fiscal capture. Patriot Bond subscription below 50% of Rp 50 trillion target signals investor resistance.

**2. Foreign Propaganda Bill legislative progress**
*Observable:* Inclusion in Prolegnas priority list; DPR committee assignment; draft text circulation; civil society mobilization response.
*Threshold:* Entry into the 2026 or 2027 priority list signals acceleration. Passage would represent the most significant legal threat to civil society since the Ormas Law amendment.
*Channel:* DPR legislative tracker, Jakarta Post parliamentary coverage, ICNL regulatory tracker.

**3. Constitutional Court rulings on TNI Law challenges**
*Observable:* Docket assignment, oral arguments, ruling timeline, composition of panel.
*Threshold:* Upholding the law confirms judicial capitulation. Partial strike-down (e.g., limiting OMSP scope) signals residual institutional independence. Full strike-down (extremely unlikely) would indicate elite fracture.
*Channel:* MK (Constitutional Court) website, academic commentary, Tempo/Kompas reporting.

**4. Student protest coordination evolution**

*Channel:* Social media monitoring (Twitter/X, TikTok, Instagram), BEM SI statements, university media.

*Channel:* Finance ministry budget documents, SIPRI database, Janes Defence Budgets.

**7. Nickel sector ownership restructuring**
*Observable:* Indonesian vs. Chinese ownership shares in operating smelters; enforcement of Regulation 28/2025; US IRA qualification negotiations; new Indonesian-led ventures.
*Threshold:* If Indonesian-owned share rises above 15%, the resource nationalism agenda is gaining traction. If Chinese share exceeds 80%, dependency deepens beyond manageable levels.
*Channel:* BKPM (Investment Ministry) data, industry publications (Mining.com, Reuters), C4ADS tracking.

**8. Papua conflict intensity and access**
*Observable:* IDP numbers, extrajudicial killing reports, international journalist/NGO access requests, UNGA/UNHRC statements.
*Threshold:* IDP numbers exceeding 100,000 or confirmed use of armed drones against civilian areas would signal qualitative escalation. Any grant of international media access would signal strategic softening.
*Channel:* Human Rights Monitor quarterly reports, TAPOL, HRW, ULMWP statements, satellite imagery analysis.

**9. NU/Muhammadiyah leadership elections (NU congress July/August 2026)**
*Observable:* Candidate platforms, level of state interference, outcome relative to Prabowo preferences.
*Threshold:* If Yahya Cholil Staquf is replaced by a more independent figure despite Prabowo's preference, this signals limits to religious co-optation. If a regime loyalist consolidates control, it confirms Section 17.4 assessment.
*Channel:* NU official statements, ISEAS analysis, Indonesian religious media.

**10. OECD accession milestones vs. BRICS institutional engagement**
*Observable:* OECD review committee reports, legislative compliance assessments, BRICS NDB lending volumes, attendance/initiative levels at BRICS summits.
*Threshold:* If OECD accession stalls while BRICS engagement deepens (particularly NDB lending exceeding $5 billion), Indonesia's structural orientation toward the non-Western institutional order is confirmed. Simultaneous progress on both suggests the "everywhere" doctrine is sustainable.
*Channel:* OECD accession tracker, NDB annual reports, BRICS summit communiqués.

*Observable:* RSF annual ranking, AJI violence tracking, journalist imprisonment numbers, media outlet closures.
*Threshold:* RSF ranking below 140 (from current 127) signals approaching crisis comparable to Suharto-era conditions. More than 5 journalists imprisoned simultaneously would represent qualitative escalation.
*Channel:* RSF annual report, AJI annual survey, CPJ imprisonment tracker.

**12. Rupiah exchange rate and capital flow patterns**
*Observable:* IDR/USD rate, foreign portfolio flows, Bank Indonesia intervention frequency, foreign reserve levels.
*Threshold:* Sustained rupiah depreciation beyond 17,000/USD combined with net foreign outflows exceeding $5 billion per quarter would signal loss of investor confidence that constrains Prabowo's fiscal maneuver space and could trigger the coalition-fracturing distributional conflict of Judgment 2.

## SECTION 20: PIPELINE INTEGRATION NOTES

### 20.1 — Source interpretation guidance for Indonesian media

**Tier 1 (Highest analytical value):** Tempo (Indonesia's premier investigative outlet; has faced severed pig heads and dead rats as intimidation, confirming its independence), Tirto.id (data-driven journalism, digital-native), Kompas (establishment paper but maintains editorial standards), and Jakarta Post (English-language; important for international positioning signals — what the government wants external audiences to see).

**Tier 2 (Valuable with calibration):** CNN Indonesia (Trans Corp ownership creates oligarch linkage; useful for government messaging interpretation), Kumparan (digital platform, younger audience), Mongabay Indonesia (environmental reporting). Detik.com has high traffic but is aggregation-heavy with limited original analysis.

**Tier 3 (Indicator value only):** Government-aligned media (ANTARA news agency, TVRI) are useful primarily as indicators of official narrative — divergence between ANTARA framing and Tempo/Jakarta Post framing on the same event signals contested terrain. Social media monitoring of pro-government "buzzers" provides early warning of narrative campaigns.

**Key calibration:** Self-censorship affects **80% of Indonesian journalists** per AJI surveys. Absence of coverage on sensitive topics (Papua operations, Danantara finances, 1965 history) is itself an indicator. The ITE Law's criminalization of online expression means that digital sources exercise significant self-restraint. Tempo's willingness to publish despite intimidation makes it the single most reliable domestic source.

**International analytical sources:** ISEAS-Yusof Ishak Institute (Singapore) produces the most consistent, high-quality Indonesia analysis — its Perspective series and FULCRUM platform are essential. Indonesia at Melbourne (University of Melbourne) provides rigorous academic analysis with Indonesia specialist depth. New Mandala (ANU) offers critical political analysis. East Asia Forum publishes timely Indonesia commentary. Lowy Institute provides Australian-perspective strategic assessment. CSIS Jakarta is well-connected to Indonesian policy elites but may reflect proximity to power.

### 20.2 — Event significance thresholds

**Routine (expected fluctuation):** Individual protest events in fewer than 5 cities; cabinet-level personnel changes among deputy ministers; bilateral MoU signings without binding commitments; quarterly GDP variation within 4.5–5.5% band; individual press freedom incidents.

**Elevated (monitor closely):** Protests spreading to 10+ cities simultaneously; credit rating outlook changes; Constitutional Court decisions on TNI Law or other structural legislation; defense procurement contract signings (vs. LoIs/MoUs); Danantara audit requests or denials; major foreign leader visits with substantive agreements; rupiah crossing 17,000/USD; any Papua access concession.

**Critical (potential structural shift):** Credit rating downgrade to sub-investment grade; mega-coalition party defection; military faction split indicators (public disagreement among senior commanders); protests exceeding August 2025 in scale (50+ cities, fatalities exceeding 15); passage of Foreign Propaganda Bill; Constitutional Court dissolution or restructuring; Prabowo health incident; sustained GDP below 4% for two quarters.

### 20.3 — Common misreading patterns

**Misreading 1: Treating Indonesia's multi-alignment as strategic incoherence.** Western analysts frequently characterize Prabowo's simultaneous engagement with the US, China, Russia, France, and Turkey as contradictory or evidence of foreign policy confusion. This misreads the structural logic. Multi-sourcing is Indonesia's dominant strategy precisely because it maximizes autonomy — the "thousand friends" approach is coherent within its own framework, even if it produces tactical contradictions (the joint China statement, the SPIEF-over-G7 choice). The correct frame is not "alliance choice" but "dependency avoidance."

**Misreading 2: Projecting Western democratic recession frameworks onto Indonesia.** Indonesia is not Hungary or Turkey. The mega-coalition does not rest on ideological polarization but on patronage distribution. The constraint on authoritarian consolidation is not opposition party strength but elite competition — the same oligarchic structure that enables backsliding also prevents any single actor from achieving hegemonic control. Prabowo cannot complete what Orbán completed because he must continuously buy off coalition partners who retain their own resource bases.

**Misreading 3: Underestimating protest capacity because movements lack institutional form.** The five protest waves of 2025 demonstrate that leaderless, social media-coordinated movements can achieve scale rapidly without the organizational infrastructure that Western analysts look for. The absence of formal opposition party leadership or labor union coordination does not indicate weakness — it indicates a different mobilization model that is harder to decapitate but also harder to sustain.

**Misreading 4: Assuming BRICS membership signals anti-Western alignment.** Indonesia's simultaneous OECD application undermines this reading. BRICS membership is better understood as status acquisition and institutional diversification rather than alignment choice. The risk is not that Indonesia "joins the anti-Western bloc" but that it becomes a free-rider in all institutions simultaneously, contributing to none while extracting benefits from each.

**Misreading 5: Conflating defense procurement volume with military capability.** Indonesia's $50+ billion in announced defense deals across 7+ supplier nations will not translate proportionally into combat power. Interoperability challenges, maintenance bottleneck risks, human capital constraints, and the absence of a Defense White Paper since 2015 mean that headline procurement numbers systematically overstate actual capability acquisition. Many agreements remain at LoI/MoU stage; Indonesia's procurement history shows frequent cancellation and renegotiation.

### 20.4 — Cross-dossier connection points with other middle powers

**Australia:** The Jakarta Treaty 2026 is the primary structural connection. Watch for: Australian intelligence assessments of Indonesia-Russia ties (particularly Biak airbase reports), AUKUS reassurance dynamics, Australia-Indonesia economic integration depth (Danantara-Australian investment flows), shared Papua equities (Australia's historical Papua/PNG role creates friction), and the structural tension between Australia's alliance system and Indonesia's multi-alignment.

**Japan:** Indonesia's largest cumulative aid donor and third-largest trade partner with 2,000 companies employing 7.2 million Indonesians. The Mogami-class frigate joint development discussions represent a potential new structural link. Watch for: Japan's FOIP strategy integration, Official Security Assistance flows, competition with China for infrastructure investment, and Japan-Indonesia economic partnership evolution as Japan seeks supply chain diversification from China.

**South Korea:** The KF-21 Boramae program creates a 25+ year structural dependency. The espionage incident (January 2024) and cost-share renegotiation reveal the fragility of defense-industrial partnerships with Indonesia. Watch for: IF-21 delivery timelines, technology transfer depth, and whether the barter-for-commodities model becomes a template for other middle power defense relationships.

**India:** The BrahMos deal (confirmed March 2026) opens a new defense relationship vector. Both countries are BRICS members, both hedge between the US and China, and both are major commodity players. Watch for: deepening Quad-adjacent cooperation dynamics, India-Indonesia maritime security cooperation in the Indian Ocean/Malacca Strait interface, and competition/cooperation in nickel supply chains as India seeks battery material security.

**France:** The most consequential new defense partner. The Rafale and Scorpène programs create 30+ year platform dependencies with full technology transfer commitments. Watch for: France's Indo-Pacific strategy integration, Naval Group's PT Naval Group Nusantara as a local industrial footprint, and whether France displaces the US as Indonesia's preferred Western defense partner — which would represent a significant structural shift in the regional security architecture.

**Turkey:** The KAAN deal's viability is the critical question. If the aircraft cannot be freed from US export controls, the agreement functions as diplomatic signaling rather than defense acquisition. Watch for: Turkish engine development timeline, Baykar drone factory construction in Indonesia (Baykar-Republikorp joint venture), and whether Turkey becomes a sustained defense partner or an episodic deal counterpart.

**Brazil:** Both are large, resource-rich, middle-income BRICS members with democratic backsliding trajectories. Brazil participated in Super Garuda Shield 2025. Watch for: BRICS NDB co-borrowing dynamics, commodity market coordination (palm oil/soy competition), and parallel democratic erosion patterns that may create mutual legitimation dynamics.

**South Africa:** BRICS co-membership creates institutional connection. Limited bilateral depth but shared Global South positioning. Watch for: BRICS summit dynamics, NDB governance evolution, and whether the Indonesia-South Africa-Brazil axis develops coherence within BRICS as a democratic middle-power caucus or fragments under domestic authoritarian pressures.
