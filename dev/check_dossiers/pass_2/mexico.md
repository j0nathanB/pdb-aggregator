## SECTION 8: ENVIRONMENTAL AND CLIMATIC STRUCTURE

### Natural disasters as regime stress tests

Mexico's relationship between climate events and political legitimacy follows a clear structural pattern: catastrophic natural disasters expose governance failures that accelerate institutional change. The **September 19, 1985, magnitude-8.0 earthquake** killed an estimated 5,000–10,000 people in Mexico City, collapsed 412 buildings, and left 250,000 homeless. The PRI government under Miguel de la Madrid initially refused foreign aid, deployed the military to recover factory machinery rather than rescue survivors, and distributed assistance along clientelist lines favoring party members. Citizen self-organization into rescue brigades — notably the Coordinadora Única de Damnificados — catalyzed Mexico's democratic transition, contributing directly to the creation of a popularly elected Mexico City mayor and feeding the chain of legitimacy crises (1968 massacre, 1982 debt crisis, 1988 tainted elections) that ended PRI dominance in 2000.

**Hurricane Otis** (October 25, 2023) reproduced this pattern under different conditions. The first recorded Category 5 Pacific hurricane landfall, with 165 mph winds and the largest NHC intensity forecast errors in basin history, devastated Acapulco — a city where **60% of the population lived below the poverty line**. Official death tolls of 52 dead and 32 missing are almost certainly undercounts; local officials told the Washington Post they counted 120 dead or missing. Damage reached **$12–16 billion**, making Otis the costliest tropical cyclone in Mexican history. AMLO's promise that homes would be "better than before" by March 2024 was falsified by February 2024, when 666,000 tons of debris remained in mountain communities. UNICEF estimated 1.07 million people needed humanitarian assistance.

The **October 2025 floods** in eastern Mexico — affecting Veracruz, Puebla, Hidalgo, Querétaro, and San Luis Potosí with rainfall exceeding 530mm — killed 76 people, damaged approximately 100,000 homes, and cut off 300+ villages. Described as the worst such event in more than eight decades, the floods exposed the consequences of AMLO's **elimination of FONDEN** (the Natural Disaster Fund) in October 2020. FONDEN had averaged approximately $800 million annually and demonstrated a benefit-cost ratio of at least 3.2 (per American Economic Journal, 2024). Its replacement — ad hoc budget allocations, with 19 billion pesos (~$1.03 billion) earmarked for emergencies in 2025 — drew opposition criticism as "improvisation." Mexico retains a $485 million World Bank catastrophe bond but lacks the institutional mechanism for rapid, transparent disaster reconstruction that FONDEN provided.

### Transboundary water as a sovereignty flashpoint

The 1944 U.S.-Mexico Water Treaty requires Mexico to deliver a minimum **1.75 million acre-feet (MAF)** of Rio Grande tributary water to the United States over each five-year cycle, while the U.S. must deliver 1.5 MAF annually from the Colorado River to Mexico. This framework, administered by the International Boundary and Water Commission (IBWC/CILA), has become the most contentious bilateral environmental issue since 2020.

Mexico's compliance failures are chronic and worsening. The country first missed its five-year deadline in 1997, missed again in 2002, and nearly defaulted in 2020, transferring water just three days before the deadline. **Cycle 36 (2020–2025) ended in outright default**: Mexico delivered only approximately **880,000 AF of the 1.75 MAF owed** — less than half — leaving a deficit exceeding 800,000 AF. A Texas A&M study estimated economic losses in the Rio Grande Valley at **$994 million in 2023 alone**.

The Trump administration has weaponized this issue with escalating intensity. In March 2025, the U.S. denied Mexico's Colorado River water delivery request to Tijuana for the first time in treaty history. Trump threatened tariffs and sanctions on Truth Social in April 2025. By December 9, 2025, he imposed a **5% tariff on Mexican imports** specifically citing water treaty violations. A December 2025 agreement requiring Mexico to release 202,000 AF by January 31, 2026, represented only a partial resolution. The structural problem — that 75% of Mexico experienced moderate-to-exceptional drought in 2024, making compliance physically difficult — is intensifying under climate change.

Colorado River allocations to Mexico have also tightened. Under basin-wide conservation measures (Minutes 319, 323, 330), Mexico's allocation was reduced by **263 million cubic meters in 2024 (~14% of entitlement)** and was projected to increase to a 346 MCM reduction in 2025 (~20%). This reduction equals the combined annual water consumption of Mexicali, Tijuana, Tecate, Rosarito, and Ensenada — approximately 3.5 million people.

### Mexico City's aquifer crisis defies political timelines

Mexico City's water system is structurally unsustainable regardless of policy interventions. Approximately **70% of the city's water** comes from the aquifer directly beneath it, with extraction occurring at twice the recharge rate. This overdraft has caused parts of the city to sink at rates up to **50 centimeters per year** — among the fastest subsidence rates globally. Roughly **40% of water** entering the distribution system is lost to leaks or theft across 2.5 million legal connections and an estimated 900,000 illegal ones.

The Cutzamala system, which supplies approximately 5 million Valley of Mexico residents via a 127-kilometer aqueduct lifting water over 1,000 meters in elevation, dropped to **26% capacity in June 2024** — its worst level in 70 years. UN estimates placed a definitive "Day Zero" for Mexico City as early as 2028 without effective management. Heavy 2025 rains brought Cutzamala to 95.5% capacity by October 2025, prompting Mayor Clara Brugada to declare the capital would not face shortages in 2026. But experts describe this drought-to-deluge swing as the "new hydrological identity" of central Mexico — a pattern that relieves surface storage while doing nothing to address aquifer depletion. The first major Cutzamala upgrade in 40 years, announced for 2025–2026 with 680 million pesos ($39.6 million), is necessary but insufficient against structural demand.

### Emissions, energy, and the Pemex paradox

Mexico is the world's **14th-largest greenhouse gas emitter** and among the 10 largest methane emitters. Emissions (excluding land use) increased 60% between 1990 and 2018 to approximately **724 MtCO₂e**. The energy sector accounts for 64–70% of total emissions, with transport the largest contributor at 30–34% of fuel combustion emissions. Climate Action Tracker rates Mexico's trajectory as **"Highly Insufficient"** — if all countries followed Mexico's approach, warming could reach 3–4°C.

The contradiction at the center of Mexico's climate position is Pemex. Production averaged **1.61 million barrels per day** (January–October 2025), down from a 2004 peak of 3.4 million bbl/d. Pemex carries approximately **$100 billion in debt** and received an unprecedented $21.7 billion in federal support in 2025 alone, while facing $24 billion in debt maturities in 2025–2026. The Dos Bocas (Olmeca) refinery, designed for 340,000 bbl/d capacity, processed only 118,000 bbl/d on average through October 2025. At current production rates without further discoveries, Pemex oil reserves would be depleted by approximately 2030. Yet the 2025–2030 Pemex strategic plan calls for increased fossil fuel production and a 34% increase in refining capacity.

Mexico's electricity mix remains **75% fossil-fuel-based** — among the most fossil-dependent in the OECD. Solar accounts for just 7.7% and wind 5.8%. The 4T government cancelled clean energy auctions in 2019 and issued 10 executive orders derailing private renewable investment. CFE proposes raising its clean energy share to 38% by 2030 through 51 projects with $22.4 billion in investment, but its "clean energy" classification controversially includes certain fossil gas plants. Sheinbaum's Sonora Plan envisions the largest solar PV plant in Latin America, representing a potentially significant shift, but structural barriers from CFE dominance remain. Mexico's 2035 NDC, submitted at COP30, set an absolute emissions cap for the first time and included a 2050 net-zero target announced at COP29 — but no updated Long-Term Strategy has been submitted to the UNFCCC.

### Deforestation driven by flagship programs

The Tren Maya railway (1,554 km across five states, approximately $27 billion cost, completed December 2024) has caused documented deforestation of at least **6,659 hectares** as of June 2023, with **87% of clearing occurring without required SEMARNAT authorization**. Independent estimates suggest over 10 million trees were felled. SEMARNAT Minister Alicia Bárcena acknowledged to Congress that 8 cenotes and caverns along Section 5 were impacted. The railway fragmented the Calakmul Biosphere Reserve — the second largest in Latin America — and interfered with Great Maya Aquifer recharge.

The Sembrando Vida reforestation program ($3.4 billion, 442,000 participants) created a perverse incentive structure that may have caused **72,830 hectares of forest loss in 2019 alone** — its first year — according to WRI analysis. The program requires unforested land to qualify, leading farmers to clear forest to enroll. Losses concentrated in biodiversity-rich Chiapas, Tabasco, Yucatán, and Quintana Roo. Mexico's heaviest deforestation years — 2019 and 2020 — coincided precisely with Sembrando Vida's first two enrollment cycles.

## SECTION 9: ILLICIT NETWORKS AND SHADOW GOVERNANCE

### Two simultaneous succession crises reshape the cartel landscape

Mexico entered 2026 with both of its transnational mega-cartels in violent succession crises — a situation without precedent in the country's modern security history. The arrest of Ismael "El Mayo" Zambada García on July 25, 2024, at a private airfield in El Paso, Texas — where he was lured under false pretenses by Joaquín Guzmán López, sedated, and delivered to U.S. authorities — triggered a **civil war within the Sinaloa Cartel** that has killed over 1,000 people and disappeared thousands since September 2024. Homicides in Sinaloa rose by over **400%** in the year following the arrest. The factional split pits Los Chapitos (led by Iván Archivaldo and Jesús Alfredo Guzmán Salazar, each with $10 million State Department bounties) against La Mayiza (Zambada loyalists led by his son Ismael "Mayito Flaco" Zambada Sicairos). Violence has been concentrated in Culiacán but has spread to Durango, Nayarit, and Baja California, with at least 1,763 families displaced and approximately 50 rural communities abandoned entirely.

The killing of Nemesio Rubén Oseguera Cervantes ("El Mencho"), CJNG's founder and supreme commander, during a Mexican military operation in **Tapalpa, Jalisco, on February 22, 2026**, created the second succession crisis. The predawn raid, supported by U.S. intelligence via the Joint Interagency Task Force–Counter Cartel (JIATF-CC), involved 6 helicopters and ground forces. Eight CJNG bodyguards were killed; El Mencho died from wounds during transport. CJNG launched massive retaliatory violence within hours: **252 roadblocks** across 20+ states, attacks on convenience stores and gas stations, and coordinated strikes that **killed 25 National Guard troops** in six separate attacks in Jalisco. Airlines cancelled flights to Guadalajara and Puerto Vallarta.

CJNG's succession dynamics are structurally more dangerous than Sinaloa's. El Mencho's son ("El Menchito"), brother, and daughter are all imprisoned. Unlike Sinaloa's family-based factions, CJNG's power rests with geographically separated regional commanders — Ricardo Ruiz Velasco ("El Doble R"), Audias Flores ("El Jardinero"), Hugo Mendoza Gaytán ("El Sapo") — with no blood ties to bind them. Global Guardian assessed in February 2026 that CJNG is "highly likely to fragment violently." CSIS identified three scenarios: consolidation around a single leader, fragmentation and civil war, or the Mexican government pressing advantage during CJNG's vulnerability.

### The territorial mosaic of 442 criminal groups

A 2022 Lantia Intelligence study identified **442 distinct criminal groups** operating across Mexico — extreme fragmentation since the Calderón-era strategy of targeting cartel leadership. The territorial map as of early 2026 shows the Sinaloa Cartel (now fractured) dominant across northwestern Mexico — Sinaloa, Sonora, Durango, Chihuahua, and Baja California — while CJNG (now leaderless) operates from its Jalisco base across Colima, Michoacán, Guanajuato, Guerrero, and Chiapas, with presence in 21+ of 32 states. Regional cartels persist: the Gulf Cartel in Tamaulipas (split into Los Metros and Los Escorpiones), Cartel del Noreste in Coahuila, La Nueva Familia Michoacana fighting for Guerrero's gold mines, and Cartel de Santa Rosa de Lima contesting Guanajuato with CJNG.

A notable development emerged in July 2025, when Mexican intelligence revealed that Colombian and Mexican cartel mercenaries had been sent to fight in Ukraine's International Legion to acquire combat tactics, particularly **FPV drone operations**. By August 2025, CJNG had reportedly established a dedicated paramilitary unit of at least 10 members for FPV drone warfare — a doctrinal evolution with potentially severe implications for both state security forces and rival organizations.

### Fentanyl as a structural U.S.-Mexico crisis

Mexico remains "the most significant source of illicit fentanyl significantly affecting the United States," according to the 2025 International Narcotics Control Strategy Report. The Sinaloa Cartel and CJNG are jointly identified as the "primary and most dangerous" trafficking organizations. The largest single fentanyl seizure in history — **1,500 kilograms** — occurred on December 3, 2024, in Sinaloa.

The precursor chemical supply chain runs primarily from China, with over 3,100 chemicals potentially usable in fentanyl manufacture shipped by container to Mexican ports — principally **Lázaro Cárdenas and Manzanillo**. Following the November 2025 Trump-Xi summit in Busan, China placed export controls on 13 specific precursor chemicals to North America and completed scheduling all INCB-listed fentanyl precursors by June 2025. India is emerging as an alternative source country. U.S. fentanyl deaths declined to approximately **48,422 in 2024** from 76,282 in 2023, though causation is debated. Trump designated fentanyl as a "Weapon of Mass Destruction" via Executive Order 14367 in December 2025.

### State-criminal integration and the extortion economy

El Mayo Zambada's August 2025 guilty plea included an extraordinary admission: "The organization I led promoted corruption in my home country by paying police, military commanders and politicians... It goes back to the very beginning when I was a young man starting out and it continued for all those years." He admitted trafficking approximately **1.5 million kilos of cocaine** and agreed to forfeit $15 billion. The "plaza system" — in which cartels pay bribes to local police, military, and politicians for territorial operating rights — creates governance structures where the cartel effectively administers security and economic activity in exchange for tax-like payments (derecho de piso).

Extortion has reached industrial scale. INEGI's 2024 business victimization survey documented **747,000 extortion crimes in 2023**, with a compliance rate of 67% (up from 44.7% in 2011). Over 90% of crimes against businesses go unreported; the uninvestigated crime rate against businesses reached **96.7% in 2023**. Coparmex estimates economic losses exceeding $1.1 billion annually. Typical cobro de piso demands range from 500–1,000 pesos per week ($25–50) for small merchants. The American Chamber of Commerce in Mexico reported in 2024 that 45% of member companies had received extortion demands and 12% reported organized crime had taken partial control of their sales, distribution, or pricing.

Fuel theft (huachicoleo) has become the largest source of non-drug cartel revenue according to U.S. Treasury. An estimated **987 million liters** were stolen in 2024, with annual financial losses of approximately **$12 billion**. In some states, contraband fuel accounts for over 40% of annual consumption. A September 2025 crackdown arrested Vice Admiral Manuel Roberto Farías Laguna and 14+ officials for running a massive fuel theft network through Tampico port customs — illustrating the depth of state-criminal integration even within military institutions.

### Money laundering through Chinese underground networks

A landmark FinCEN Financial Trend Analysis (August 28, 2025) analyzed 137,153 Bank Secrecy Act reports from January 2020 through December 2024, documenting approximately **$312 billion in suspicious transactions** involving Chinese Money Laundering Networks (CMLNs). These networks — heavily utilized by Mexican cartels — operate as unregistered brokers charging commissions under 6%, far below usual rates, because they profit twice: once from laundering and again from selling dollars at premium to Chinese citizens evading the PRC's $50,000/year foreign exchange cap. The three primary methodologies are trade-based money laundering (goods purchased in China, exported to Mexico, sold for local currency), money mule networks, and mirror transactions where no cross-border wire occurs.

### FTO designations enacted but impact uncertain

On February 20, 2025, the Trump administration designated eight organizations as Foreign Terrorist Organizations, including the Sinaloa Cartel, CJNG, Cartel del Noreste, Gulf Cartel, La Nueva Familia Michoacana, and Cárteles Unidos. Material support violations carry sentences up to life imprisonment. Companies in financial services, transportation, tourism, and agriculture face new compliance risks — extortion payments could constitute "material support" under 18 U.S.C. §2339B. FinCEN issued Geographic Targeting Orders requiring border-town financial institutions to file currency transaction reports at a **$200 threshold** (versus the usual $10,000).

Mexico's response has been publicly defiant but pragmatically cooperative. Sheinbaum declared "sovereignty is not for sale" and proposed constitutional reforms barring foreign intervention, while simultaneously transferring 55 high-level drug traffickers to U.S. custody and cooperating on the intelligence operation that killed El Mencho.

## SECTION 10: INFORMATION ECOSYSTEM AND MEDIA STRUCTURE

### A concentrated media landscape shaped by state advertising

Mexico's information ecosystem is dominated by a television duopoly that, while weakening, still commands the primary news diet for most Mexicans. **Televisa** (now TelevisaUnivision, the world's largest Spanish-language content producer) holds approximately **43% of the TV audience**, while **TV Azteca** (owned by Ricardo Salinas Pliego's Grupo Salinas) commands roughly **31%**. Together they controlled approximately 90% of viewership until the 2014 telecom reform and still hold roughly 74% combined. Grupo Imagen's Imagen TV (launched 2015) holds the third national network license with approximately 21% market share.

Government advertising spending functions as the primary lever of state influence over media. During the AMLO sexenio (December 2018–September 2024), the top recipients were Televisa ($1.877 billion MXN, 10.2% of total), **La Jornada ($1.596 billion MXN, 8.7%)**, and TV Azteca ($1.34 billion MXN, 7.3%). La Jornada's disproportionate allocation relative to its readership — as a left-leaning newspaper closely aligned with the 4T — is widely cited as evidence of political favoritism. Critical independent digital outlets including Aristegui Noticias, Animal Político, and Sin Embargo received minimal government advertising, widely interpreted as punishment for critical coverage. An RSF Media Ownership Monitor analysis found that of 42 media outlets analyzed, 38 gained significant revenues from government advertising, with half of the total budget flowing to just 10 business groups.

State-level spending has surged as federal spending declined, creating a diffuse but equally coercive dynamic. By 2023, states accounted for approximately 78% of combined government advertising. In 2024, **13 state governments spent more than double** their authorized advertising budgets; five states exceeded 1,000% of approved levels.

### The mañanera as information architecture

The daily presidential morning press conference — the mañanera — represents a structural innovation in executive communication that functions as both agenda-setting mechanism and instrument of media delegitimization. AMLO's version ran 2–3 hours daily from the National Palace, broadcast across government social media channels. SPIN analysis documented an average of **88 false or unverifiable claims per conference**. The "Quién es Quién en las Mentiras" segment, launched July 2021, purported to debunk media "lies" but was itself found to contain factual errors. AMLO used the platform to reveal journalists' private information and to label critical media as "prensa fifí" — part of the "mafia del poder."

Sheinbaum's version maintains the daily format but introduced a themed weekly schedule (Monday: healthy living; Wednesday: "lie detector" — a renamed version of Quién es Quién; Thursday: women in history). Key differences: shorter responses, more structured format, less aggressive personal attacks on journalists, and greater TikTok integration. Assessments from Americas Quarterly and Bloomberg indicate Sheinbaum is less effective at setting the daily media agenda than AMLO but continues the anti-pluralistic framing of critics as "oligarchic opposition."

### The deadliest country in the Western Hemisphere for journalists

Mexico's press freedom crisis is structural, not episodic. The **2025 RSF World Press Freedom Index** ranks Mexico **124th of 180 countries**, classified as a "difficult situation." RSF notes Mexico holds the **record for the highest number of missing journalists worldwide: 28 unsolved cases**. CPJ data document **153–158 journalists killed** since 1992, with approximately **90% of cases remaining unresolved** and an impunity rate of 98–99.75% for crimes against freedom of expression.

Article 19 documented that on at least 176 occasions in 2022 alone, the executive branch made "stigmatizing comments" against media and journalists from the mañanera. The Federal Protection Mechanism for journalists, created in 2012, had approximately 2,400 people requiring protection by 2024 — deemed "untenable" given insufficient personnel. Eight journalists have been killed while enrolled in the Mechanism over the past seven years.

Academic research from Cambridge University Press (2024) identifies a causal link between military deployments against cartels and violence against journalists: "criminal wars" — localized turf wars triggered by federal military intervention — are the primary driver. Perpetrators are typically a combination of organized crime groups and local/state officials, who together create "zones of silence" where independent reporting becomes impossible through self-censorship.

### Digital platforms and cartel information warfare

Mexico's digital landscape encompasses **110 million internet users** (83.3% penetration), with social media reaching 93–99 million user identities. **TikTok** (99 million users aged 18+) and **Facebook** (93.5 million users) dominate, with WhatsApp the primary messaging platform at approximately 93% reach. The digital divide persists: approximately 21.9 million people remain offline, predominantly in rural areas.

Cartels have evolved from narcomantas (physical banners) to sophisticated digital operations. An internal Meta report (2021) acknowledged that "criminal organisations blatantly misuse our platform for coordination of offline harm, recruitment, promotion of narcocultura and trading drugs." During the February 2026 crisis following El Mencho's death, OCCRP documented CJNG **"weaponizing algorithms"** — mixing AI-generated images with authentic photographs to amplify fear. International Crisis Group reports characterize cartel social media strategy as threefold: threatening rivals, winning civilian sympathy, and recruiting through glamorized depictions of narco life.

Russian disinformation operations target Mexico primarily through **RT en Español** (which Reuters Institute found "more successful than any of the other services of RT") and Sputnik Mundo. The U.S. State Department documented Kremlin campaigns by the Social Design Agency and affiliated entities to "covertly coopt local media and influencers" in Mexico. Chinese influence is subtler, operating through economic leverage and surveillance technology provision rather than overt propaganda.

## SECTION 11: INTERNATIONAL INSTITUTIONAL COMMITMENTS AND LEGAL FRAMEWORKS

### The USMCA review as Mexico's most consequential near-term event

The July 1, 2026 USMCA joint review deadline represents the single most important institutional event on Mexico's horizon. If the three parties do not agree to extend the agreement for an additional 16 years (through 2042), annual reviews begin and the agreement terminates July 1, 2036. **On March 5, 2026, USTR Jamieson Greer and Mexico's Secretary of Economy Marcelo Ebrard announced the first round of bilateral discussions**, with negotiators to meet beginning the week of March 16, 2026.

The review occurs amid maximum tension. A USMCA dispute settlement panel ruled **in favor of the United States on all claims** on December 20, 2024, regarding Mexico's energy policies favoring CFE and Pemex — potentially exposing Mexico to up to **$30 billion in retaliatory tariffs** if non-compliance persists. The Rapid Response Labor Mechanism has triggered **37+ cases** since May 2021 (plus one by Canada), with 71% successfully resolved and 61% originating in the auto sector. As of February 2026, 12 facilities had formal remediation plans. The mechanism has contributed to a **22% increase** in union representation in Mexico since 2018 and generated more than 10,000 employee-supported union contracts.

**Article 32.10** — the "non-market economy" clause — effectively prevents Mexico from pursuing a comprehensive FTA with China by allowing the other two parties to terminate USMCA on six months' notice if such an agreement is signed. This provision, described by analysts as a "poison pill," represents an unprecedented constraint on Mexican trade sovereignty. Mexico responded by raising tariffs on 1,000+ products from non-FTA countries to **50% effective January 1, 2026**, primarily targeting Chinese and Indian goods.

### Structural dependency makes Western defection catastrophic

Mexico's institutional lock-in to Western frameworks is quantifiable. In 2024, Mexican exports to the United States totaled **$505.85 billion — 84% of total exports**. Total bilateral trade approached **$930 billion**, making Mexico the number-one U.S. trading partner. Remittances reached a record **$64.7 billion** in 2024 (96.6% from the U.S.), representing approximately 3.5% of circulating money and supporting an estimated 12 million families. Cumulative U.S. FDI from 1999 to 2024 totals $327 billion. USMCA utilization among Mexican exporters surged from 44.8% to **88.7% in 2025**.

Mexico's engagement with alternative frameworks reflects hedging, not defection. Sheinbaum initially stated in October 2024 that "our interest is to strengthen USMCA," but by July 2025 Mexico attended the **17th BRICS Summit in Rio de Janeiro as an observer**, sending Foreign Minister Juan Ramón de la Fuente. Russia's ambassador confirmed Mexico has "no intention of joining" BRICS. Mexico rejoined the G77+China in 2023 under AMLO, who declared "Mexico belongs to the Global South." Under Sheinbaum, Mexico has continued CELAC engagement, attending the April 2025 summit in Tegucigalpa and calling for Latin American economic diversification — language calibrated to signal independence without triggering Article 32.10 consequences.

Mexico has **14 FTAs covering 52 countries and 60% of global GDP** — making it the most trade-connected major manufacturing destination globally. The CPTPP (entered into force for Mexico December 30, 2018) opens Asia-Pacific markets with 89%+ tariff lines eliminated. The modernized EU-Mexico Global Agreement covers a $90.7 billion trade relationship. The Pacific Alliance with Chile, Colombia, and Peru eliminates 90% of tariffs among members.

### Institutional positions and legal exposure

Mexico is not currently a UNSC non-permanent member; its last term was 2021–2022 (its fifth overall). Mexico adheres to the **Estrada Doctrine** of non-intervention and self-determination, voting for UNGA resolutions condemning Russia's invasion of Ukraine while maintaining dialogue-based positions and declining to impose sanctions. The IMF's most recent Article IV consultation (October 2025) projected **1% growth for 2025 and 1.5% for 2026**, praising Mexico's "strong fundamentals" while urging fiscal consolidation and structural reforms.

Mexico's ICC jurisdiction is constitutionally conditional: Article 21 requires presidential recognition on a case-by-case basis with Senate approval. At the ICJ, Mexico has been more active — winning the **Avena case (2004)** on consular rights for Mexican death row inmates and filing against Ecuador in 2024 after police stormed Mexico's embassy in Quito.

The 2026 FIFA World Cup, with Mexico co-hosting 13 matches in Mexico City (Estadio Azteca), Guadalajara, and Monterrey, physically embodies the USMCA trilateral relationship. The **June 11, 2026, opening match** (Mexico vs. South Africa at Estadio Azteca) makes it the first stadium to host a World Cup opener three times. Guadalajara's hosting of group-stage matches beginning June 18 — amid ongoing CJNG post-El Mencho violence — represents a significant security test. The December 2025 FIFA World Cup draw in Washington provided a diplomatic sidecar, with Sheinbaum, Trump, and Canadian PM Carney meeting on trade issues.

## SECTION 12: MILITARY AND SECURITY SECTOR DNA

### An unprecedented military-commercial transformation

Mexico's armed forces have undergone the most dramatic institutional transformation in their post-revolutionary history under the 4T project. The military assumed an estimated **246 additional tasks** beyond its original mandate during AMLO's sexenio — an expansion continued by Sheinbaum that has no parallel in modern Mexican governance.

SEDENA, under Secretary of Defense **General Ricardo Trevilla Trejo** (appointed October 1, 2024), now controls through its corporate arm Mundo Maya Group (formerly GAFSACOMM): **12 airports** including AIFA, the **Tren Maya railway**, the revived **Mexicana de Aviación** airline, hotels, national parks, gas stations, 2,700 Banco del Bienestar branches (construction), and 21 mining authorizations in Quintana Roo. SEMAR, under **Admiral Raymundo Pedro Morales Ángeles** (educated at the U.S. National Defense University), controls Mexico City's Benito Juárez International Airport, the entire national port system, customs/aduanas management, and the Interoceanic Corridor of the Isthmus of Tehuantepec. A December 2025 cruise ship tariff of **$42 per passenger** directs two-thirds of revenue to military coffers. Together, SEDENA and SEMAR control approximately **one in every ten pesos** of the 2026 administrative budget.

SEDENA's budget trajectory tells a complex story: **MX$259.43 billion (~$14.4 billion) in 2024**, cut to **MX$151.99 billion (~$8.4 billion) in 2025** (a 44% reduction under Sheinbaum's fiscal austerity), then partially restored to MX$170.75 billion (~$9.5 billion) for 2026, of which 66.5% goes to personnel remunerations alone. Combined SEDENA and SEMAR spending for 2026 reaches approximately MX$236.7 billion (~$13.2 billion). Mexico's defense spending remains approximately **0.6–0.7% of GDP**, well below Brazil (1.2%), Colombia (3.2%), and Chile (1.9%).

AIFA handled **7.08 million passengers** in 2025 (up 11.5% year-over-year, now Mexico's 7th busiest airport) and became Mexico's largest air cargo hub at 406,193 tons. However, it has received MX$5.25 billion ($287 million) in subsidies since 2022 and is projected to recover only approximately 15% of construction costs by 2033. The Tren Maya carried approximately 1,600 passengers daily as of December 2025 — far below the 8,200/day target — and generated only 10% of operating costs in 2024 revenue.

### The Guardia Nacional completes militarization

The **September 30, 2024, constitutional reform** — published on AMLO's last day in office — transferred the Guardia Nacional from the civilian Security Ministry (SSPC) to SEDENA command. The reform modified Articles 13, 16, 21, 32, 55, 73, 76, 78, 82, 89, 123, and critically **Article 129**, which previously restricted military functions in peacetime to those with "exact connection with military discipline." The reformed article permits armed forces to perform functions outlined in the Constitution and derivative legislation — a broad expansion of permissible military activity.

The GN's current strength of approximately **130,000–140,000 elements** (80% from military backgrounds) combines with SEDENA's existing forces to create a total militarized federal force exceeding 350,000. Mexico now **lacks a nationwide civilian police force at the federal level**. July 2025 secondary legislation formally completed the transfer. The reform was approved by 362–131 in the Chamber of Deputies and ratified by 26 state legislatures.

### García Harfuch: the dynasty that bridges eras

**Omar García Harfuch**, Secretary of Security and Citizen Protection since October 1, 2024, represents perhaps the most consequential figure in Mexico's security establishment. At 44, he brings a dynastic connection to Mexico's darkest security episodes: his **grandfather, General Marcelino García Barragán, was Secretary of National Defense during the 1968 Tlatelolco massacre**; his **father, Javier García Paniagua, headed the Federal Security Directorate** (1976–78) during the Dirty War's most intense period. García Harfuch trained at Harvard, the FBI, and the DEA, and survived a 2020 CJNG assassination attempt on Mexico City's Paseo de la Reforma that killed two bodyguards.

His Senate testimony on October 22, 2025, claimed a **46% decrease** in crime-related homicides versus 2018, 1,500+ meth labs dismantled, 35,000+ "violence generators" captured, and 29+ cartel leaders extradited to the United States. He led the intelligence operation that culminated in El Mencho's death. Dubbed "Mexico's Batman" by the Los Angeles Times, he consistently ranks as the top potential successor to Sheinbaum for the 2030 presidential race in internal Morena polling. However, the Truth Commission report placed him at meetings where officials fabricated the "historical truth" covering up military involvement in the Ayotzinapa disappearances — a vulnerability that could resurface.

### Human rights: the unresolved structural crisis

Mexico's National Registry of Disappeared Persons documents **133,215 cumulative disappearances** since 1952 — though 90% occurred between 2000 and 2025. In Sheinbaum's first year (October 2024–September 2025), 14,765 people remained unaccounted for after being reported missing — a **16% increase** over AMLO's last year and 54% above AMLO's annual average. In April 2025, the **UN Committee on Enforced Disappearances activated Article 34** for the first time in its history, finding disappearances in Mexico to be "widespread or systematic." The impunity rate exceeds 98%; only 36 judgments had been issued nationally as of 2021.

The Ayotzinapa case — 43 students disappeared on September 26, 2014 — remains fundamentally unresolved. Only 3 students' remains have been identified. Evidence implicates local police, the Guerreros Unidos cartel, the Mexican Army's 27th Infantry Battalion, and federal police. SEDENA continues to refuse to hand over case documents, confirmed by HRW's World Report 2026. The special prosecutor was forced out in 2022 and fled the country. SEDENA used Pegasus spyware against investigators and Ayotzinapa families' lawyers.

### U.S.-Mexico security cooperation in structural limbo

The bilateral security architecture has deteriorated to its most uncertain state in nearly two decades. The Mérida Initiative (2008–2021) delivered approximately **$3.5 billion** over its lifetime. Its replacement, the Bicentennial Framework (October 2021), operated under Biden but the Trump administration **does not operate within its framework** — leaving, per CSIS analysis, no current juridical structure for bilateral security cooperation. Trump's FY2026 budget requested only $125 million for International Narcotics Control and Law Enforcement globally — less than 10% of FY2025 levels.

The operational relationship has nonetheless produced results through ad hoc cooperation. The El Mencho operation demonstrated that tactical intelligence sharing continues: U.S. JIATF-CC provided the "targeting package" through NORTHCOM, while Sheinbaum confirmed no U.S. ground forces participated. Mexico transferred 55 high-level drug traffickers to U.S. custody, including 29 in a February 2025 "mega-handover" that bypassed standard Article 119 constitutional extradition procedures — widely interpreted as a gesture to forestall tariff escalation and military intervention threats. The NDAA FY2026 explicitly states no authorization for military force against Mexico (§8365), though it requires reports on joint training and counter-drug cooperation.

## Conclusion

The structural picture emerging across these five domains is of a state caught in a tightening vise. Mexico's environmental vulnerabilities — particularly the Mexico City aquifer crisis and transboundary water disputes — are becoming instruments of U.S. leverage at precisely the moment when climate change makes compliance harder. The simultaneous leadership crises in both the Sinaloa Cartel and CJNG create a window of maximum instability coinciding with the FIFA World Cup and USMCA review. The military's expansion into commercial governance has created an institution with unprecedented institutional interests in self-perpetuation but also new accountability vulnerabilities, including a $12 billion fuel-theft economy that reaches into naval customs offices.

The deepest structural finding is the asymmetry between Mexico's institutional dependencies and its sovereignty aspirations. With 84% of exports flowing to a single market, 96.6% of remittances originating from one country, and the USMCA "poison pill" foreclosing a China FTA, Mexico's room for genuine strategic autonomy is far narrower than its diplomatic rhetoric suggests. The BRICS observer seat, the CELAC speeches, and the sovereignty-protecting constitutional proposals are calibrated signaling — not defection preparation. The July 2026 USMCA review will determine whether this balancing act can be sustained or whether Trump-era pressures force Mexico into a more explicitly subordinate institutional position, with cascading consequences for every domain covered in this assessment.
