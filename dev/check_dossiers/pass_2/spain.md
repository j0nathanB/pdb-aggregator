## SECTION 8: ENVIRONMENTAL & CLIMATIC STRUCTURE

### Spain faces the most severe water crisis in modern European history

**Spain's multi-year drought (2022–2025) exposed structural vulnerabilities in a country where 74% of territory faces desertification risk and agriculture consumes 80% of available water.** The crisis triggered political confrontation over inter-basin transfers, accelerated a massive desalination build-out, and culminated in the deadliest flood disaster in Spain's modern history — the October 2024 Valencia DANA — which killed over 220 people and caused an estimated €16–50 billion in damage. Climate projections suggest the Iberian Peninsula will be Europe's hardest-hit region, with precipitation declining **20–40%** and semi-arid conditions becoming the country's dominant climate type by 2050.

Spain's water infrastructure was designed for a wetter country. Average water availability has already decreased **12% since 1980**, and projections indicate a further **14–40% decline by 2050**. The drought that began in late 2022 was the longest since AEMET records began in 1961, with Catalonia experiencing its worst three consecutive dry years since 1914. The Sau Reservoir supplying Barcelona dropped to **1% of capacity** in March 2024. By mid-2025, southern basins remained critically low — Guadalquivir at **25.6%**, Guadiana at **34.7%**, Segura at **35.8%** — while northern basins like Duero (74%) and Miño-Sil (78.5%) fared better. Heavy rains in March 2025 broke the Catalan drought but did little for the south.

The claim that 2024 was Spain's lowest-rainfall year in 150 years requires qualification. National average precipitation actually rose to **663 mm in 2024** from 527 mm in 2023, though the Canary Islands recorded their driest year since 1961 (only 139 liters). The statistic likely refers to specific regions or hydrological-year measurements rather than the national calendar-year average. What is unambiguous is that **20% of mainland Spain is already desertified** and arid climates have doubled since the mid-twentieth century, expanding at roughly **1,500 km² per year**.

### The Tagus-Segura transfer is dying — and taking southeast Spain's economy with it

The Tagus-Segura inter-basin transfer, operational since 1979 via a 286-km canal, is Spain's most politically explosive water infrastructure. Designed to move up to 600 hm³/year from central Spain to the arid southeast, actual transfers averaged 328 hm³/year. A **May 2025 Supreme Court ruling** declared that deferring ecological flow enforcement in the Tagus violated environmental law, particularly in Natura 2000 protected areas. Castilla-La Mancha's president Emiliano García-Page declared the transfer "history in its current form," while Murcia, Valencia, and Andalusia — where the transfer sustains **€2.4 billion in agricultural GDP** — fiercely resisted. Climate studies project **70–79% reduction** in transferable flows, making transfers physically impossible by 2070 and threatening annual economic losses of €380–425 million in the Segura basin.

Spain compensates partly through desalination. It is **Europe's largest desalinated water producer** and the world's fourth or fifth largest, operating **765 plants** with a combined daily output of approximately 5 million m³. The Torrevieja plant (Alicante), Spain's largest, is being expanded from 80 to 120 hm³/year. Barcelona's El Prat plant, Europe's largest for human consumption, went from supplying **3% of drinking water in 2021 to 33% in 2023**. New plants near Barcelona (Tordera and Foix) will add 220,000 m³/day by 2028–2029, backed by €467 million in investment.

### Climate projections paint an alarming picture for the peninsula

Average temperatures rose **1.5°C between 1965 and 2015**. Heatwave frequency has doubled since 2010. June 2025 was the hottest June on record, with temperatures exceeding 46°C in Andalusia. The August 2025 heatwave was the most intense ever measured — 16 days with a **+4.6°C anomaly** above the norm. Projections under high-emission scenarios indicate **20–40% annual precipitation reduction** by century's end, reaching **80% in summer**, with temperatures rising 2.5–5°C in mountain areas.

Sea levels around Spain have risen **9 cm since 1993**. By 2100, projections range from +58 cm (Alicante) to +81 cm (Santa Cruz de Tenerife). Cádiz faces the prospect of being cut off from the mainland. Barcelona's beaches lose an estimated **6–10 meters of sand per year**. The Ebro Delta, Doñana wetlands, and Llobregat Delta face existential threats.

The 2025 wildfire season was catastrophic — the worst in over 30 years, with approximately **400,000 hectares** burned by late August, six times the 2006–2024 average. Ourense province alone lost 150,000 hectares — one-seventh of its total surface. Record CO₂ emissions from fires reached **17.68 million tonnes**, exceeding any previous full-year total since 2003. At least 8 people died, 36,000 were evacuated, and 37 individuals were detained for suspected arson.

### Agriculture's 80% water share collides with climate reality

Agriculture consumes approximately **80–85% of Spain's total water** while contributing only **2.3% of GDP** — a disproportion at the heart of the crisis. Nearly 4 million hectares are under irrigation, among the highest in Europe. Spain's position as the world's largest olive oil producer (45–50% of global production) is directly threatened. The 2022/23 harvest collapsed to **663,000 tonnes** — half the historical average — sending extra-virgin prices to record peaks. The 2024/25 harvest recovered to approximately **1.4 million tonnes**, but farmers warn that two more drought years could "kill olive tree plantations in the south."

Almería's "sea of plastic" — **31,600–40,000+ hectares** of greenhouses producing one-third of Europe's winter vegetables — requires 800–1,000 mm of water annually in a region receiving only 200 mm of rain. Over 80% of agricultural water comes from groundwater now suffering salinization and nitrate contamination.

### Transboundary water disputes with Portugal intensify

Spain and Portugal share five major river basins governed by the 1998 Albufeira Convention. The convention's institutional weaknesses are severe: the UNECE found no integrated basin management plans, no functioning secretariat, no public participation mechanisms, and a website unupdated since 2020. In September 2024, environment ministers signed new agreements at the convention's 25th anniversary, including Spain agreeing to pay Portugal **€2 million annually** for water abstracted from the Alqueva reservoir. Portuguese civic movements have demanded cessation of what they describe as twenty years of illegal pumping by Spanish farmers. Water pollution compounds tensions — Tagus waters flowing to Portugal are "highly contaminated," and in 2023, **161 villages** in Salamanca and Zamora lost water supply due to pesticide contamination.

### The October 2024 Valencia DANA exposed institutional failure

On October 29, 2024, a DANA (cut-off low-pressure system) dropped **500+ mm in 8 hours** on parts of Valencia. Turís recorded **772 mm in 24 hours** — the second-highest daily total in Spain's history. Between **223 and 237 people** were killed, most in Valencia's Horta Sud area, particularly Paiporta (~40 deaths). Nearly **1 million people** were impacted and **15,000 displaced**.

Institutional failures compounded the disaster. The PP-led Valencian government of **Carlos Mazón** had disbanded the Valencian Emergencies Unit established after 2019 floods, dismissing its €9 million budget as a "superfluous expense." Critical delays occurred between AEMET's meteorological warnings and civil protection alerts to citizens. Political consequences were severe: crowds threw mud at **King Felipe VI and Queen Letizia** during their visit to Paiporta. The Sánchez government approved **€10.6 billion** in fiscal aid, and Labour Minister **Yolanda Díaz** introduced "paid climate leave" of up to four days during weather emergencies. A Nature Communications study confirmed human-caused climate change intensified the flooding.

### Emissions decline as renewables surge, but transport lags

Spain's total GHG emissions fell to **279.9 MtCO₂-eq in 2024**, a net reduction of **39.3% from 2005** — above the EU average of 30.5%. The electricity sector drove most gains, cutting emissions 57% between 2008 and 2020 as **renewables reached 56% of net electricity generation in 2024**, making Spain the EU leader in solar share at 21%. Including nuclear, **80% of power generation was emission-free**. Transport (32.5% of emissions) remains the stubborn laggard — EV adoption stands at only **11.4%** of new car sales, below the EU average.

The EU Just Transition Fund allocated **€869 million** to Spain for coal-region adjustment. Coal employment collapsed from **45,000 in 1990** to fewer than **300 by 2023**, with only one mine remaining in Asturias. Spain is on track to phase out coal power ahead of its 2030 target. Specific transition projects include AI innovation hubs in former mining areas and offshore renewable energy development in A Coruña.

## SECTION 9: ILLICIT NETWORKS & SHADOW GOVERNANCE

### Spain has reclaimed its position as Europe's primary cocaine gateway

**In 2023, Spanish authorities seized a historic 142 tonnes of cocaine — surpassing Belgium (121 tonnes) and the Netherlands (60 tonnes) — confirming Spain's status as Europe's principal drug entry point.** Organized crime generates an estimated €139 billion in illicit proceeds annually across the EU, with authorities recovering only 2.1%. Spain ranks **fifth out of 44 European countries** on the 2023 Global Organized Crime Index, behind Serbia, Italy, Ukraine, and Russia. The country's Transparency International CPI score has dropped from 65 (2012) to **55 (2025)**, reflecting not only drug trafficking but endemic corruption scandals that have engulfed both major parties.

### Cocaine flows through Algeciras and Galicia on an industrial scale

Algeciras, Spain's busiest port and Europe's seventh-largest, is the epicenter. In October 2024, authorities made Spain's **largest-ever single cocaine seizure — 13 tonnes** concealed in bananas from Ecuador. In August 2023, Operation Nano netted 9.5 tonnes involving **30 different criminal organizations**. Increased security at Antwerp and Rotterdam has redirected trafficking through Spanish ports. Between January 2019 and June 2024, Spanish seaports accounted for at least **388 tonnes** of seized cocaine.

Galicia's role has evolved from the ostentatious 1980s-era clans — Sito Miñanco, Laureano Oubiña, the Charlín family — to more discreet networks offering maritime logistics services. The infrastructure remains potent: in December 2023, Operation Nazare dismantled a Galician clan seizing 2+ tonnes; in August 2023, **Manuel Charlín** (85 years old, the Netflix *Cocaine Coast* figure) was arrested off the Azores with 2.4 tonnes; in January 2025, a speedboat carrying 1+ tonne was intercepted at Vilanova de Arousa. Between 2023 and 2025, **21 cocaine laboratories** were dismantled across Spain, nine in Galicia, including Europe's largest lab in Pontevedra capable of producing 200 kg per day. Colombian chemists, Mexican cartel financing (Sinaloa, CJNG), and Albanian distribution networks operate through Spanish territory.

Cannabis and hashish flow from Morocco through the Strait of Gibraltar. The Campo de Gibraltar area recorded **150,802 kg of hashish** and **44,206 kg of cocaine** seized in 2024 across 12,068 operations (up 43% from 2023). In February 2024, two Guardia Civil officers were killed at Barbate port when a narco-launch deliberately rammed their patrol boat. Over **20 deaths** from drug trafficking in the Strait have occurred since 2016. La Línea de la Concepción's unemployment exceeds **30%**, creating fertile ground for recruitment. The area's Special Security Plan, launched in 2018, allocated **€200 million** for 2024–2025.

### Corruption is structural in Spain's political economy, not merely peripheral

Both major parties have been consumed by corruption scandals spanning decades. The **Gürtel case** revealed that the PP maintained parallel bookkeeping ("Caja B") **from 1982 to 2009**, funded by illegal construction-company donations. Party treasurer **Luis Bárcenas** was sentenced to 33 years and fined €44 million, having held **€48 million in Swiss bank accounts**. Mastermind Francisco Correa received 51 years. The scandal led to PM Rajoy's ouster via Spain's first-ever successful no-confidence vote in June 2018.

The **ERE case** — Spain's largest corruption case by monetary scope — found that PSOE Andalusian governments diverted **€680 million** in public unemployment funds to party-aligned individuals between 2001 and 2010. Former presidents **José Antonio Griñán** (6 years prison) and **Manuel Chaves** (9-year political ban) were among 21 convicted officials. Spain's Constitutional Court later overturned several convictions on procedural grounds in 2024, generating fierce political controversy.

The **Koldo case** (ongoing since February 2024) is the most significant active scandal, centering on fraudulent COVID-19 mask contracts worth **€53 million**. Former Transport Minister **José Luis Ábalos** was indicted in October 2024; PSOE organization secretary **Santos Cerdán** resigned after leaked recordings. Businessman **Víctor de Aldama** testified he paid bribes to multiple officials, including Ábalos (€400,000). October 2025 police reports revealed Ábalos received cash in PSOE-branded envelopes, with codewords: €500 notes called "txistorras," €200 notes "suns," €100 notes "lettuces." Connected threads include the Begoña Gómez influence-peddling investigation (PM Sánchez's wife), the prosecution of Attorney General **Álvaro García Ortiz** for leaking confidential information, and scrutiny of the €1.1 billion Globalia/Air Europa bailout.

### Money laundering thrives through real estate on the Costa del Sol

Spain's property market — particularly Marbella, Barcelona, and the Balearics — remains highly vulnerable to money laundering. Russian organized crime has been entrenched since the 1990s. Operation Troika (2008), Judge Garzón's massive raid, collapsed in 2018 when charges against 17 accused couldn't be proven. In July 2024, **Dmitry Artyakov** (son of a senior Rostec executive) was arrested in Girona with €200,000+ in cash. In 2025, police raided Mallorca villas linked to sanctioned Russian defense figure **Nikolai Kolesov**. Operation White Lion (2024) dismantled what authorities called "the largest underground bank serving organized crime in Europe," with 80+ arrests and €100+ million in frozen assets.

**SEPBLAC**, Spain's financial intelligence unit, receives generally favorable FATF assessments — Spain is compliant or largely compliant on **38 of 40 FATF Recommendations**. However, Spain still lacks a national anti-corruption strategy, a dedicated independent anti-corruption authority, and robust conflicts-of-interest legislation. The revolving door between politics and business is systemic, particularly in energy, telecoms, construction, and banking — research by ESADE found that two-thirds of Spain's politically connected firms employ former public servants on their boards. The **King Juan Carlos I scandal** — involving an alleged $100 million Saudi "gift," €65 million transferred to a former lover, and subsequent exile to Abu Dhabi since August 2020 — epitomizes elite impunity, as prosecutors shelved all investigations citing statute of limitations and constitutional immunity.

## SECTION 10: INFORMATION ECOSYSTEM & MEDIA STRUCTURE

### Spain's media system exemplifies the Polarized Pluralist model — and is becoming more fragmented

**Spain's media landscape features high political parallelism, increasing ownership concentration by international financial actors, declining public trust (only 33% of Spaniards trust news), and a digital transformation that has empowered both investigative journalism and disinformation.** The country's media system closely fits the Hallin & Mancini Mediterranean/Polarized Pluralist model: outlets have clear but often unacknowledged ideological alignments, state intervention shapes public broadcasting governance, professional self-regulation is weak, and journalists routinely cross into politics. Spain ranks **23rd globally** on the 2025 RSF Press Freedom Index (score: 77.35), up from 30th in 2024, with its weakest sub-indicator being economic freedom (rank 34).

The print/digital landscape divides sharply along ideological lines. **El País** (PRISA Group; majority shareholder Joseph Oughourlian of Amber Capital at ~29.6%, Vivendi SE at ~11.9%) remains Spain's largest newspaper with **400,000+ digital subscribers** and a center-left alignment historically linked to the PSOE. **El Mundo** (Unidad Editorial/RCS MediaGroup, Italian-owned) occupies center-right territory with 163,000 digital subscribers. **ABC** (Vocento) is conservative and monarchist. **La Razón** (Grupo Planeta) aligns closely with PP; its editor **Francisco Marhuenda** previously served as chief of staff in a PP government — emblematic of Spain's political parallelism. **La Vanguardia** (Grupo Godó, Barcelona) offers a centrist Catalan perspective with 145,000 digital subscribers and is one of only two major outlets edited outside Madrid.

Digital-native outlets have reshaped the ecosystem. **elDiario.es**, founded in 2012 by Ignacio Escolar with roughly 12 staff, now employs 92 journalists across all 17 autonomous communities, reaches **17 million+ monthly unique visitors**, and sustains itself through **106,000+ paying members** averaging €71.50/year. Revenue hit €15.5 million in 2024 with €1.53 million profit — a rare success story for membership-funded journalism. On the right, **OKDiario** (founded 2015 by Eduardo Inda) has been rated Spain's worst-valued news outlet by the University of Valencia and carries a "Low" factual reporting rating from Media Bias/Fact Check. **El Español** (founded by former El Mundo editor Pedro J. Ramírez) and **El Confidencial** compete as center-right digital outlets with strong readership.

### Television audiences fragment as Atresmedia dominates and RTVE politicization deepens

**Antena 3** (Atresmedia/Grupo Planeta) led television ratings for a fourth consecutive year in 2025 with **12.8% share**, and is the most trusted outlet in Spain (64% trust per Pew) — a European anomaly where a private broadcaster beats the public one. **Telecinco** (Mediaset España/MFE-MediaForEurope, Berlusconi family heirs) hit a historic low of **9.5%**. RTVE's La 1 recovered to **11.0%**, its best since 2012, partly due to the acquisition of David Broncano's late-night show for €12 million/year. Combined, Atresmedia (26.1%) and Mediaset (24.5%) hold roughly half the television market, with RTVE at 16.5%.

RTVE's governance remains deeply contested. A Royal Decree-Law in October 2024 expanded the board from 10 to 15 members and lowered the appointment threshold from a two-thirds majority to a simple absolute majority — a change press freedom organizations including the International Press Institute condemned as enabling partisan control. **José Pablo López** was appointed president in December 2024. The State Media Monitor 2025 found that **8 of 15 regional public broadcasters** are now classified as "State Controlled," up from just 1 in 2022, with most takeovers by PP-led regional administrations. RTVE has operated without commercial advertising since 2010, relying on state budget subsidies and levies on private broadcasters, leaving it perpetually vulnerable to political pressure.

### Social media reshapes political discourse as disinformation surges

**WhatsApp** dominates with **91%+ penetration** among internet users, serving as a primary news distribution channel. **TikTok** was the "big winner of 2024" with 66% growth in views, but also a critical disinformation vector — Maldita.es identified **129 hoax videos** with 10,000+ views during the Valencia floods alone. **X/Twitter** is declining but remains important for polarized political discourse, with the most viral flood disinformation coming from monetized (paid-badge) accounts. **Telegram** has become a significant political communication channel; far-right MEP **Alvise Pérez** built his following through it. In February 2026, Telegram founder Pavel Durov sent a mass message to all Spanish users attacking government plans for social media regulation.

The October 2024 Valencia DANA was a watershed disinformation event. **Maldita.es identified 112+ distinct disinformation narratives**, including HAARP weather-manipulation conspiracies, false claims that the Sánchez government had demolished dams, accusations that Morocco orchestrated the floods, and fake emergency numbers used for scams. Russian amplification was confirmed, with initial distribution traced to Pravda channels. Platform responses were inadequate: no visible action on 75% of YouTube disinformation and 70% on X. Spain's fact-checking ecosystem — led by **Maldita.es** (IFCN-accredited), **Newtral** (founded by journalist Ana Pastor), **IBERIFIER**, and **EFE Verifica** — was overwhelmed by the volume.

### Regional media ecosystems in Catalonia and the Basque Country operate as distinct information spheres

**TV3** (Corporació Catalana de Mitjans Audiovisuals) has been the most-watched channel in Catalonia for **16 consecutive years** with ~14.2% share, ahead of all national channels. Its 3Cat digital platform has 1.6 million registered users. Catalan-language digital outlets — **VilaWeb**, **Ara**, **El Nacional**, **NacióDigital** — create a distinct information sphere largely invisible to Spanish-language audiences. Catalonia is one of only three regions with an independent audiovisual authority (CAC) and the only one with a quantitative limit on media ownership concentration (50% cap).

In the Basque Country, **EITB** (Euskal Irrati Telebista) operates ETB 1 (Basque-language) and ETB 2 (Spanish), reaching 300,000+ daily radio listeners. **Berria** serves as the daily of record for the Basque-language ecosystem. Both regional systems capture higher audience loyalty than national channels within their territories.

## SECTION 11: INTERNATIONAL INSTITUTIONAL COMMITMENTS

### Spain punches below its weight in NATO but leveraged its EU presidency to shape the Union's agenda

**Spain — the EU's fourth-largest member by population with 61 MEPs and approximately 10.5% of EU population — combines significant institutional weight with chronic underinvestment in defense, a tension that reached crisis point in March 2026 when it denied US forces access to jointly operated military bases during strikes on Iran.** Spain belatedly reached the NATO 2% GDP target in 2025 after a 43% year-over-year spending increase, only to become the sole NATO member refusing the new 5% GDP target adopted at The Hague Summit. Its July–December 2023 EU Council presidency achieved landmark results including the opening of Ukraine/Moldova accession talks, the Migration and Asylum Pact, the AI Act agreement, and Bulgaria/Romania's Schengen integration.

Within the EU, Spain supports extending qualified majority voting to foreign and security policy, as a member of the "Group of Friends on QMV." It joined the Atlantic Group (with Portugal, Ireland, Belgium, Netherlands, Denmark) conditioning eastward enlargement on institutional and budgetary reform. Spain's public debt at **~101.8% of GDP** in 2024 exceeds the 60% SGP reference value, though it has benefited from the reformed EU fiscal framework that replaced rigid numerical targets with country-specific adjustment paths. Spain's Recovery and Resilience Plan totals **€165.5 billion** (€80.9 billion in grants, €83.7 billion in loans), with 40% allocated to green transition.

### NATO membership has been politically fraught from day one

Spain joined NATO on May 30, 1982 under PM Calvo-Sotelo. The PSOE under Felipe González had campaigned "No to NATO" but reversed course in power, holding a referendum on March 12, 1986 that narrowly carried **52.5% to 39.8%** (turnout: 59.4%) under conditions: no integration into NATO's military command structure, no nuclear weapons on Spanish soil, and gradual reduction of US military presence. Spain remained outside the integrated command until 1997 under PM Aznar.

Defense spending languished for over a decade — 2022 spending matched 2008 levels in real terms. At the 2024 Washington Summit, Spain spent only **~1.28% of GDP**. PM Sánchez announced in April 2025 an Industrial and Technological Plan for Security and Defence adding ~€10.5 billion, and by end-2025 NATO confirmed Spain had reached **2% of GDP** (approximately €33.1 billion). However, at the June 2025 Hague Summit, when NATO adopted a **5% GDP target by 2035** (3.5% core defense, 1.5% broader security), Spain was the only member to refuse. Sánchez called the commitment "unreasonable and counterproductive" in a letter to Secretary General Rutte. In October 2025, Trump suggested NATO should consider Spain's expulsion.

### The March 2026 base access denial marks the worst US-Spain crisis in decades

When the US and Israel commenced missile strikes against Iran on February 28, 2026, Sánchez refused to allow US forces to use Rota and Morón for operations. On March 2, Foreign Minister **José Manuel Albares** formalized the denial. Trump responded on March 3 by threatening to "cut off all trade with Spain" and making what many interpreted as a sovereignty threat: "We could use their base if we want. We could just fly in and use it. Nobody's going to tell us not to use it." The White House claimed on March 4 that Spain had agreed to cooperate; Spain "categorically" denied this. Defense Minister **Margarita Robles** stated: "No single country should act as a guardian of the world." US aircraft repositioned from Morón to Ramstein. As of early March 2026, the crisis remained unresolved.

**Rota Naval Station** hosts four (expanding to six) US Navy Aegis BMD-capable Arleigh Burke-class destroyers — core to NATO's ballistic missile defense against Iran and Russia. The fifth destroyer (USS Oscar Austin) arrived in fall 2024; the sixth is due in 2026. **Morón Air Base** hosts the SPMAGTF-CR-AF with capacity for up to **3,500 Marines and 35 aircraft**. Both bases operate under Spanish sovereignty via the 1988 Agreement on Defense Cooperation, which requires mutual consultation for expansions — a legal framework Spain invoked to deny access.

### Spain's foreign policy balances Western alignment with Global South engagement

Spain recognized Palestinian statehood on **May 28, 2024**, alongside Ireland and Norway, based on pre-1967 borders with East Jerusalem as capital. Israel recalled its ambassador. In March 2022, Sánchez reversed Spain's decades-long Western Sahara position, recognizing Morocco's autonomy plan as "the most serious, realistic and credible basis" for resolution — ending a diplomatic crisis triggered by Morocco's weaponization of migration (10,000+ migrants allowed into Ceuta in May 2021).

Spain participates in **44 PESCO projects** — third-most of any EU member after France (52) and Italy (51). The Ibero-American system, with **SEGIB** headquartered in Madrid and Spain as its principal architect, provides unique European access to Latin America. The Union for the Mediterranean secretariat in Barcelona reflects Spain's central Mediterranean role. Spain has served on the UN Security Council five times, most recently **2015–2016**, and has accepted compulsory ICJ jurisdiction with standard reservations.

## SECTION 12: MILITARY & SECURITY SECTOR DNA

### Spain's military is thoroughly depoliticized but chronically under-resourced

**Spain's Armed Forces — approximately 120,000–122,000 active personnel across Army, Navy, and Air and Space Force — underwent complete depoliticization after the failed 23-F coup of 1981 and present zero coup risk today.** The professional military has struggled with chronic recruitment shortfalls since conscription was abolished in 2001, maintains a reserve force of only **3,000–5,000** (experts recommend 20,000–50,000), and deployed approximately **3,000–4,000 personnel** across 16 missions on four continents as of 2025. Defense Minister **Margarita Robles** oversees a force led by JEMAD Admiral General **Teodoro E. López Calderón**.

The post-Franco transition fundamentally reshaped civil-military relations. The 1978 Constitution placed armed forces under civilian authority. The February 23, 1981 coup attempt — when Lieutenant Colonel **Antonio Tejero** stormed the Congress of Deputies and King Juan Carlos I ordered troops to their barracks on television — became the defining moment of Spain's democratic consolidation. NATO integration from 1982 onward served as a powerful civilianizing force, exposing Spanish officers to allied democratic norms. The March 2026 base crisis demonstrated that military leadership follows government directives without hesitation, even on politically fraught decisions.

### Major procurement programs face mixed fortunes

The **F-110 Bonifaz-class frigate program** represents Spain's largest current naval procurement: €4.325 billion for five frigates, the first ships globally to carry the AN/SPY-7(V)2 radar integrated with the Aegis combat system. The lead ship F111 Bonifaz was launched September 2025 (over 70% complete), with three frigates now under simultaneous construction at Navantia's Ferrol yard. Deliveries are scheduled one per year from 2028 through 2032.

The **S-80 Isaac Peral-class submarine program** became synonymous with procurement dysfunction — original delivery dates of 2011–2018 slipped by over a decade after excessive weight issues required General Dynamics Electric Boat assistance and hull lengthening from 71m to 80.8m. Costs escalated from the original €1.756 billion to approximately **€4 billion** (€977 million per unit). S-81 Isaac Peral was finally commissioned November 2023 and made its first NATO deployment in October 2025. S-82 Narciso Monturiol was launched October 2025, with S-83 and S-84 expected in 2028 and 2029.

Spain operates Eurofighter Typhoons across multiple wings, with **Project Halcón** acquiring 20 additional aircraft with AESA radar for €906 million. The **Future Combat Air System (FCAS)** — a trilateral program with France (Dassault) and Germany (Airbus) estimated at ~€100 billion — faces existential Franco-German industrial disputes. In October 2025, German Defense Minister Pistorius publicly threatened to end the program. Spain, through **Indra** (sensors, >€600 million share) and **Airbus Spain** (low-observable technologies), has committed to FCAS and ruled out the F-35. By December 2025, France and Spain reaffirmed commitment, but Germany remained silent.

In December 2025, the government approved **31 Special Modernization Programs (PEMs)**, including SDR radios (€1.421 billion via Indra), 23 NH-90 helicopters (€1.3 billion), Santiago II electronic warfare systems (€373 million), MH-60R naval helicopters, Pizarro IFV modernization (€263 million), C-295 transport aircraft (€1.209 billion), and 30 Hürjet trainers from Turkish Aerospace Industries.

### The defense industry anchors Spain's strategic autonomy ambitions

**Navantia**, the state-owned shipbuilder, operates yards at Ferrol (frigates), San Fernando/Puerto Real (corvettes), and Cartagena (submarines). Its **Alsarawat program** — five Avante 2200 corvettes for Saudi Arabia at €1.8 billion — was the largest export contract in Spanish shipyard history. Navantia's Hobart-class program for Australia and Fridtjof Nansen-class frigates for Norway established its international reputation. **Airbus Spain** (Getafe) is critical to A400M and Eurofighter production. **Indra Sistemas**, Spain's leading defense electronics company, was the largest beneficiary of the 2025 PEMs. **GDELS-Santa Bárbara Sistemas** (General Dynamics subsidiary) produces Leopard 2E tanks and Pizarro IFVs. Spain was the world's **seventh-largest arms exporter** in the 2015–2019 SIPRI period and Saudi Arabia's second-largest supplier (10% of Saudi imports) in 2020–2024.

### Overseas deployments span four continents with UNIFIL as the flagship

Spain leads the Multinational Brigade East in **UNIFIL Lebanon** with **~660–700 troops** at the "Miguel de Cervantes" base — the third-largest European contributor. In the NATO eastern flank, approximately **600 troops** serve in Latvia's Forward Land Forces, with 128+ personnel and 8 Eurofighters conducting Baltic Air Policing from Šiauliai. A **Patriot battery** deployed to Turkey under NATO Active Fence provided data enabling US forces to intercept an Iranian ballistic missile during the March 2026 crisis — demonstrating that Spain didn't completely disengage from allied operations even while denying base access. Additional deployments include approximately 400 troops in Iraq, support to EU operations in Somalia (EUNAVFOR Atalanta, commanded from Rota), and training missions in Mali and the Central African Republic.

### Intelligence and law enforcement face accountability deficits

The **CNI** (Centro Nacional de Inteligencia), directed by **Esperanza Casteleiro** since May 2022, operates under a 1968 Official Secrets Act that classifies all CNI information as secret — widely criticized as inadequate. The **Pegasus scandal** revealed two strands: CNI legally surveilled at least 18 of 63+ targeted Catalan/Basque independence figures (2017–2020), while PM Sánchez's own phone and those of Ministers Robles and Grande-Marlaska were compromised by an apparent foreign state actor. On **January 23, 2026**, Audiencia Nacional Judge **José Luis Calama** shelved the investigation for the second time, citing Israel's refusal to cooperate on five requests for judicial assistance sent between May 2022 and February 2025. Separately, a Barcelona court in March 2025 ordered the first-ever indictment of NSO Group executives.

The **Guardia Civil** (~77,000–80,000 personnel) operates as a national gendarmerie with military status under the Interior Ministry (Defense Ministry in wartime), covering rural areas, highways, borders, and coasts. The **Policía Nacional** (~87,000 officers) handles provincial capitals and urban areas. Autonomous police forces — the **Mossos d'Esquadra** (~18,000, Catalonia), **Ertzaintza** (~8,000, Basque Country), and **Policía Foral** (Navarre) — assume full policing duties in their territories. Spain has not joined the German-led **European Sky Shield Initiative**, aligning with France and Italy in concerns about over-reliance on non-European defense systems, though it recently signed a **$1.7 billion Patriot system** deal with the United States.

## Conclusion

These five dimensions of Spain's structural profile reveal a country defined by acute tensions between ambition and capacity. Environmentally, Spain faces existential water and climate threats that its institutional framework — fragmented between 17 autonomous communities — struggles to address. Its illicit economy is not peripheral but intertwined with political party financing, real estate markets, and port infrastructure at a scale that structural reforms have failed to contain. The media ecosystem's sharp polarization and declining trust (33%) undermines democratic deliberation precisely when complex policy trade-offs — on water, defense, immigration — require informed publics. Internationally, Spain leverages EU institutional weight and Latin American soft power but chronically underinvests in hard security, producing the NATO spending crisis and the March 2026 base confrontation that together represent the most serious test of the transatlantic relationship from a Southern European ally. The military itself is thoroughly depoliticized and professionally competent but structurally undersized, with a reserve force of barely 3,000–5,000 in a continent rearming for potential great-power competition. The common thread is a gap between Spain's fourth-largest-EU-economy status and the institutional, fiscal, and political capital required to address converging environmental, security, and governance challenges simultaneously.

**Middle Powers Monitor — March 2026**

Spain enters 2026 as a paradox: Europe's fastest-growing major economy, led by a minority government that cannot pass a budget, embroiled in the most severe diplomatic rupture with the United States since the Iraq War. Pedro Sánchez's refusal to permit US strikes on Iran from Rota and Morón bases in March 2026 crystallized Spain's distinctive multi-vector positioning — a NATO member willing to defy Washington on international law grounds, shielded by EU collective trade policy, and motivated by deeply embedded anti-war collective memory. Beneath this headline crisis, Spain's civil society is undergoing a housing-driven mobilization cycle comparable to 15-M, its far right is surging to historic highs, and the constitutional settlement that has governed since 1978 faces accumulating stresses it was not designed to absorb.
