## SECTION 8: ENVIRONMENTAL & CLIMATIC STRUCTURE

### The monsoon remains India's most consequential single variable

Roughly **55–60% of India's net sown area** (139 million hectares) is rain-fed, and the southwest monsoon (June–September) delivers approximately **75% of total annual rainfall**. Agriculture contributes ~18% of GDP but employs 42–50% of the workforce, making monsoon performance a direct determinant of rural welfare, food inflation, fiscal policy, and political stability. The structural dependency is not merely economic — monsoon failures have repeatedly triggered governance crises. The 1965–66 drought produced the Bihar famine and forced India into humiliating "ship-to-mouth" food aid dependency on the United States, reshaping both domestic agricultural policy (the Green Revolution) and foreign policy posture. The 2009 late monsoon caused a 22% decline in rice yields and forced emergency procurement interventions. The 2015 drought affected **334 million people** across India, with only 51% of districts receiving adequate rainfall.

Recent monsoon performance has been mixed but broadly favorable. The 2024 monsoon delivered **108% of the Long Period Average (LPA)**, the best distribution in over a decade, though spatial unevenness persisted — central and southern India received excess rains (15% above LPA) while the northeast experienced shortfalls. The 2025 monsoon onset arrived 8 days early (May 24), the earliest since 2009, with IMD forecasting 105% of LPA. IMD's forecasting capacity has improved markedly: **100% accuracy** in seasonal forecasts from 2021–2024 (within ±5% margin), supported by 47 operational Doppler Weather Radars covering ~87% of the country, up from roughly 15 in 2013–14. However, climate change is altering monsoon patterns in ways that undermine historical predictability. Northwest India has seen rainfall increases of 60–120 mm per decade since the 1950s, concentrated in more extreme precipitation events, while the Indo-Gangetic plains show declining totals. The frequency of compound drought-heatwave events has risen sharply over the past four decades.

### Transboundary water crises are intensifying on all three frontiers

India's water security is now under simultaneous pressure along the Indus (Pakistan), Brahmaputra (China), and Ganges (Bangladesh) basins, each escalating in distinct ways.

The **Indus Waters Treaty (IWT)**, signed in 1960 and long considered one of the world's most durable water-sharing arrangements, entered acute crisis following the April 22, 2025 Pahalgam terrorist attack. On April 23, India's Cabinet Committee on Security declared the treaty **"in abeyance with immediate effect"** — an unprecedented unilateral action conditioning water-sharing on Pakistan's counterterrorism conduct. India had already ceased Permanent Indus Commission meetings since 2023 and closed the Baglihar Dam on the Chenab. Pakistan called the suspension an "act of war." The Hague-based Court of Arbitration ruled on June 27, 2025 that the treaty contains no provision for unilateral abeyance and remains in full force; India "categorically rejected" the ruling. The practical impact remains constrained — India lacks the infrastructure to fully divert the western rivers allocated to Pakistan — but the political signal was transformative.

On the Brahmaputra, China officially approved construction of the **Motuo mega-dam** in December 2024 — the world's largest hydropower project at **60,000 MW** capacity (triple the Three Gorges Dam), with an estimated cost of $137–167 billion. The Brahmaputra supports approximately 130 million people and 6 million hectares of farmland in India's northeast. India responded by launching a **$77 billion hydropower initiative** to construct 200+ dams in the northeast with 75 GW planned capacity — a counter-infrastructure strategy. No binding water-sharing treaty exists between China and any downstream neighbor; only a 2006 Expert Level Mechanism for hydrological data sharing.

The **1996 Ganges Water Treaty with Bangladesh expires at the end of 2026**, and renewal negotiations are complicated by the deterioration of India-Bangladesh relations following Sheikh Hasina's ouster in August 2024. The Teesta River dispute remains unresolved since 2011 due to West Bengal Chief Minister Mamata Banerjee's opposition. Bangladesh's new government has been improving ties with China and Pakistan, adding geopolitical complexity to what was already a technically difficult negotiation.

### Climate vulnerability is accelerating across every indicator

India recorded **extreme weather events on 314 of 365 days in 2024**, killing 2,933 people, 92,000 animals, and destroying 2 million hectares of crops. The year 2024 was the hottest on record globally and in India, with New Delhi reaching **49.9°C** and Churu, Rajasthan hitting 50.5°C. Heatwave days have increased more than 55% over the past decade, with Indians experiencing an average of 19.8 heatwave days each in 2024. The labor impact is staggering: heat exposure in 2024 resulted in the loss of an estimated **247 billion potential labor hours** — a record, with agriculture accounting for 66% and construction 20% of the losses.

Sea-level rise threatens India's coastal megacities. Over **18 million people** live less than one meter above sea level. Mumbai and Kolkata face the highest compound flood risks from sea-level rise, subsidence, and storm surge. India experienced **5.4 million internal climate displacements** in 2024, the highest in South Asia. The Wayanad landslide of July 30, 2024 — triggered by 500mm of rainfall in 48 hours — killed over 350 people in Kerala, illustrating how extreme precipitation events are intensifying.

India's groundwater situation presents a paradox. India is the **world's largest groundwater user** at ~230 km³/year (over 25% of global extraction). The 2024 CGWB assessment shows 11.13% of assessment units are "over-exploited," down from 17.24% in 2017, but Punjab, Haryana, and Rajasthan still show extraction rates exceeding 100%. GRACE satellite data reveals a net loss of **450 km³ of groundwater** between 2002 and 2022. Himalayan glacier retreat is accelerating, threatening the long-term viability of the Ganga, Brahmaputra, and Indus systems that sustain hundreds of millions.

### India's climate policy achieves targets while dodging structural transformation

India's renewable energy deployment has been genuinely impressive. Non-fossil fuel capacity reached **262.74 GW (51.5% of total installed capacity)** by November 2025, achieving the 50% target **five years ahead** of the 2030 NDC deadline. Solar capacity stands at 132.85 GW. On July 29, 2025, renewables met 51.5% of India's total electricity demand for the first time. Record additions of 44.51 GW of renewable energy were made through November 2025, nearly double the previous year.

Yet the structural contradiction is stark. Coal-fired capacity stands at ~215 GW, with **36 GW under construction** and government plans to add 100 GW of new coal capacity over the next seven years. Coal generates 75% of India's electricity. No coal phase-out plan exists. India's emissions intensity has already fallen 33% from 2005 levels, exceeding the original NDC target ahead of schedule — but Climate Action Tracker rates India's overall climate trajectory as "Insufficient," noting the targets were set low enough that current policies already exceed them without driving real emissions reductions. India has not submitted its 2035 target (due February 2025) as of March 2026. The net-zero 2070 target is 20 years later than most major economies' commitments.

### Air pollution kills more Indians than any single disease

The air pollution crisis dwarfs most other public health threats. Over **1.7 million deaths** were attributable to anthropogenic PM₂.₅ air pollution in India in 2022, a 38% increase since 2010, with an economic cost of **$339.4 billion (9.5% of GDP)**. The entire Indian population lives in areas exceeding WHO PM₂.₅ guidelines. Delhi recorded **zero "good" air quality days** for the entire year of 2025 by US EPA standards. In December 2025, GRAP Stage 4 restrictions were imposed as AQI levels exceeded 600 at multiple monitoring stations. Contrary to popular narrative, crop stubble burning has declined 77.5% since 2021 — vehicular emissions (51.5%) now dominate Delhi's pollution profile. The institutional response remains fragmented: heatwaves were not eligible for full SDRF/NDRF funding as of March 2025, and only 2 of 15 reviewed state heat action plans had undertaken proper vulnerability assessments.

India's disaster response capacity shows a notable bifurcation. The NDRF has developed into a credible rapid-response force, conducting over 12,000 operations since 2006 and rescuing more than 158,000 persons. Cyclone preparedness is a recognized global success story, with death tolls declining dramatically from the 1999 Odisha super-cyclone (~10,000 dead) to comparable subsequent storms. But capacity varies enormously between states, and the institutional framework remains stronger for acute events than for slow-onset threats like heat, groundwater depletion, and sea-level rise.

## SECTION 9: ILLICIT NETWORKS & SHADOW GOVERNANCE

### India's shadow economy is the largest proportional to GDP among major powers

The illicit and informal economy constitutes a deep structural feature of India's political economy, not merely a marginal phenomenon. The EY Global Shadow Economy Report (2025) values India's shadow economy at approximately **$931–951 billion, or 26–27% of GDP** — the third-largest globally in absolute terms but the largest as a share of GDP among the top ten economies. Academic estimates using broader methodologies place it as high as 41% of GDP. India's tax-to-GDP ratio remains approximately 6%, among the lowest for any major economy, partly reflecting the scale of informal activity.

Narcotics trafficking exploits India's geographic position between the Golden Crescent (Afghanistan-Pakistan-Iran) and Golden Triangle (Myanmar-Laos-Thailand). Myanmar supplies an estimated 80% of India's heroin. Record drug seizures worth **₹16,914 crore (~$2 billion)** were reported in 2024 — a 55% increase. Punjab remains the epicenter: police seized 1,150 kg of heroin in 2024, and the BSF recorded **163 drone-borne drug smuggling cases**, up from just 3 in 2021. An estimated 10% of Punjab's youth are affected by drug addiction. International cartel penetration is deepening — a methamphetamine lab linked to Mexico's Jalisco cartel was discovered in Greater Noida in 2024.

Sand mining has become one of India's most violent illicit industries. Between December 2020 and March 2022 alone, **418 people were killed and 438 injured** in sand mining-related incidents. Since 2014, at least 70 people have been killed by sand miners, including 7 police officers and multiple journalists. The iron ore scandal in Bellary, Karnataka documented by the Lokayukta exposed ₹16,085 crore in losses to the state exchequer, while the Shah Commission found Goa lost ₹35,000 crore. Illegal rat-hole coal mining in Meghalaya continues despite a 2014 NGT ban — a February 2026 dynamite explosion at an illegal mine killed 27–30 miners, six years after the 2018 Ksan disaster that trapped 15.

### The criminalization of politics is accelerating, not declining

The most structurally significant dimension of India's illicit networks is their integration into formal democratic politics. According to the Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR), **251 of 543 newly elected Lok Sabha MPs (46%)** in the 2024 elections declared pending criminal cases — the **highest proportion ever recorded**, up from 43% in 2019, 34% in 2014, and 23% in 2004. Of these, **170 MPs (31%)** face serious charges including murder, attempted murder, rape, and kidnapping — a 124% increase since 2009. Candidates with criminal cases had a 15.3% win rate versus just 4.4% for clean candidates, indicating that criminality confers electoral advantage through the capacity to mobilize resources, intimidate, and deliver patronage. Across parties, 93% of winners are crorepatis (worth over ₹1 crore), with average declared assets of ₹46.34 crore.

The deployment of the **Enforcement Directorate (ED)** and CBI as instruments of political control represents a distinct phenomenon from individual criminality. Between April 2015 and February 2025, the ED registered **193 cases against politicians** — yielding only **2 convictions**, a rate barely above 1%. An Indian Express investigation found that of 121 politicians investigated by the ED since 2014, **115 (95%) were from opposition parties**. During the UPA period (2004–2014), only 26 political leaders were investigated, with 54% from the opposition. ED caseloads exploded from 209 ECIRs in 2013–14 to 1,180 in 2021–22. Notable targets include Delhi Chief Minister Arvind Kejriwal (arrested March 2024), Jharkhand Chief Minister Hemant Soren (arrested January 2024), and Rahul Gandhi. The "washing machine" phenomenon — opposition politicians seeing investigations diminish after joining the BJP coalition — is documented most prominently in Maharashtra's NCP split. Fourteen opposition parties petitioned the Supreme Court in March 2023, presenting statistical evidence of systematic weaponization.

### Naxal insurgency approaches its end state, but at a human cost

The Naxalite/Maoist insurgency, once affecting ~180 districts, has been reduced to **6 "most affected" districts** as of April 2025, with an estimated **500 active fighters** remaining (down from 30,000+ at peak). Operation Kagaar, launched January 2024 as the "final mission" to eradicate Left-Wing Extremism by March 2026, deployed approximately **100,000 personnel** and has killed over 300 Maoists, arrested 1,033, and induced 925 surrenders. CPI-Maoist General Secretary Nambala Keshava Rao was killed in May 2025. The movement's own 20th anniversary statement in September 2024 acknowledged shrinking territory and public base. The total conflict toll since 2000 exceeds **12,100 deaths** — including 4,134 civilians, 2,722 security forces, and 4,994 Naxalites. Human rights concerns persist: bounty systems (up to ₹25 lakh per dead Naxalite) incentivize lethal operations, and independent verification of encounter claims remains inadequate.

India's anti-corruption institutional framework is strikingly weak relative to the scale of corruption. The **Lokpal**, established by the 2013 Act after years of civil society agitation, did not receive its first appointee until 2019 — five years after the law's passage. Of 8,703 complaints received, ~68% were disposed without action; only **7 cases reached prosecution stage** with zero convictions. Its prosecution wing was constituted only in 2025. The CBI, famously described by the Supreme Court as a **"caged parrot speaking in its master's voice"** (2013), operates under direct executive control with no operational autonomy statute. Several states have withdrawn consent to CBI investigations. India's Corruption Perceptions Index score of **38 (ranked 96th of 180)** has stagnated over the past decade.

## SECTION 10: INFORMATION ECOSYSTEM & MEDIA STRUCTURE

### Two conglomerates now dominate India's information architecture

The defining structural development in India's media landscape is the consolidation of ownership by industrial conglomerates with deep dependencies on government contracts and regulatory favor. **Reliance Industries** (Mukesh Ambani) controls over **70 media outlets** reaching at least **800 million Indians** through its Network18 subsidiary, acquired via a complex financial maneuver beginning in 2012 with a ₹5,400 crore infusion. Properties include CNN-News18, News18 India, CNBC-TV18, Firstpost, Moneycontrol, and multiple ETV regional channels. The November 2024 completion of the **$8.5 billion Reliance-Disney joint venture** — merging Viacom18 and Star India into an entity with 100+ TV channels and the JioCinema/Hotstar streaming platforms — further consolidated entertainment and sports broadcasting.

The **Adani Group** acquired a controlling 64.71% stake in NDTV through a complex chain originating in a 2009 loan with conversion rights, completing the takeover in December 2022. Founders Prannoy Roy and Radhika Roy resigned alongside four independent directors. The editorial consequences were immediate: senior journalists including Magsaysay Award–winner **Ravish Kumar** (26 years at NDTV), Sreenivasan Jain (nearly three decades), executive editor Nidhi Razdan, and group president Suparna Singh all resigned within weeks. Kumar observed: "How can a channel, bought by a corporation whose success is seen to be linked to contracts granted by the government, now criticise the government?" Adani also acquired full control of Quintillion Business Media (BQ Prime). No Indian laws regulate cross-holdings between media and industrial conglomerates.

Other major houses include the **Times Group** (Sahu Jain family — Times of India, Economic Times, Times Now), **India Today Group** (Purie family — Aaj Tak, India Today TV), **Zee Media** (Essel Group — facing financial difficulties after the Zee-Sony merger collapsed in 2024), and regional powerhouses like **ABP Group** (Bengali), **The Hindu Group** (Chennai), **Sun TV Network** (Tamil, with DMK political connections), and **Dainik Jagran/Dainik Bhaskar** (Hindi). India has approximately **140,000 publications** in 20+ languages and ~900 privately owned TV channels, half dedicated to news.

### Press freedom has deteriorated sharply under the Modi government

The quantitative decline is documented across every major international assessment. India's RSF World Press Freedom Index ranking fell from **140th in 2014** (when Modi took office) to **161st in 2023**, with a modest recovery to 151st in 2025 that RSF itself describes as "deceptive." Freedom House **downgraded India from "Free" to "Partly Free" in 2021**, with a further 3-point decline in 2024 and a cumulative 15-point drop over the past decade. The V-Dem Institute classifies India as an **"Electoral Autocracy"** as of 2020. At least 91 journalists and media workers have been killed since CPJ began tracking in 1992, with 11 killed since 2019. At least 15 journalists have been charged under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act since 2014.

The state-media relationship operates through multiple control mechanisms beyond ownership. The **DAVP (Directorate of Advertising and Visual Publicity)** channels government advertising — a critical revenue source for smaller outlets — as both carrot and stick. In 2019, advertising was cut to the Times of India, The Hindu, and The Telegraph, with combined readership exceeding 26 million, in what media executives described as retaliation for critical reporting. **Modi has not held a single solo press conference since taking office in 2014** — an extraordinary anomaly for a democratic leader — preferring unilateral channels like the Mann Ki Baat radio show (reaching ~230 million listeners). The February 2023 tax raids on the BBC's Delhi and Mumbai offices, following its documentary critical of Modi's role in the 2002 Gujarat riots, exemplified the pattern. The NewsClick raid of October 2023, in which founder Prabir Purkayastha was arrested under UAPA before the Supreme Court declared his arrest "illegal" in May 2024, demonstrated the deployment of anti-terror legislation against media organizations.

### WhatsApp is India's most consequential political communication platform

India's digital information environment is shaped by WhatsApp's dominance — approximately **400 million monthly active users**, the world's largest market. The BJP operates at least **5 million WhatsApp groups**, with party leaders claiming messages can be disseminated from Delhi to any location in India within 12 minutes. During the 2019 elections, the party deployed ~900,000 "WhatsApp Pramukhs" (local leaders) managing groups at booth level. A Princeton Digital Witness Lab investigation (2024) found that in the small town of Mandi (population 26,000), BJP volunteers ran 400+ WhatsApp groups with a team of 500, segmenting voters by location, profession, age, religion, gender, caste, and tribe. The closed, encrypted nature of WhatsApp makes this content largely invisible to public scrutiny or fact-checking.

India leads the world in internet shutdowns — **855 distinct incidents since 2012**, averaging 65 per year and representing a 2,000% increase from 2012 (3 shutdowns). India held the global number-one position from 2018 through 2023 before falling to second behind Myanmar in 2024 (84 vs. 85 shutdowns). The Kashmir internet shutdown beginning August 5, 2019, lasting **552 days** of partial or no connectivity, was the **longest in any democracy**, costing the Kashmir economy an estimated ₹400 billion and 100,000+ jobs. The 2023 Manipur shutdown — over 5,000 hours across 200+ days — concealed evidence of atrocities, including a video of two women being paraded naked that took over two months to surface publicly.

The IT Rules of 2021 and 2023 amendments introduced a government-designated Fact Check Unit empowered to identify "fake or false or misleading" content about the Central Government, with social media platforms risking loss of safe harbor protection for non-compliance. In a landmark ruling on September 20, 2024, the Bombay High Court struck down the 2023 amendment by a 2:1 majority, declaring it violated constitutional protections for free speech, equality, and the right to trade. The Supreme Court had previously stayed the provision in March 2024. Key fact-checking organizations include **Alt News** (whose co-founder Mohammed Zubair was arrested in 2022 and faced sedition charges in December 2024 for flagging hate speech) and BOOM.

## SECTION 11: INTERNATIONAL INSTITUTIONAL COMMITMENTS & LEGAL FRAMEWORKS

### India's institutional positioning reflects calculated multi-alignment, not ambiguity

India's international institutional strategy is best understood as **multi-alignment** — the systematic cultivation of partnerships across competing geopolitical blocs while avoiding binding commitments to any single one. This is not residual non-alignment but an active, interest-driven hedging strategy that enables India to simultaneously deepen Quad cooperation with the United States, maintain a $69 billion bilateral trade relationship with Russia (2024), seek BRICS expansion on its terms, and position itself as the voice of the Global South. External Affairs Minister Jaishankar articulated the philosophy at the 2025 Munich Security Conference: "Having a unidimensional approach to building relationships is not as sensible as having multiple options."

India's **G20 presidency** (December 2022–November 2023) was the signature institutional achievement of this strategy. The New Delhi Leaders' Declaration was adopted unanimously — all 83 paragraphs on the first day — an achievement given the Ukraine polarization. Key outcomes included the admission of the **African Union as a permanent G20 member** (the first new member since the G20's formation), the announcement of the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC), the launch of the Global Biofuels Alliance, and a call for tripling global renewable energy capacity by 2030. India leveraged the presidency to embed itself as the bridge between Global North and Global South, a positioning reinforced by consecutive BRICS-member G20 presidencies (India 2023, Brazil 2024, South Africa 2025).

### The UNSC seat bid defines India's multilateral ambition

India's campaign for a **permanent UN Security Council seat** is supported by four of five current P5 members — the United States (reaffirmed at the September 2024 Quad Summit and under the Trump administration), the United Kingdom, France, and Russia. **China remains the primary obstacle**, conditioning any support on India dropping its backing for Japan's G4 bid. The "Uniting for Consensus" group (Italy, Canada, Pakistan, Turkey, South Korea) opposes expanding permanent membership. India has served 8 terms as a non-permanent member, most recently 2021–2022. A 2024 US proposal for two new permanent African seats signaled momentum for reform, but no formal Charter amendment — requiring two-thirds General Assembly approval and no P5 veto — has been initiated.

India's UNGA voting record reflects its multi-alignment calculus. India has **abstained on all 21 resolutions** across UN bodies concerning Russia's invasion of Ukraine, with the sole exception of a procedural vote allowing Zelenskyy's virtual participation. On Palestine, India endorsed the BRICS Kazan Declaration (October 2024) supporting Palestinian statehood within 1967 borders. India has been a UNHRC member for 16 of 18 years but has received over 200 communications from UN Special Procedures since 2011, responding to fewer than one-third, with 19 pending visit requests — some unanswered since 1999. In UN peacekeeping, India has deployed approximately **286,000 soldiers** since 1950, lost 177 peacekeepers (highest fatalities among member states), and currently ranks 4th globally with 5,394 personnel across 12 missions.

### Quad, BRICS, and SCO form a triangulated hedging architecture

The **Quad** (US, Japan, Australia, India) reached its highest institutionalization under Biden, with four in-person leaders' summits (2021–2024) and the US Quad Act (2024) mandating a long-term strategy. The September 2024 Wilmington Summit produced the MAITRI maritime initiative, the first Quad-at-Sea Ship Observer Mission, $140 million for Pacific undersea cables, and a Cancer Moonshot Initiative. India confirmed hosting the 2025 summit. However, the Lowy Institute assessed the Quad as "either dead or on life support" under Trump 2.0, with US tariff disputes and India's Russian oil purchases creating friction. Working-level cooperation continues — Quad foreign ministers met in July 2025 and jointly condemned the Pahalgam attack — but strategic-level commitment is uncertain. India emphasizes non-military cooperation and opposes framing the Quad as an anti-China alliance.

Within **BRICS** (expanded January 2024 to include Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, and UAE, now representing 45% of world population and 37.3% of global GDP), India plays a moderating role against the anti-Western posture pushed by Russia and China. At the October 2024 Kazan Summit, India and Brazil opposed explicit anti-Western framing. The summit facilitated the **first Modi-Xi bilateral in five years**, building on the October 2024 border disengagement agreement. India is working to block Pakistan's potential BRICS membership. In the **SCO**, India is the only member opposing China's Belt and Road Initiative. At the 2025 Tianjin Summit, Modi made his first visit to China in 7 years, agreeing on "Early Harvest" border delimitation proposals. India refused to endorse a joint statement at the June 2025 SCO defence ministers' meeting.

### Defense agreements and trade posture reflect strategic pragmatism

India's bilateral defense architecture with the United States has deepened through four foundational agreements: **LEMOA** (logistics support, 2016), **COMCASA** (secure communications, 2018), **BECA** (geospatial intelligence, 2020), and the **iCET** technology initiative (2023, rebranded as TRUST under Trump 2.0). India holds Major Defence Partner and Strategic Trade Authorization Tier-1 status. US-India defense trade grew from near zero in 2008 to over **$20 billion by 2020**. With France, the Rafale deal (36 jets, deliveries completed 2022) and Scorpène submarine program anchor the relationship. Japan is a G4 partner for UNSC reform and ACSA logistics agreement partner. Australia signed the ECTA trade agreement in April 2022 — India's first with a developed country in over a decade.

India's trade posture has evolved from the defensive protectionism that produced its November 2019 **RCEP withdrawal** (driven by fears of Chinese goods flooding domestic markets) toward active FTA diversification. The **India-UK FTA was signed May 6, 2025** after 14 rounds; the **India-EFTA TEPA** (March 2024) secured a $100 billion investment commitment; and negotiations with the EU, GCC, Israel, Chile, and the EAEU are advancing. India's unique nuclear status — outside the NPT but with a 2008 NSG waiver enabling civilian nuclear trade — persists, with China blocking NSG membership and India's nuclear liability legislation impeding foreign reactor projects.

India has never signed the Rome Statute and is not an ICC member, objecting to sovereignty concerns and prosecutorial powers. Its most significant ICJ engagement was the **Kulbhushan Jadhav case** — a 15-1 ruling in India's favor in July 2019 finding Pakistan violated the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations.

## SECTION 12: MILITARY & SECURITY SECTOR DNA

### Operation Sindoor revealed India's evolved strike capability and escalation management

The May 2025 India-Pakistan confrontation — the most significant between the nuclear-armed states since 1971 — provides the definitive recent test case for India's military-security architecture. Triggered by the April 22 Pahalgam terrorist attack (26 killed, including 25 Hindu tourists), **Operation Sindoor launched at 1:05 AM on May 7** with a 25-minute precision strike campaign against 9 targets in Pakistan and Pakistan-occupied Kashmir. Weapons employed included French **SCALP-EG cruise missiles, HAMMER guided munitions, BrahMos missiles**, and drones — striking JeM headquarters in Bahawalpur, LeT facilities in Muridke, and associated camps. India claimed at least 100 militants killed, including JeM chief Masood Azhar's family members and IC-814 hijacking operatives.

Pakistan retaliated with mortar attacks on Poonch and Jammu (killing 16 civilians in the worst shelling since 1971), convened its Nuclear Command Authority on May 9 in what analysts interpreted as "soft" nuclear signaling, and attacked Indian airbases at Adampur and Punjab on May 10. India's counter-retaliation struck **11 PAF airbases** including Jacobabad, Chaklala (near Strategic Plans Division headquarters in Rawalpindi), and Sargodha — the most extensive Indian air assault since 1971, with strikes penetrating up to 150 km inside Pakistan. India's indigenous **Akashteer air defense system** intercepted hundreds of Pakistani drones and missiles. Pakistan requested ceasefire via the DGMO hotline at 3:35 PM on May 10; hostilities halted at 5:00 PM. India termed it an "understanding" rather than a "ceasefire." India lost 3 combat aircraft. The operation demonstrated both improved strike precision and escalation management — though the nuclear dimension (Pakistan's NCA convening) introduced risks of a kind not seen since the 1999 Kargil conflict.

### Civilian supremacy is unbroken but the CDS institution remains immature

India's military-civilian relationship stands out as an unbroken tradition of **civilian control over 75+ years** — contrasting sharply with Pakistan, Myanmar, and Bangladesh. The military has remained apolitical with no coup or coup attempt in the country's history. This institutional culture, rooted in colonial-era British traditions of professional subordination, has held through multiple wars, internal crises, and political transitions. The President is Supreme Commander; executive authority rests with the PM and Cabinet.

The **Chief of Defence Staff** institution, created in December 2019 after recommendations from the Kargil Review Committee and Shekatkar Committee, remains structurally incomplete. Gen. Bipin Rawat, the first CDS, died in a helicopter crash on December 8, 2021. After a 9-month vacancy, Lt Gen. Anil Chauhan (retired) was appointed second CDS on September 30, 2022. Critically, the CDS has **no operational command** over the three service chiefs — ranking only as "primus inter pares." The CDS oversees the revenue budget but capital procurement remains with the Defence Secretary, creating an awkward bifurcation. A significant reform step came on June 24, 2025, when the CDS was empowered to issue binding joint orders to all three services. The theaterization debate — reorganizing 17 service-specific commands into Integrated Theatre Commands — remains in early stages. The Indian Air Force and Navy resist, fearing loss of autonomy. Only the Andaman and Nicobar Command (established 2001) exists as a tri-service theatre command. India lags the US (Goldwater-Nichols, 1986) and China (PLA reorganization, 2016) by decades.

### Modernization is diversifying away from Russia but dependency persists

India's defense procurement is undergoing a structural shift. Russia's share of Indian arms imports fell from **76% (2009–2013) to 36% (2019–2023)** according to SIPRI, with France rising to 33% and the US gaining significant market share. The Ukraine war accelerated diversification by delaying S-400 deliveries (3 of 5 regiments delivered by end-2023, deployed along the Ladakh and Punjab borders), disrupting spare parts supply for the massive Russian-origin fleet (259 Su-30MKIs, 2,400 T-72 tanks), and raising questions about Russian weapons' battlefield performance.

Indigenous defense production has shown genuine growth: **₹1,50,590 crore (~$18 billion) in FY 2024-25**, an 18% year-on-year increase and 90% growth since FY 2019-20. Defense exports reached an all-time high of ₹23,622 crore (~$2.8 billion), a roughly 30-fold increase from 2014, though India still lags South Korea ($9.5 billion) and Turkey ($7.1 billion). BrahMos — the primary strike weapon during Operation Sindoor — has secured export contracts with the Philippines ($375 million), with Vietnam discussions at ~$700 million. The **Agni-V MIRV test (Mission Divyastra, March 11, 2024)** was a milestone, making India the sixth country with MIRV capability — the missile's 5,000+ km range (reportedly 7,000+ km) covers the entire Chinese mainland. The Kaveri jet engine program, however, remains a "39-year failure" that constrains the AMCA fifth-generation fighter program.

Post-Operation Sindoor, the Defence Acquisition Council approved massive new procurement including **114 additional Rafales, 288 S-400 missiles**, and other systems totaling approximately ₹3.60 lakh crore. The FY 2026-27 defense budget rose approximately **15% to ~2% of GDP** — a significant increase driven by Sindoor's lessons and the two-front threat calculus.

### The nuclear triad is maturing, with second-strike capability now credible

India's nuclear arsenal grew from an estimated **172 warheads** (January 2024) to **180 warheads** (January 2025, SIPRI), compared to Pakistan's 170 and China's 600. The Nuclear Command Authority, constituted January 4, 2003, maintains clear civilian control — the Political Council (chaired by the PM) holds sole nuclear strike authorization. The **No First Use** doctrine remains official policy, though Defence Minister Rajnath Singh's August 2019 statement that policy "might change depending on circumstances" introduced deliberate ambiguity.

The sea-based deterrent is strengthening. **INS Arighaat**, the second Arihant-class SSBN, was commissioned August 29, 2024, carrying K-4 SLBMs. **INS Aridhaman** (third of class), launched November 2021, is expected for induction in 2025–26. Project 75-Alpha envisions 6 indigenous nuclear SSNs, with the first in service by 2032. The successful November 2024 test of a **long-range hypersonic missile (Mach 6, 1,500+ km range)** added another dimension. SIPRI's 2025 assessment notes that canisterization of land-based missiles and continuous sea patrols may indicate a shift from India's historical de-mated peacetime posture toward partial operational readiness.

### Paramilitary forces constitute a parallel army with political functions

India's **Central Armed Police Forces total approximately 1 million personnel** — approaching the active military strength of ~1.46 million. The CRPF (~310,000) handles internal security from Kashmir to Naxal zones. The BSF (~257,000) guards the Pakistan and Bangladesh borders, with a controversial 2021 expansion of jurisdiction from 15 km to 50 km inside border states. The CISF (~148,000–200,000) protects critical infrastructure including 59 airports and, since May 2024, Parliament itself (with 3,300+ personnel). The ITBP (~75,000) guards the China border. Total security deployment in Kashmir — military plus paramilitary — is estimated between 352,000 and 700,000 for a civilian population of approximately 8 million, making it one of the most militarized regions on earth. AFSPA remains in force in J&K and parts of the northeast, granting powers including lethal force and warrantless arrest with immunity from prosecution absent central government sanction — which is rarely granted. The October 2024 J&K elections (the first since 2014, with 64% turnout) produced a JKNC-Congress coalition government, but any prospect of AFSPA withdrawal was likely postponed by the Pahalgam attack and Operation Sindoor.

### The defense budget confronts a structural affordability gap

India's FY 2025-26 defense budget stands at **₹6,81,210 crore (~$78–81 billion)**, making it the world's third-largest behind the United States and China. However, the headline figure obscures critical structural weaknesses. Pensions alone consume **₹1,60,795 crore (23.6% of the total)** for 3.4 million defence pensioners. Revenue expenditure (salaries, maintenance, operations) consistently dominates capital spending (modernization). DRDO receives only **3.94% of the total defense budget** — widely considered insufficient for India's indigenous development ambitions. Stripping pensions, the effective defense allocation drops to approximately 1.4% of GDP. The Standing Committee on Defence and independent analysts consistently argue that capital allocation is inadequate for the two-front threat scenario (China plus Pakistan) that defines India's strategic planning environment. The post-Sindoor FY 2026-27 budget, with its ~15% increase and approach toward 2% of GDP, may signal a structural shift — but sustaining elevated defense spending alongside India's development needs and fiscal constraints will test the government's allocation priorities.

## Conclusion

These five sections reveal a recurring structural pattern in India's national architecture: **ambitious formal frameworks layered over deep implementation gaps and democratic governance deficits**. India achieves its renewable energy NDC targets five years early while planning 100 GW of new coal capacity. It builds credible nuclear second-strike capability while the CDS institution lacks operational command authority. It hosts a triumphant G20 presidency while its press freedom ranking sits at 151st. It deploys sophisticated precision-strike operations in Sindoor while 46% of its parliamentarians face criminal charges.

Three structural dynamics deserve particular intelligence attention through 2026–27. First, the **transboundary water crisis** is escalating simultaneously on three fronts, with the Indus Waters Treaty abeyance, China's Motuo dam, and the 1996 Ganges Treaty expiration creating compound risks without historical precedent. Second, **media-industrial consolidation** has reached a tipping point where two conglomerates (Reliance and Adani) with deep government dependencies control the information diet of hundreds of millions — a structural vulnerability for democratic accountability regardless of partisan orientation. Third, **Operation Sindoor's aftermath** is reshaping defense procurement, budget trajectories, and regional escalation dynamics in ways that will define India-Pakistan relations and great power calculations for years. India's capacity to manage these converging pressures — environmental, informational, and security — will determine whether its "middle power" trajectory bends toward resilience or fragility.
