## SECTION 8: ENVIRONMENTAL & CLIMATIC STRUCTURE

### Climate disasters as recurring tests of Indonesian state legitimacy

Indonesia ranks sixth globally in susceptibility to high-impact natural hazards. Located on the Pacific Ring of Fire with 100+ active volcanoes, exposed to El Niño/La Niña oscillation, Indian Ocean Dipole shifts, and equatorial cyclogenesis, the archipelago's environmental structure imposes a relentless cadence of governance stress-tests. **BNPB recorded 2,107 disaster incidents in 2024 alone**, claiming 547 lives and displacing 6.3 million people. The pendulum swings between drought-fire years (El Niño: 2015, 2019, 2023) and catastrophic flooding (La Niña: 2024–2025), each cycle exposing institutional capacity and political will.

Three episodes illustrate the climate-governance nexus most clearly. The **2004 Aceh tsunami** (220,000 killed) forced open a previously sealed conflict zone to international humanitarian access, catalyzing the August 2005 Helsinki Peace Agreement that ended a 30-year insurgency — and drove creation of BNPB in 2007. The **2015 peatland fire crisis** burned 2.6 million hectares, caused an estimated $16 billion in damages, triggered transboundary haze emergencies across Southeast Asia, and exposed governance failure severe enough to establish the Peatland Restoration Agency (BRG). Most recently, **Tropical Cyclone Senyar in November 2025** — an extremely rare equatorial cyclone — devastated Aceh, North Sumatra, and West Sumatra: 961 killed, 234 missing, 156,000+ homes damaged, approximately one million displaced. The Prabowo government's refusal to declare a national disaster and reluctance to accept bilateral or ASEAN AHA Centre assistance became a major political controversy. Amnesty International Indonesia called it "a human rights catastrophe reflecting a deeper failure of governance and legitimacy." Scientists directly linked severity to deforestation, industrial expansion, and weak spatial planning. As of February 2026, 23 of 52 affected districts remained in emergency response, many lacking clean water, electricity, and medical supplies.

Jakarta's compound crisis anchors the vulnerability story. North Jakarta subsides at **7–25 cm per year**; roughly 40% of the city now sits below sea level. Projections suggest a third of Jakarta could be underwater by 2050. This drove President Jokowi's 2019 decision to relocate the capital to **Nusantara** in East Kalimantan — officially inaugurated August 17, 2024, with a projected cost of $30–35 billion. Yet construction has destroyed 14,000+ hectares of forest, investor participation has disappointed, and the 28 million Jakartans left behind face an uncertain future of escalating flood risk with no comprehensive mitigation plan. Semarang and Surabaya face analogous coastal subsidence trajectories at smaller scales.

### Transboundary haze diplomacy remains structurally unresolved

The haze-palm oil nexus is Indonesia's most persistent transboundary environmental friction. The 2002 ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution (AATHP) — the bloc's first binding environmental agreement — was ratified by Indonesia only in January 2015, twelve years after other members. The Lowy Institute characterizes it as "toothless" given ASEAN's non-interference principle. Singapore enacted its own Transboundary Haze Pollution Act in 2014 as a unilateral response. The 2023 El Niño-driven haze spike (API readings exceeding 300 in South Sumatra) renewed bilateral tension, with Malaysia's Environment Minister formally urging Indonesian action. Malaysian and Singaporean companies own 18–30% of Indonesian palm oil operations, making the issue transboundary in causation as well as effect.

The palm oil sector generates **4.5% of Indonesia's GDP**, creating powerful vested interests resistant to environmental regulation. The Second ASEAN Haze-Free Roadmap (2023–2030) focuses on peatland management but inadequately addresses illegal deforestation and weak enforcement — a structural constraint that no amount of summit diplomacy has altered.

### Indonesia's emissions profile reveals deep contradictions

Indonesia is the **world's largest thermal coal exporter by tonnage and sixth-largest CO₂ emitter**. Coal provides approximately 60% of electricity generation; solar PV and wind contribute less than 1%. Its Enhanced NDC (2022) committed to 29% unconditional and 41% conditional emissions reductions below business-as-usual by 2030. The Second NDC (submitted October 2025) shifts to absolute targets, with peak emissions projected at 1,345,707–1,491,474 Gg CO₂e by 2030 — requiring an estimated **$472.6 billion in investment**. Climate Action Tracker rates Indonesia's trajectory "Critically Insufficient."

The **Just Energy Transition Partnership (JETP)**, a $20 billion commitment announced at the 2022 G20 Bali summit, has become a case study in implementation failure. The US withdrew from all JETPs in early 2025; Germany assumed co-lead. Other partners raised pledges to $21.4 billion, but of this, only **$3.1 billion (14.5%) has been approved**, and only 1.5% of public funding was in grants rather than loans. The JETP Progress Report 2025 shows zero reduction in grid-connected coal power capacity through 2035. Captive coal power nearly quadrupled from 5.5 GW (2019) to nearly 20 GW (2025). The Cirebon-1 early retirement plan — the first major Energy Transition Mechanism project — was cancelled in late 2025. Indonesia's RUPTL 2025–2034 delays over 70% of planned 42.6 GW renewable capacity to post-2030 while frontloading 12.7 GW of new fossil capacity by 2029.

The carbon tax, legislated in 2021 at a minimum rate of IDR 30,000/tCO₂e (~$2/tCO₂e), has been **repeatedly delayed since its original April 2022 start date and remains unimplemented as of early 2026**. The IDX Carbon exchange, launched in September 2023, achieved only IDR 78 billion (~$4.9 million) in total transactions over its first two years, with just 8 projects listed.

### Institutional capacity is eroding under Prabowo

The **Peatland and Mangrove Restoration Agency (BRGM) was dissolved in December 2024** when the Prabowo administration chose not to renew its mandate. Over 2016–2024, the agency restored 1.6 million hectares of peatland and 84,396 hectares of mangroves, falling short of targets (2.6 million and 600,000 hectares respectively). Functions were transferred to a directorate within the Ministry of Forestry. A World Bank mangrove project was rated "Moderately Unsatisfactory" as of June 2025, with only 10.97% disbursement. Political appointees with no environmental expertise were placed in FOLU program management positions in March 2025, prompting alarm about institutional continuity.

Indonesia holds the world's largest tropical peatland (~36% of global total), storing an estimated **46–57 billion tons of carbon** and contributing up to 74% of Indonesia's total natural climate solution mitigation potential. The dissolution of the dedicated agency responsible for managing this asset represents a significant strategic risk.

### Deforestation is declining but fragile, with new drivers emerging

Indonesian government data shows net deforestation fell from **1.9 million ha/year (1990–1996) to ~133,000 ha (2022–2023)** — a 90% reduction confirmed by FAO review. Global Forest Watch data shows a 64% drop in primary forest loss between 2015–2017 and 2020–2022. However, 2023 saw a 27% uptick from the prior year (El Niño-driven), and a critical structural shift has occurred: **97% of 2024 deforestation occurred within legal concessions**, reflecting a move from illegal to quasi-legal destruction. Nickel mining is now a major emerging driver, particularly in Sulawesi, Maluku, and Kalimantan; a 2022 study found Indonesia accounted for 58% of mining-driven forest loss across 26 tropical countries. Law No. 2 of 2025 (Fourth Amendment to the Mining Law, enacted March 2025) expanded permit access and offered mining licenses to religious organizations, while the 2020 Omnibus Law had already weakened environmental impact assessment requirements.

## SECTION 9: ILLICIT NETWORKS & SHADOW GOVERNANCE

### A shadow economy worth nearly a quarter of GDP

Indonesia's illicit and informal economy operates at scale. The **Global Shadow Economy EY Report 2025 valued it at $326 billion, equivalent to 23.8% of GDP**. Indonesia's Financial Intelligence Unit (PPATK) estimates a wider 30–40% range. Informal employment encompasses 60–70% of the workforce, with 99% of all businesses classified as informal MSMEs — 66 million units employing approximately 117 million workers. The tax-to-GDP ratio remains chronically low at roughly 10–11%, reflecting the state's limited reach into this parallel economy.

The principal illicit sectors operate with distinct geographic and commodity signatures. **Illegal mining (PETI)** operates at approximately 2,741 identified locations (Ministry of ESDM, December 2023), with President Prabowo citing estimated state losses of **Rp 300 trillion (~$19 billion) annually** in his August 2025 State of the Nation address. Gold operations concentrate in Central Kalimantan, Riau, West Sumatra, and Papua; tin in Bangka Belitung (where refined production fell 30.7% in 2024 to its lowest in 20+ years following crackdowns); coal in South and East Kalimantan; and nickel expanding into Sulawesi and Raja Ampat. The **2.1-ton crystal methamphetamine seizure** near Batam in May 2025 — the largest in Indonesian history, valued at $300+ million and linked to Golden Triangle syndicates — punctuated the scale of drug trafficking through the archipelago. UNODC reported 236 tons of methamphetamine seized across East and Southeast Asia in 2024, a 24% increase. **Illegal fishing** costs Indonesia an estimated $1.85–4 billion annually, with the Arafura Sea, North Natuna Sea, and Malacca Strait as primary hotspots.

### State-criminal integration is structural, not aberrant

The most analytically significant feature of Indonesia's illicit economy is its entanglement with security institutions. WikiLeaks cables from 2006–2007 documented Indonesian officials telling US diplomats that the TNI had "far more troops in Papua than it is willing to admit, chiefly to protect and facilitate TNI's interests in illegal logging operations" and operated "as a virtually autonomous governmental entity." Freeport's Senior Vice President admitted that "main allegations about direct payments by the company to the military are true." In September 2025, Papuan lawmaker Yan Mandenas publicly accused rogue TNI and police members of protecting unlicensed gold and nickel mining in Yahukimo, Nabire, and Raja Ampat.

Police involvement is equally systematic. Former PT Timah executives testified that illegal tin mining in Bangka Belitung was "unstoppable because it enjoyed police protection." In Central Kalimantan, police "crackdowns" function as theater — word leaks from district level, miners hide equipment temporarily, and payments of Rp 500,000–1 million settle unannounced patrols. As PWYP Indonesia concluded in 2025: **"Institutions tasked with upholding the law, including the police, military and regulatory bodies, are themselves implicated in shielding, facilitating and profiting from illicit mining."**

Indonesia's official defense budget covers only one-third to one-half of actual military spending, with contributions from private companies, military-run businesses, and illicit economic activities filling the gap — a structural dependency that incentivizes ongoing security-sector involvement in extractive economies.

### The KPK has been systematically neutralized

The Corruption Eradication Commission (KPK), once Indonesia's most effective anti-corruption institution, has been progressively defanged since 2019. Law 19/2019, passed in just 12 days, created a Supervisory Council controlling operations, reclassified employees as civil servants, and required board approval for surveillance. The TWK (National Insight Test) was used to purge personnel. Between January and November 2020, 38 personnel left KPK due to disappointment with organizational changes. Public trust fell from 83.1% (2017) to 72.7% (2022). Indonesia's Corruption Perceptions Index score stands at **37 (ranked 99th of 180 countries)** on TI's 2024 index.

The **Hasto Kristiyanto case** crystallizes how anti-corruption has become a political weapon. PDI-P Secretary General Hasto — the most prominent critic of Jokowi and the sole effective opposition figure — was named as a suspect in December 2024, detained in February 2025, and sentenced in July 2025 to three years and six months for bribing a former election commissioner with Rp 600 million. Hasto publicly alleged: "This is just a political issue because of my criticism of Jokowi." KPK leadership was handpicked by Jokowi. The subsequent July 2025 Prabowo clemency — extending to Hasto, Thomas Lembong, and 1,000+ other convicts — indicated that the case served its political purpose and was resolved through dealmaking. This pattern of selective prosecution recurs across administrations: under SBY, the KPK-police "gecko vs. crocodile" conflict; under Jokowi, the systematic legislative weakening; under Prabowo, clemency for graft convicts who return stolen assets.

### Elite financial exposure is significant but strategically buffered

Indonesian oligarch wealth is heavily offshored. A **Dartmouth study (2025) found 70–90% of offshore assets** by Indonesian elites are in "blacklisted jurisdictions" — among the highest globally. An estimated $93 billion was held in Singapore by 2010 (Capgemini/Merrill Lynch data). ICIJ investigations found nine of Indonesia's 11 richest families held 190+ offshore trusts and companies. Global Financial Integrity estimated Indonesia lost $10 billion annually in illicit financial outflows between 2001 and 2010.

Bloomberg reported in April 2025 that wealthy Indonesians were shifting "hundreds of millions of dollars offshore" amid concerns over Prabowo's fiscal discipline, with USDT stablecoins gaining favor for moving $100,000+ undetected and the Middle East emerging as an alternative to Singapore's tighter post-scandal due diligence. Heavy reliance on Singapore's financial infrastructure creates theoretical vulnerability to Western sanctions architecture, though direct targeting remains unlikely absent a major geopolitical rupture.

## SECTION 10: INFORMATION ECOSYSTEM & MEDIA STRUCTURE

### Ten conglomerates control Indonesian media, most with direct political party ties

Indonesia's mainstream media landscape is defined by the **intersection of ownership, party affiliation, and oligarchic interest**. Three of the major conglomerate owners are active political party leaders: **Hary Tanoesoedibjo** (MNC Group — RCTI, MNC TV, GTV, iNews; chairs Perindo Party); **Surya Paloh** (Media Group — Metro TV, Media Indonesia; chairs NasDem Party); and **Aburizal Bakrie** (Viva Group — tvOne, ANTV; former Golkar chairman). **Chairul Tanjung** (Trans Media — Trans TV, Trans7, CNN Indonesia, Detik.com) has no formal party role but actively campaigned for both Jokowi and Prabowo. **Kompas Gramedia Group** retains the most editorial independence among major conglomerates but faces increasing economic pressure and self-censorship. Only **Tempo** (uniquely employee-owned), **Tirto.id**, and **Narasi** (founded by journalist Najwa Shihab) operate as genuinely independent outlets. All major conglomerates have been part of governing coalitions, creating a system where media functions as a bargaining chip for political access rather than a public accountability mechanism.

RSF's assessment is stark: "Around ten large media groups share most of the mainstream media market... the media oligarchy linked to political interests has grown stronger." The Press Council counted 5,019 active media companies in 2024, with 77.4% being online outlets — but the concentration of broadcast media (which reaches the rural majority) in oligarch hands is the structural constraint that matters.

### Press freedom is declining sharply under Prabowo

Indonesia **dropped 16 places on the RSF World Press Freedom Index in 2025, falling from 111th to 127th** — its lowest ranking since 2016. The classification shifted from "Problematic" to "Difficult." The steepest deterioration was in the security indicator (down 38 positions to 125th) and economic indicator (142nd). RSF noted that journalists reporting on corruption and protests "often faced multiple forms of intimidation by police and military personnel." Freedom House scored Indonesia 49/100 (Partly Free) on its Freedom on the Net 2024 index, down from 56/100 in 2016.

Specific incidents illuminate the trajectory. In March 2024, three navy officers abducted journalist **Sukandi Ali**, interrogated and physically assaulted him for reporting on a navy barge seizure, and forced him to sign papers ceasing journalism practice. During the August 2025 protests, music creators behind a protest anthem were coerced by police into revealing their identities and pulling the song from Spotify — Amnesty International called for investigation.

### The digital repression toolkit has become sophisticated

Indonesia's 212 million internet users (74.6% penetration) and 143 million social media accounts make the digital space the primary arena for political contestation. WhatsApp leads with approximately 187 million monthly active users; Facebook reaches 208 million; Instagram 103–119 million; TikTok 100+ million.

The **buzzer (buzzer politik) phenomenon** is a defining feature of Indonesia's information ecosystem. Oxford University identified Indonesia as one of 70 countries using "cyber troops." Buzzer networks, which emerged in the 2012 Jakarta gubernatorial election and have since professionalized, operate year-round — shaping narratives through anonymous accounts, hashtag spamming, mass retweeting, doxxing, and character assassination. During the 2024 election, buzzers helped sanitize Prabowo's image from accused human rights violator to "gemoy" (cute) figure. In January 2020, Reuters discovered the military was funding a network of 10 news sites publishing pro-government propaganda. The Lowy Institute documented Chinese Spamouflage networks amplifying anti-Rafale narratives in Indonesian social media using the hashtag #HentikanRafaleDeal — an instance of foreign actors exploiting Indonesia's buzzer infrastructure.

The legal architecture for speech control rests on the **Electronic Information and Transactions Law (UU ITE)**, under which Amnesty International documented at least 530 cases of criminalization affecting 563 victims between 2019 and 2024, with 146 cases in 2024 alone. The **new Criminal Code (KUHP), effective January 2, 2026**, adds provisions criminalizing insults to the president (up to 3 years), blasphemy (up to 5 years), and spreading communist ideology (4–10 years). Amnesty International's Executive Director called it "a significant blow to civil liberties."

### August 2025 demonstrated a new model of digital-age repression

The August 2025 protests — triggered by announcement that each of 580 DPR members would receive Rp 50 million monthly housing allowances (~10x Jakarta minimum wage) — produced the most comprehensive digital repression operation to date. At least **10 civilians were killed**, hundreds injured, and 1,200+ arrested in Jakarta alone. SAFEnet documented the full toolkit: ITE Law criminal charges against student activists for posting satirical memes; doxxing of civil society leaders; TikTok Live suspended by ByteDance under government pressure; Meta removing video of the Police Chief ordering subordinates to shoot trespassers; multiple pro-democracy Instagram accounts suspended; police confiscation and forced data extraction from protesters' phones; and the Broadcasting Commission instructing outlets not to air scenes of brutality.

As analysis in Magdalene.co concluded: **"The most striking innovation was not state censorship, but how the government persuaded platforms to do its bidding... Jakarta outsourced repression to platforms, maintaining plausible deniability."** This represents a model adaptable to future crises — and one that operates with minimal great-power pushback in an era of American retrenchment from democracy promotion. Indonesia has six IFCN-certified fact-checking organizations (including Mafindo, Tirto.id, Tempo.co, Kompas.com), but they operate against a structural tide where 68% of Indonesians get news primarily from social media and only 39% trust news media overall.

## SECTION 11: INTERNATIONAL INSTITUTIONAL COMMITMENTS & LEGAL FRAMEWORKS

### Indonesia is pursuing multi-alignment, not non-alignment

The central finding across Indonesia's international institutional engagements is a shift from traditional "free and active" non-alignment toward what the Lowy Institute terms **"multi-alignment"** — building partnerships issue by issue, shifting with material interest. Under Prabowo, Indonesia simultaneously joined BRICS (January 2025), concluded the EU-Indonesia CEPA (September 2025), signed a new security treaty with Australia (November 2025), maintained US defense cooperation (Super Garuda Shield 2025 was the largest ever iteration), deepened French defense ties ($8+ billion Rafale program), and attended Beijing's September 2025 military parade alongside Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin before appearing at UNGA and a Gaza peace summit. This is additive hedging, not pivot — but it operates on the assumption that American attention to Southeast Asia is declining.

**BRICS accession on January 6, 2025** made Indonesia the first Southeast Asian full member. The Jokowi government had hesitated after the 2023 Johannesburg Summit endorsement, fearing damage to non-aligned credibility. Prabowo moved swiftly: FM Sugiono attended the October 2024 Kazan Summit and secured consensus within three months. Indonesia frames membership through its "free and active" doctrine and simultaneously pursues OECD membership as a counterbalance. BRICS provides access to the New Development Bank (NDB), alternative economic frameworks, and rhetorical alignment with Global South priorities. In March 2025, Prabowo announced plans to join the NDB, though required share purchases create fiscal pressure. Critically, Indonesia is part of BRICS's push for local currency trade settlements — which could trigger friction with the Trump administration.

### ASEAN centrality is eroding as Prabowo looks beyond the region

Multiple authoritative sources document a significant downgrading of ASEAN in Indonesia's foreign policy hierarchy. The Lowy Institute assessed in 2025: "ASEAN, once Indonesia's anchor, now serves mainly as a ceremony. Prabowo's government participates but rarely leads." ISEAS noted that "ASEAN is now overlooked as a cornerstone" of foreign policy. Foreign Minister Sugiono skipped an ASEAN Foreign Ministers meeting in Thailand (December 2024) focused on Myanmar. Prabowo explicitly argued: "If we want to be truly non-bloc, if we want to befriend everyone, that means we are on our own. If we are threatened or attacked, nobody is going to help us" — a remarkably transactional formulation that departs from decades of ASEAN institutionalism.

Indonesia's UN engagement remains substantial. It was elected **President of the UN Human Rights Council for 2026** and ranks **5th globally in peacekeeping contributions** with 2,752 uniformed personnel (January 2025), deployed primarily to UNIFIL in Lebanon. Indonesia has served four UNSC non-permanent terms, most recently in 2019–2020.

### Trade agreements reflect institutional hedging

The **Indonesia-EU CEPA**, concluded September 23, 2025 after nearly a decade of negotiations, eliminates tariffs on 98.5% of tariff lines. Bilateral trade stood at €27.3 billion in 2024. The deal includes a Protocol on Palm Oil — the most contentious issue — and an Investment Protection Agreement. Ratification targets January 1, 2027. In parallel, Indonesia won a WTO panel ruling against EU palm oil biofuel restrictions (DS593, January 2025) and requested authorization to suspend EU trade concessions. US-Indonesia bilateral goods trade reached $45.8 billion in 2025, and in February 2026 the two countries signed an Agreement on Reciprocal Trade cutting US tariffs on Indonesian goods to 19%.

Indonesia participates in RCEP and IPEF, though the latter's future under the second Trump administration is uncertain — making BRICS membership partly a hedge against IPEF's potential discontinuation.

### Climate commitments are conditional and increasingly fragile

Prabowo's brother and special envoy **Hashim Djojohadikusumo** questioned Indonesia's continued Paris Agreement participation in early 2025: "If the United States does not want to comply... why should a country like Indonesia comply with it?" Energy Minister Bahlil Lahadalia described staying in Paris as "a dilemma." Indonesia ultimately submitted its 2035 NDC in October 2025, but Hashim called the JETP a "failed program," noting: "The promised $5 billion out of $20 billion has not materialized." As of December 2024, donors had disbursed only $230 million in grants for 44 projects. Germany signed a €200 million policy-based loan in June 2025, and the JETP CIPP is being revised with coal phase-out deprioritized from second to fifth priority. American withdrawal from the JETP directly weakened the architecture — a concrete instance of the retrenchment dynamic at work.

Indonesia has not ratified the Rome Statute and is unlikely to do so. Military resistance is a primary factor; international law expert Hikmahanto Juwana testified to the Constitutional Court in 2023 that there is hesitancy "if anyone who is deemed a hero to the nation is declared a loser someplace else."

### Defection costs are high but declining as alternatives mature

Indonesia's deep integration into Western trade ($45.8 billion US bilateral goods trade; €27.3 billion EU bilateral trade), financial systems (regular IMF Article IV consultations; World Bank/ADB as primary development finance sources), and defense ecosystems (F-15EX, Super Garuda Shield, Australia security treaty) makes a sharp pivot costly. However, the growing availability of BRICS/NDB/AIIB alternatives, China's position as Indonesia's top trading partner, and deliberate defense diversification across French, Turkish, South Korean, and potentially Chinese platforms provide genuine optionality. The BRICS Contingent Reserve Arrangement ironically reinforces IMF dependency (requiring IMF programs for access beyond 30% of quotas), but NDB lending and AIIB membership offer supplementary financing channels. Indonesia's critical minerals (nickel, cobalt, bauxite), demographic weight (280+ million), and strategic geography at the intersection of Indian and Pacific oceans mean that Western partners also face significant costs from losing Indonesian cooperation — giving Jakarta leverage in both directions.

## SECTION 12: MILITARY & SECURITY SECTOR DNA

### Prabowo is executing the most significant military restructuring since Suharto

The 2025 TNI Law (Law No. 3 of 2025), passed unanimously by the DPR on March 20, 2025, expanded the civilian institutions where active-duty military can serve from 10 to 14, adding the Attorney General's Office, BNPB (Disaster Mitigation), BNPT (Counterterrorism), Bakamla (Maritime Security), and BNPP (Border Management). Article 7 expanded non-war military operations to include cyber threat defense and protection of citizens abroad. Retirement ages were raised to 58 for enlisted personnel, 60 for generals, and 63 for four-star generals with presidential extension. The law was rushed through in less than two months, with deliberations held in a luxury hotel rather than the parliamentary complex. **186 civil society organizations** launched a petition opposing it, mass student protests erupted, and multiple constitutional challenges were filed.

But Imparsial, a Jakarta-based watchdog, had already documented that **at least 2,569 active-duty officers were serving in civilian roles** before the amendments — making the law less an expansion than a legalization of existing practice. As scholars at IACL-AIDC noted: "It merely strengthens the legal foundation for these practices."

In August 2025, Prabowo inaugurated **six new Army Kodams** (bringing the total to 21), **14 naval base commands, three Air Force commands, six Kopassus groups, 20 territorial brigades, 100 territorial development battalions**, five Marine infantry battalions, and five Air Force commando battalions — the largest military restructuring since the Suharto era. The new territorial development battalions contain agriculture, health, and construction companies, explicitly tasked with economic development alongside combat roles. The stated goal is one territorial battalion per regency/city and one Kodam per province. The army chief of staff openly stated the new Kodams were intended to **"balance" the police**, which already has provincial-level command structures.

### The Kopassus network runs the government

New Mandala analysis found **9 of 16 ex-army appointees in Prabowo's cabinet have Kopassus backgrounds**. Defense Minister Sjafrie Sjamsoeddin — Prabowo's military academy classmate, who commanded Kodam Jaya during the 1998 crisis and served as Suharto's military adjutant — also serves as ad interim Coordinating Minister for Political and Security Affairs. Foreign Minister Sugiono is a former Kopassus first lieutenant. BIN (National Intelligence) chief Muhammad Herindra is ex-Kopassus. Most remarkably, Cabinet Secretary Teddy Indra Wijaya is an **active-duty Kopassus officer** who served as Prabowo's personal assistant. Kopassus commanders have been elevated from two-star to three-star general positions. The cabinet held a military-style retreat at the Military Academy in Magelang with ministers in camouflage fatigues, 4 AM wake-ups, and marching exercises.

### Force structure and defense spending remain mismatched with ambitions

Indonesia fields approximately **400,000 active-duty personnel** (300,400 Army, remainder Navy and Air Force), 400,000 reserves, and 250,000 paramilitary — ranking 13th globally on the Global Firepower index. The Navy operates three fleet commands with Martadinata-class frigates, PKR SIGMA frigates, approximately four submarines (Type 209-class), and 326 total vessels. The Air Force currently flies F-16C/D and aging Su-27/Su-30 variants, with the first three Rafale B jets arriving at Roesmin Nurjadin Air Base on January 23, 2026.

Defense spending reached **Rp 335.3 trillion (~$23.4 billion) for 2026** — a 37% increase — though ASPI assessed the MoD-specific allocation of Rp 187.1T as "more of a political-fiscal compromise than a strategic breakthrough." At approximately **0.8% of GDP**, Indonesia's defense spending remains among the lowest in Southeast Asia, compared to Singapore's 3.0% and Vietnam's 2.3%. Prabowo's campaign promise to reach 1.5% of GDP by 2029 would require roughly doubling expenditure. Only about 25% of the current budget goes to capital expenditure; the rest is consumed by operational costs.

### Equipment diversification is the signature procurement strategy

Indonesia is deliberately acquiring military platforms from **France, South Korea, Turkey, the United States, China, Italy, and Japan** — a multi-source strategy designed to avoid dependence on any single supplier. The flagship acquisition is the **42 Dassault Rafale fighter jets (~$8.1 billion)**, with potential for 8–24 more under a May 2025 Letter of Intent. During President Macron's state visit, over 20 agreements worth $11+ billion were signed, including **two Scorpène Evolved submarines (~$2 billion)** to be built at PT PAL in Surabaya and CAESAR self-propelled howitzers. Indonesia contracted **48 Turkish KAAN fifth-generation fighters** in July 2025. The **KF-21 Boramae** program with South Korea was restructured, with Indonesia's cost share reduced from ~$1.15 billion to $438 million, retaining rights to 48 aircraft. An F-15EX MoU with Boeing covers up to 24 jets. Chinese J-10C fighters have also been confirmed.

Asian Military Review warns this "bewildering fighter smorgasbord" creates severe interoperability, logistics, and training burdens. **85% of defense procurement is funded through overseas loans**, further constraining flexibility.

### TNI-POLRI rivalry is intensifying under Prabowo

SETARA Institute documented at least **37 instances of military-police conflict between 2014 and 2024**. Under Jokowi, police budgets soared from Rp 44 trillion (2014) to Rp 126 trillion (2024) as he used police as a power base. Under Prabowo, power has shifted decisively toward the military. In March 2025, three police officers were shot dead by two soldiers during a cockfighting raid in Lampung. In February 2025, dozens of army troops stormed Tarakan Police HQ in North Kalimantan after a nightclub brawl escalated. During the August-September 2025 protests, TNI intelligence (Bais) personnel were spotted at protest sites, and allegations emerged that TNI provoked violence to discredit police. The TNI remains **Indonesia's most trusted institution at 95.8%** (Indikator Politik Indonesia survey, May 2025), compared to POLRI at 72.2% — a gap Prabowo is exploiting institutionally.

### Doctrinal orientation is shifting toward maritime deterrence and cyber

The traditional Sishankamrata (Total People's Defense) doctrine, emphasizing comprehensive mobilization of national resources across Main (TNI), Reserve, and Supporting components, is being supplemented by a new maritime and A2/AD focus. The **Perisai Trisula Nusantara (Nusantara Trident Shield)** concept replaces the Minimum Essential Force framework, emphasizing integrated deterrence across land, naval, air, space, and cyber domains. The Air Force's IAADS ("Cakra") blueprint covers posture through 2044. The 2025 TNI Law explicitly adds cyber threat defense as a military operation, with a planned dedicated cyber force. Relocation of the capital to Nusantara in East Kalimantan positions strategic assets near ALKI II (the vital sea lane of communication) and maritime flashpoints in the North Natuna Sea.

### Defense partnerships span all major power centers

**Super Garuda Shield 2025** (August 25–September 4, 2025), the largest iteration yet, involved 6,500 troops from 13 nations including HIMARS live fire, amphibious operations, cyber defense, and airborne operations. Indonesia conducted **115 bilateral/multilateral exercises in 2023–24** — more than any other Southeast Asian nation. The **Australia-Indonesia Common Security Treaty**, announced November 2025 during Prabowo's first state visit to Australia, commits both governments to leader- and ministerial-level consultations when security is threatened — stopping short of automatic mutual defense but representing potentially the most significant security partnership under Prime Minister Albanese. A December 2025 Australia-PNG-Indonesia trilateral defense ministers' meeting agreed to meet annually.

Simultaneously, Indonesia resumed joint military exercises with China after a decade-long hiatus (August 2024). Prabowo visited Moscow in June 2025 and signed a "declaration on strategic partnership" with Putin. Reports of potential revival of the stalled Su-35 deal circulate despite sanctions risks. The pattern is unmistakable: Indonesia is diversifying its defense relationships across every available power center, treating military partnerships as a portfolio to be optimized rather than an alliance to be maintained — a strategy that American retrenchment both enables and incentivizes.

---

## Conclusion: five structural dynamics shaping Indonesia's trajectory

First, **environmental governance is deteriorating precisely as climate risks accelerate**. The dissolution of BRGM, stalled JETP, unimplemented carbon tax, and expanding coal capacity create a widening gap between international commitments and domestic policy that American withdrawal from climate leadership has made politically easier to sustain.

Second, **illicit networks are embedded in the security architecture**, not peripheral to it. The TNI's structural funding gap, police protection rackets, and systematic neutralization of the KPK ensure that state-criminal integration will persist as a governance feature rather than a bug — particularly as territorial development battalions create new vectors for military economic involvement.

Third, **the information ecosystem has evolved from oligarchic media capture to sophisticated digital repression**. The August 2025 prototype — outsourcing censorship to platforms while maintaining plausible deniability — represents a scalable model for controlling dissent without the crude internet shutdowns that draw international attention.

Fourth, **multi-alignment is Indonesia's institutional hedging strategy**, adding BRICS to existing Western frameworks rather than substituting one for the other. The defection costs remain high in both directions, giving Jakarta genuine leverage — but the balance is shifting as alternative institutions mature and American institutional commitment fades.

Fifth, **the re-militarization under Prabowo is the deepest structural shift**, with the 2025 TNI Law, massive force restructuring, Kopassus network in government, and expanded civilian mandates representing the most significant rollback of post-1998 democratic reforms. This is occurring with minimal international pushback — arguably the clearest single indicator of what American retrenchment means in practice for Indonesian democracy.
