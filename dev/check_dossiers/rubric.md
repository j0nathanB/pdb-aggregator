# Dossier Verification Rubric

Extracted from `pass_1.md`, `pass_2.md`, and `pass_3.md`. Each numbered item is a discrete, checkable ask.

## Cross-Cutting Asks (Apply to ALL Sections)

1. Every analytical claim is concrete and pattern-matchable (not hedged generalities)
2. Favor falsifiable assertions over vague statements
3. Define regional terms, acronyms, and institutional names in-line on first use
4. When referencing political figures, briefly identify their role and significance
5. Use confidence markers where evidence is thin or contested: [HIGH CONFIDENCE], [MODERATE CONFIDENCE], or [ASSESSED]
6. Write in analytical prose, not bullet points (except Sections 0, 18, 19, 20)
7. Prioritize explanatory depth and causal reasoning over encyclopedic coverage
8. Each section is produced as a separate, individually headed section (no combining/merging)
9. Each section concludes with a STRUCTURAL CLAIMS block of numbered, discrete, falsifiable assertions
10. Treat US retrenchment as the baseline operating environment (Structural Claim #0), not a temporary disruption
11. Address four analytical questions per country: Exposure, Adaptation, Constraint, Russia axis
12. Do not editorialize about whether behavior is good or bad; explain the logic from the country's structural position
13. When the historical record is contested or inference uncertain, say so explicitly
14. Be specific: name dates, figures, percentages, treaty names where they strengthen the argument
15. Assume the consuming LLM has no prior regional expertise; references must be self-contained
16. Where a concept has a parallel in another country, a brief comparative aside (one sentence) is acceptable but not overused
17. Cite sources using Markdown footnote syntax ([^N]) for every substantive factual claim, statistic, date, treaty reference, or institutional detail
18. Footnotes defined in a ## Sources section at the end of each pass's output
19. Minimum 15-25 citations per pass
20. Prioritize primary and institutional sources over journalistic sources
21. Favor causal explanation over description ("X happened because Y, which means Z for today")

**Total cross-cutting asks: 21**

## Section 1: Formation Trauma & Founding Mythology

**Pass: 1 | Asks: 10**

1. Explain how the state came into being (revolution, decolonization, partition, unification, secession, collapse of predecessor state)
2. State the founding narrative the state tells itself and how it diverges from the historical record
3. Identify specifically what the official narrative suppresses, rewrites, or mythologizes -- and what those distortions reveal about current political anxieties
4. Identify specific historical wounds or triumphs that continue to shape elite threat perception, national identity, and political legitimacy
5. For countries with multiple layered formation traumas: analyze how they interact rather than treating only the most recent one
6. Explain how the origin story is actively maintained, contested, or instrumentalized today
7. Where multiple competing founding narratives exist: map which political forces champion which version, and what is at stake in the contest
8. Identify which narrative is ascendant, which is being suppressed, and what political shifts would change the balance
9. Identify specific institutional channels through which the founding narrative is transmitted: school curricula, military training/oath language, state media, national holidays, museums/memorials, official historical commissions
10. Note that changes to transmission channels (revised textbook, renamed holiday, reframed museum exhibit) are observable indicators of narrative renegotiation

## Section 2: Geographic Determinism & Persistent Strategic Problems
