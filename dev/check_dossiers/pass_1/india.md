# India: Middle Powers Monitor analytical dossier

India is the world's most populous country, its fourth-largest economy, and one of only two states facing nuclear-armed adversaries on two land borders simultaneously. Under Prime Minister Narendra Modi's third consecutive term — now governing via coalition for the first time — India is undergoing a simultaneous civilizational rebranding, military modernization, digital transformation, and geopolitical repositioning that makes it arguably the most consequential "middle power" of the 2020s. This dossier maps the structural architecture of Indian power: its institutions, traumas, geography, inheritances, fractures, demographics, economy, and infrastructure.

---

## Section 0: Key actors and institutions

### The BJP's centralized power architecture

India's political system is dominated by the **Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP)** under Narendra Modi, now serving a historic third consecutive term as Prime Minister (sworn in June 9, 2024). The 2024 general elections, however, delivered a significant correction: BJP won **240 Lok Sabha seats**, down from 303 in 2019, falling short of the 272-seat majority mark and forcing reliance on coalition partners for the first time under Modi. The National Democratic Alliance (NDA) coalition holds **293 of 543 seats**, with critical support from Telugu Desam Party (16 seats, Chandrababu Naidu), Janata Dal (United) (12 seats, Nitish Kumar), and Shiv Sena's Eknath Shinde faction (7 seats).

The power structure beneath Modi is highly centralized. **Amit Shah** serves as Home Minister and is widely considered the second most powerful figure in government. **Rajnath Singh** holds Defence, **S. Jaishankar** leads External Affairs, and **Nirmala Sitharaman** manages Finance. The Prime Minister's Office under Principal Secretary P.K. Mishra (IAS, 1972 Gujarat cadre) functions as the paramount decision-making node — described by analysts as the most centralized PMO in Indian democratic history. The **Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS)**, chaired by Modi with Shah, Singh, Jaishankar, and Sitharaman, is the apex national security body and approved Operation Sindoor during the May 2025 India-Pakistan confrontation.

**National Security Advisor Ajit Doval** (IPS retired, 1968 batch, serving since 2014, now in his third term at cabinet minister rank) is the de facto architect of India's security posture. Since 2018, the Strategic Policy Group reports to him rather than the Cabinet Secretary, consolidating intelligence and security coordination under a single figure.

In the **Rajya Sabha** (upper house), the NDA holds approximately 115-121 seats of an effective 234, giving it a workable but not commanding majority. The opposition INDIA bloc holds roughly 85 seats, with Congress at approximately 27.

### The opposition landscape

The opposition operates through the loosely constituted INDIA alliance, which secured approximately 234 Lok Sabha seats. The **Indian National Congress** under president Mallikarjun Kharge and campaign leader Rahul Gandhi (now Leader of Opposition, the first formally recognized since 2014) won **99 seats** — a meaningful recovery. The **Samajwadi Party** under Akhilesh Yadav surged to 37 seats in Uttar Pradesh on caste-based mobilization. **Trinamool Congress** (Mamata Banerjee, 29 seats), **DMK** (M.K. Stalin, 22 seats), and smaller parties round out the opposition. The **Aam Aadmi Party** under Arvind Kejriwal was reduced to 3 seats and lost Delhi to BJP in February 2025.

### Security and military apparatus

India maintains **over 1.5 million active military personnel** — the world's second-largest military force and largest volunteer army. Chief of Defence Staff General Anil Chauhan oversees integration reforms including the contested theaterization initiative. The paramilitary and Central Armed Police Forces add another **million-plus personnel** across CRPF, BSF, CISF, ITBP, SSB, and Assam Rifles. The defence budget for FY2026-27 exceeded **$86.7 billion** (a 15% increase), the fourth-largest globally, driven partly by the May 2025 India-Pakistan confrontation.

The intelligence architecture operates through RAW (external, under Secretary Parag Jain) and the Intelligence Bureau (domestic, under Director Tapan Kumar Deka). The CBI handles major investigations under Director Praveen Sood, while the NIA manages terrorism cases.

### Economic power brokers

The **Reserve Bank of India** under Governor Sanjay Malhotra (since December 2024) manages monetary policy with a 4% inflation target. SEBI under Chairman Tuhin Kanta Pandey (since early 2025) succeeded the controversial tenure of Madhabi Puri Buch. India's corporate landscape is dominated by three conglomerates with outsized political significance. **Reliance Industries** (Mukesh Ambani) is India's most valuable company with over $125 billion in annual revenue — its telecom arm Jio commands 488 million subscribers. **Adani Group** (Gautam Adani) controls ports, airports, energy, and cement but faces ongoing legal challenges including a November 2024 US DOJ indictment on bribery charges. **Tata Group** under N. Chandrasekaran represents the most diversified portfolio across IT, steel, airlines, and automobiles.

The Supreme Court's February 2024 ruling striking down **electoral bonds** as unconstitutional revealed that BJP received approximately 57% of all bond-denominated political funding (₹6,000 crore), and that 15 of the top 30 corporate donors were under investigation by enforcement agencies at the time of their donations.

### The RSS-BJP relationship

The **Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh**, which celebrated its centenary in October 2025, operates over **80,000 daily shakhas** with an estimated 7.5 million sworn members under Sarsanghchalak Mohan Bhagwat. Its affiliate network — the Sangh Parivar — includes the VHP, ABVP (student wing), BMS (labor), Swadeshi Jagran Manch (economic nationalism), and Bajrang Dal. While RSS provides BJP its ideological framework, cadre, and electoral mobilization capacity, the relationship has grown increasingly complex. Bhagwat explicitly denied in August 2025 that RSS decides BJP matters, and Modi has been described as having "eclipsed" Bhagwat's authority — a departure from the historical pattern of BJP leaders deferring to RSS leadership.

President **Droupadi Murmu** (the first tribal woman to hold the office, since July 2022) serves as constitutional head of state and supreme commander of the armed forces, with largely ceremonial powers.

## Section 1: Formation trauma and founding mythology

### Partition's unhealed wound

The 1947 Partition of British India remains the foundational trauma shaping Indian state identity, security posture, and communal politics. Scholarly estimates of the death toll range from **200,000 to 3.2 million**, with Harvard researchers estimating 3.7 million "missing" persons. Between **12 and 20 million people** were displaced — the largest mass migration in human history — as Muslims moved toward Pakistan and Hindus and Sikhs toward India. The Radcliffe Line, drawn by a British lawyer who had never visited India and was given only five weeks, was published two days after independence to avoid disrupting celebrations. Sir Cyril Radcliffe destroyed all his papers before leaving.

The institutional impacts were profound. The British Indian Army was divided roughly 2:1, with India receiving approximately 260,000 troops and all 16 ordnance factories. The railway network was severed, and the Radcliffe Line bisected Punjab's irrigation system — headworks in India, canals in Pakistan. Integration of over **560 princely states** covering 40% of India's landmass fell to Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, who used diplomacy, financial incentives, and force. The contested accession of Kashmir produced the first Indo-Pakistani War and a dispute that persists to this day.

### Two competing origin stories

India's founding narrative is now a site of active contestation between two incompatible visions. The **Nehruvian narrative** — dominant from 1947 through the 1990s — framed India as a secular, socialist, democratic, non-aligned republic built on the "Tryst with Destiny" vision of scientific temper, planned development, and equal respect for all religions. The Congress party served as institutional carrier of this consensus for four decades.

The **Hindutva counter-narrative**, rooted in V.D. Savarkar's 1923 pamphlet and the RSS's 1925 founding, frames India as an inherently Hindu civilizational state with an unbroken chain stretching millennia. Under BJP governance since 2014, this counter-narrative has moved from the margins to state policy. The effort is systematic: **Nehru has been progressively de-emphasized** in NCERT textbooks and national commemorations (he was conspicuously absent from India's 75th anniversary celebrations in 2022), while **Sardar Patel has been elevated** through the 182-meter Statue of Unity (inaugurated 2018, world's tallest statue, ~$410 million), the creation of National Unity Day on his birthday, and framing him as the "true" unifier of India — a muscular alternative to Nehru.

NCERT textbook revisions document the scope of the rewriting: Mughal history chapters have been deleted, references to the 2002 Gujarat riots removed, the Babri Masjid renamed as "three-dome structure," and new textbooks under the National Education Policy eliminate references to the Delhi Sultanate and Mughal period entirely from Class 7, replacing them with chapters on ancient Hindu dynasties and "sacred geography." City renamings reinforce the project — Allahabad to Prayagraj, Aurangabad to Chhatrapati Sambhaji Nagar, Aurangzeb Road to A.P.J. Abdul Kalam Road.

### Ram Mandir as narrative culmination

The consecration of the **Ram Mandir in Ayodhya on January 22, 2024** — on the site of the Babri Masjid demolished in 1992 — fulfilled the BJP's most foundational campaign promise dating to the late 1980s. Modi performed the Pran Pratishtha ceremony before 7,000 invitees. The temple, together with the 2019 abrogation of Article 370, completed two of three core BJP commitments (the Uniform Civil Code being the third).

Yet the electoral payoff was surprisingly limited. The BJP **lost the Ayodhya constituency itself** in the 2024 elections, and nationally fell from 303 to 240 seats. Analysts suggest the temple's utility as a mobilization tool peaked with its construction, and that economic grievances and caste-based counter-mobilization blunted the expected Hindutva dividend.

B.R. Ambedkar — Dalit leader and chief architect of the Constitution — now occupies a uniquely contested position in Indian political mythology, claimed simultaneously by BJP (which highlights his conflicts with Congress), Dalit movements (which venerate him as anti-caste icon), and the opposition (which frames itself as protector of Ambedkar's constitutional legacy).

## Section 2: Geographic determinism

### A subcontinent locked between mountains and ocean

India's **3.29 million km²** sits at a unique geographic intersection: the Himalayan barrier to the north, the Indian Ocean to the south, and land borders with six nations totaling **15,107 km**. This geography creates both immense strategic advantages and acute vulnerabilities.

The **Himalayan arc** provides a natural defensive barrier but also produces two of India's most persistent strategic problems. The **Line of Actual Control (LAC)** with China stretches approximately 3,488 km across three disputed sectors — the Western Sector in Ladakh (where China controls Aksai Chin, ~38,000 km² claimed by India), the Middle Sector in Himachal Pradesh/Uttarakhand, and the Eastern Sector where China claims all of Arunachal Pradesh (~90,000 km²) as "South Tibet." The June 2020 Galwan Valley clash — killing 20 Indian and 4 Chinese soldiers in hand-to-hand combat — triggered a fundamental military reorientation. By 2025, India maintained **197 Border Out Posts** along the LAC (40 new posts added since 2021) and both sides had deployed approximately 50,000-60,000 additional troops. An October 2024 patrolling agreement enabled disengagement at Depsang Plains and Demchok, but trust has not returned. As one assessment concluded: "The LAC has become a continuously managed security problem rather than an episodic crisis."

The **Siliguri Corridor** — the "Chicken's Neck" connecting mainland India to its eight northeastern states — is only **20-22 km wide** at its narrowest point, with China's Chumbi Valley just 60 km away. India has established new garrisons and is exploring subterranean tunnel infrastructure to mitigate this vulnerability. China's involvement in projects near the corridor (a $1 billion industrial park in Nepal's Jhapa district, Bhutan's Gelephu Mindfulness City) compounds the concern.

### Indian Ocean as strategic moat and highway

India's peninsular projection into the Indian Ocean, combined with the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands** (positioned ~200 km from the Malacca Strait), provides extraordinary maritime leverage. Over **90% of India's trade by volume** transits the Indian Ocean. INS Baaz, India's southernmost naval air station on Great Nicobar, sits barely 100 km from the Malacca Strait — the chokepoint through which 78% of China's oil supply and 70% of its trade with Europe, Africa, and the Gulf transits.

India's counter to China's "String of Pearls" (ports at Gwadar, Hambantota, Djibouti, Kyaukpyu) is a "Necklace of Diamonds" strategy encompassing access agreements at **Changi (Singapore), Sabang (Indonesia), Duqm (Oman), Agalega (Mauritius)**, and the Chabahar port in Iran. The Indian Navy operates 130+ warships with plans to expand to 175-200 by 2035, anchored by INS Vikrant, India's first indigenous aircraft carrier commissioned in 2022. India's total EEZ spans approximately **2.0-2.4 million km²**.

### Water as a weapon and vulnerability

India's water security is under multiple pressures. The **Indus Waters Treaty** — the world's most durable transboundary water agreement since 1960 — was **declared "in abeyance"** by India on April 23, 2025, following the Pahalgam terrorist attack. India stopped flow on the Chenab River and halted hydrological data sharing, weaponizing its upstream position over Pakistan (whose 268 million people depend on the Indus basin). On the Brahmaputra, Chinese dam-building upstream in Tibet — including a mega-dam now under construction — threatens to give Beijing "hydro-hegemonic" leverage over Northeast India and Bangladesh, though only 7-10% of the river's flow originates in Chinese territory.

India is the **world's largest groundwater consumer** at 241 billion cubic meters annually (25% of global withdrawal). GRACE satellite data shows a net loss of 450 km³ during 2002-2022, with North India contributing 95% of depletion. Punjab, Haryana, and Rajasthan face the most critical shortages. Some **820 million Indians** face high to extreme water stress.

### Nuclear geography compounds the challenge

India faces the unique strategic challenge of **two nuclear-armed neighbors**. Per SIPRI's January 2025 estimates, India possesses approximately **180 warheads**, Pakistan **170**, and China **600** (growing at ~100 per year). India's nuclear triad reached operational maturity in 2024-2026 with the commissioning of INS Arighaat (second SSBN, August 2024) and the expected commissioning of INS Aridhaman (third SSBN, early 2026, with 8 missile tubes), enabling **continuous at-sea deterrence** for the first time. The March 2024 test of Agni-V with MIRV capability ("Divyastra") demonstrated the ability to deliver multiple warheads, covering all of China. India maintains a No First Use policy, though the shift toward canisterized missiles and continuous SSBN patrols represents an evolution in operational posture.

## Section 3: Imperial legacy and institutional inheritance

### The steel frame persists

India's institutional architecture is fundamentally a colonial inheritance, modified but not replaced. The **Indian Administrative Service (IAS)** — comprising roughly 5,000-6,000 officers recruited through a UPSC examination with sub-0.1% success rates — is a direct successor of the Indian Civil Service (ICS), retaining its hierarchical structure, cadre allocation system, and the district collector role that was the colonial administrator's most potent instrument. Over 50% of the first IAS cadre were absorbed ICS officers. Nehru, despite calling the ICS "neither Indian, nor civil, nor a service," retained the institution and appointed ICS officers to key posts.

The **common law legal system**, **Westminster parliamentary model** (bicameral legislature, cabinet government, first-past-the-post elections), **English language hierarchy** (English remains the language of the Supreme Court, High Courts, higher education, and elite communication), and **railway network** (built for resource extraction and military movement, not economic integration) are all direct colonial legacies. The railway, at approximately **70,000 route km**, was the most visible institutional inheritance — originally designed with multiple gauges creating over 200 "gauge breaks," a problem largely resolved through Project Unigauge (converting to broad gauge since 1992).

### Colonial census and the hardening of identity

The British colonial census beginning in 1871 transformed fluid social categories into rigid political identities. H.H. Risley's 1901 census attempted to classify castes using pseudoscientific anthropometric methods, imposing Brahminical varna hierarchy as a universal framework. As Nicholas Dirks argued, "Risley's anthropology worked not so much to retard nationalism as to render it communal." The census created unified caste categories where none had existed (the term "Yadav" consolidated geographically disparate communities), and colonial laws (Land Alienation Act 1900, Punjab Pre-Emption Act 1913) entrenched caste as a legally operative category.

The last comprehensive caste census was conducted in **1931**. In April 2025, the Modi government announced inclusion of caste enumeration in the upcoming national census — the first in nearly a century — a decision likely to reshape Indian politics as profoundly as the Mandal Commission did in 1990.

The **"martial races" theory**, developed after the 1857 Mutiny to channel recruitment toward loyal communities (Sikhs, Gurkhas, Rajputs, Jats, Dogras), was formally abolished in 1949 but persists in practice. Punjab and Haryana remain dramatically overrepresented in the armed forces.

### Land systems shaped divergent political economies

The three colonial land revenue systems created lasting regional divergence. **Zamindari** areas (Bengal, Bihar) developed entrenched landlord politics and caste-based agrarian conflicts that fueled the Naxalite movement. **Ryotwari** areas (Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra) produced stronger peasant proprietorship and different mobilization patterns. **Mahalwari** areas (UP, Punjab) shaped village-based power structures. Despite post-independence land ceiling acts, implementation was deeply uneven — strongest in Kerala and West Bengal under left governments, weakest in Bihar and UP — and land distribution remains highly unequal.

India's elite formation pathways trace directly to colonial-era institutions. The **IITs** (first established 1950 at Kharagpur, now 23 campuses) accept fewer than 0.7% of applicants. A 2023 NBER study found **62% of top-100 JEE scorers** migrated abroad. The UPSC civil service examination, IIMs (22 campuses), and English-medium education collectively reproduce an elite class that, as Macaulay intended in 1835, remains "Indian in blood and colour, but English in tastes."

## Section 4: Ethnic, sectarian, and linguistic cleavage structure

### Caste as the primary axis of Indian social life

Caste remains India's most fundamental social cleavage — more persistent than religion, language, or region in structuring life chances. Per the 2011 census, **Scheduled Castes (Dalits) comprise 16.6%** of the population (~201 million), **Scheduled Tribes 8.6%** (~104 million), and **Other Backward Classes** an estimated 40-52% (the precise figure is unknown, as no national caste census has been conducted since 1931). Bihar's 2023 state caste survey found OBCs at 63% of the population.

Caste operates through endogamy (inter-caste marriages are estimated at only **5-6% nationally**), occupational segregation (an estimated 1.3 million Dalits work as manual scavengers), and systematic violence. The NCRB recorded **57,582 crimes against Scheduled Castes** in 2022, a 13.1% increase — one crime every 18 minutes, 13 murders weekly. Conviction rates remain low at approximately 38%. Dalit land ownership stands at just **2.2%** versus a national average of 17.9%.

The **reservation system** — India's primary institutional mechanism for managing caste inequality — currently allocates 15% of government positions for SCs, 7.5% for STs, 27% for OBCs, and 10% for Economically Weaker Sections (added 2019), totaling 59.5% and exceeding the Supreme Court's nominal 50% ceiling. Tamil Nadu maintains **69% reservation** protected by Ninth Schedule inclusion. Dominant-caste agitations for reservation inclusion (Marathas, Patidars, Jats) have produced violent protests and political crises, reflecting the system's centrality to Indian political economy.

### Hindu-Muslim polarization as political strategy

India's approximately **200 million Muslims** (14.2% of the population — one of the world's largest Muslim populations) face what amounts to systematic political marginalization under BJP governance. Legislative actions include the Citizenship Amendment Act (2019), which created India's first religion-based citizenship criterion explicitly **excluding Muslims**; the criminalization of triple talaq; anti-conversion laws in BJP-governed states; and the Waqf Amendment Act (2025) mandating non-Muslim members on Waqf Boards.

Muslim political representation has declined to historic lows: only **24 Muslim MPs** in the 2024 Lok Sabha (4.4%, versus 14% of the population), with **zero from the BJP**. The party fielded only one Muslim candidate nationally. Even "secular" parties reduced Muslim nominations to an all-time low of 94 candidates.

Communal violence has intensified measurably. A 2024 report documented **59 communal riots** — an 84% increase from 2023 — with 49 of 59 occurring in BJP-governed states. The Pew Research Social Hostilities Index scored India at **9.3 out of 10** (higher than Pakistan or Afghanistan). "Bulldozer politics" — punitive demolitions of Muslim homes — has been documented in at least 100 instances since 2019, predominantly in BJP-ruled Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh.

### Linguistic diversity resists centralization

India's **22 scheduled languages** and approximately 780 living languages constitute its second major cleavage axis. Hindi (broadly defined) is spoken by ~42% of the population, but South Indian states fiercely resist Hindi imposition. Tamil Nadu follows a two-language policy (Tamil and English only), rooted in the 1965 anti-Hindi agitation that killed at least 80 people and permanently destroyed Congress in the state. The DMK under Chief Minister M.K. Stalin continues to oppose Hindi through the BJP's National Education Policy, defending what amounts to a linguistic federalism that checks centralization.

### Cross-cutting versus reinforcing fractures

The critical analytical question is whether India's multiple cleavages cross-cut (reducing conflict intensity) or reinforce each other (concentrating disadvantage). The answer is both, with the balance shifting. Cleavages cross-cut when a Tamil Brahmin and Tamil Dalit share language but not caste, or when Hindi-speaking Hindus and Muslims share language but not religion. But they reinforce powerfully along class lines: Muslims are disproportionately poor (the 2006 Sachar Committee documented Muslims trailing even Dalits in multiple development indicators), lower castes correlate strongly with poverty, and tribal areas overlap with the Naxal Red Corridor.

The **central political contest** of contemporary India is between BJP's project of subsuming caste, linguistic, and regional identities under a unifying Hindu identity, and the opposition's effort to reassert caste as the primary political axis. The 2024 election results — where BJP lost its majority partly due to OBC/Dalit counter-mobilization in UP — suggest limits to Hindu consolidation when economic grievances and caste consciousness are activated. The caste census decision of April 2025 may prove as politically transformative as the Mandal Commission was in 1990.

## Section 5: Demographic tides

### The world's largest population at a crossroads

India's population stands at approximately **1.46-1.48 billion** (2025-2026), having surpassed China in April 2023. Its median age of **~28-29 years** compares with China's 40, Japan's 49, and the United States' 38. Over 65% of the population is under 35, and the working-age cohort (15-64) constitutes approximately **68%** of the total — a dependency ratio projected to reach its lowest point around 2030.

The demographic dividend window extends from roughly **2005 to 2055**, peaking around 2041. India has used approximately one-third of this window. The UN's medium variant projects population peaking around **1.67-1.7 billion in 2050-2064** before beginning a gradual decline. But the window's value depends entirely on whether India can absorb its youth into productive employment.

### The employment crisis at the heart of the dividend

Approximately **10-12 million young Indians** enter the labor force annually, but the economy is not generating sufficient quality jobs. Official unemployment (PLFS) runs at 5-6%, but youth unemployment (15-29) stands at **13.8-14.9%**, and educated unemployment is rising sharply — the share of educated among the unemployed rose from 54.2% (2000) to 65.7% (2022). **Female labor force participation** remains strikingly low at approximately 24-37% (varying by measure), well below the global average of 47%.

The structural problem is "jobless growth": GDP grows at 6-7% but over **90% of the workforce** remains in informal employment — precarious, low-paid, with minimal social protection. Only 5% of India's workforce has formal vocational training, compared with 52% in the United States, 75% in Germany, and 96% in South Korea. Manufacturing's share of GDP has stubbornly refused to rise above 15-17%, despite the Make in India initiative.

The **Agnipath scheme** (launched June 2022), which recruits military "Agniveers" for 4-year terms with only 25% retention, exemplifies the tension between institutional reform and employment expectations. It sparked violent nationwide protests and is under review for possible extension to 7-8 year terms and 60-70% retention rates.

### Fertility decline is faster than expected

India's Total Fertility Rate has fallen to approximately **1.9** (SRS 2023) — below the 2.1 replacement level. The decline is dramatic: from 5.9 in 1950 to 2.0 in 2019-2021. However, the national figure masks extreme regional variation. Southern states have fertility rates comparable to developed nations — Sikkim at **1.1**, Tamil Nadu and West Bengal at **1.6**, Kerala at 1.7 — while Bihar remains at **3.0** and Uttar Pradesh at ~2.4. By 2023, all Indian states except UP and Bihar had fallen below replacement.

This creates a profound structural tension: the demographic dividend is concentrated in India's poorest, least-educated northern states, while the economically dynamic south faces aging populations. Kerala's 60+ cohort already constitutes approximately 20% of the state's population. The political implications are significant — southern states that successfully controlled population growth face reduced parliamentary representation under the constitution's population-based seat allocation.

### Diaspora as strategic asset

India's global diaspora numbers approximately **35.4 million**, generating **$118-125 billion in annual remittances** — the world's largest. The composition of remittance sources is shifting: advanced economies (US 27.7%, UK 10.8%) now contribute over 50% for the first time, surpassing the Gulf states (37.9%). Indian-Americans (~4.4 million) represent one of the highest-income ethnic groups in the US, with Indian-origin CEOs leading Microsoft, Google, and IBM. The BJP maintains active diaspora engagement through Overseas Friends of BJP, and Modi's stadium rallies (Houston's "Howdy Modi," Sydney, London) build his global leader image for domestic consumption.

The diaspora has also produced strategic liabilities. The alleged assassination of Khalistani separatist Hardeep Singh Nijjar in Canada (June 2023) and the US DOJ-charged plot against Sikh separatist Gurpatwant Singh Pannun triggered a major diplomatic crisis with Canada and raised questions about Indian intelligence operations on allied territory.

## Section 6: Economic structure

### A $4.3 trillion economy with $2,900 per capita income

India's economy embodies a structural duality more extreme than any comparable power. **Nominal GDP** stands at approximately $4.13-4.3 trillion (2025-2026), making India the world's fourth-largest economy ($17.7 trillion in PPP terms, third globally). Growth runs at **6.5-6.6%** projected for FY2025-26. Yet GDP per capita remains approximately **$2,900** — ranking India around 136th globally and placing it firmly in the lower-middle-income category.

The sectoral structure is unusually services-heavy for India's development stage: **services contribute 55% of GVA** but employ only 30% of the workforce, while **agriculture contributes just 18% of GVA** but employs **46%** of workers — the widest sectoral productivity gap in any major economy. The IT/BPM sector alone generates $254 billion in revenue and directly employs 5.4 million people, with the US accounting for 55% of exports. Manufacturing's persistent failure to rise above 15-17% of GDP despite PLI schemes across 14 sectors remains India's most significant economic shortcoming.

### State-business entanglement and crony capitalism

The relationship between India's dominant conglomerates and the BJP government represents a structural feature rather than an aberration. **Reliance Industries** (annual revenue exceeding $125 billion, Jio's 488 million telecom subscribers, India's largest retail chain) and **Adani Group** (India's largest private port operator, six airports, energy, cement, NDTV media) both maintain documented proximity to government.

The Supreme Court's February 2024 electoral bonds ruling exposed the architecture of political funding: BJP received **57% of all bond-denominated donations** (~$720 million), and 15 of the top 30 corporate donors were under investigation by enforcement agencies at the time of donation — all 10 top corporate donors under probe paid the BJP. After bonds were struck down, corporate donations shifted to electoral trusts, with BJP receiving 83% of trust funding in 2024-25.

Adani's trajectory illustrates the dynamic. The group's market cap peaked at $206 billion before the January 2023 Hindenburg Research report triggered a **$104 billion+ loss**. Values subsequently recovered, but the November 2024 US DOJ indictment on bribery charges and ongoing SEC proceedings add international legal risk. Both Adani and Ambani hail from Gujarat, and as France's Le Monde noted, Adani's success "cannot be attributed to innovation... the Indian state plays a significant role in allocating licenses or signing contracts."

### Trade reorientation and energy vulnerability

India's trade dependencies are shifting rapidly. The US remains the largest trading partner at ~$132 billion, but the **trade deficit with China has more than doubled in four years to $99.2 billion** (FY2024-25). India imports approximately 7x more from China than it exports, with critical dependencies in electronics, telecom equipment, and pharmaceutical APIs.

Energy vulnerability is India's most acute structural weakness: **87% of crude oil is imported**, with Russia surging from less than 1% of imports pre-2022 to approximately **35-40% by mid-2025** as India purchased discounted crude at ~1.7 million barrels per day. This triggered US retaliation: a **50% tariff on Indian exports** (25% reciprocal + 25% penalty for Russian oil purchases) imposed in August 2025, threatening to reduce GDP by ~0.5%. Post-sanctions pressure in late 2025 forced Russian crude's share below 25%.

### The digital economy leapfrog

India's most transformative structural development is its **digital public infrastructure**. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI) processed **21.6 billion transactions worth $327 billion in December 2025 alone** — accounting for approximately 50% of global real-time payments. Over **500 million unique users** and 65 million merchants are connected. **Aadhaar** has enrolled 1.42 billion individuals in the world's largest biometric identity system. India Stack (Aadhaar + UPI + DigiLocker + Account Aggregator) represents arguably the most advanced digital public infrastructure at scale globally, enabling direct benefit transfers that bypass traditional leakage channels.

India achieved a significant milestone with S&P upgrading its sovereign rating to **BBB** (stable) — the first upgrade in 18 years. Forex reserves stand at approximately **$700 billion** (fourth-largest globally), providing 11+ months of import cover. Gross NPAs have fallen to a historic low of **2.15%** for scheduled commercial banks, down from a peak of 11.2% in 2017-18.

## Section 7: Infrastructural inheritance and resource pathways

### Railways transform from colonial relic to modernization showcase

India's **~70,000 route-km railway network** — the world's fourth-largest — has undergone dramatic transformation while retaining its colonial-era institutional culture. Electrification has reached **99.4%** of the broad-gauge network (from a pace of 1.42 km/day in 2004-2014 to 15+ km/day in 2019-2025). The Eastern Dedicated Freight Corridor (1,337 km) is fully operational and the Western DFC (1,506 km) nearly complete, handling 200 million tonnes annually at 100 km/h — addressing the colonial-era problem of freight and passenger trains sharing tracks.

The **Mumbai-Ahmedabad bullet train** (508 km, Japanese Shinkansen technology, $10.6 billion JICA loan at 0.1% interest) is approximately 50% complete with full operation expected by 2029. **164 Vande Bharat semi-high-speed services** are operational, with the first sleeper variant launched in January 2026. Four additional freight corridors are planned, representing a total investment exceeding ₹2 lakh crore.

### Energy infrastructure at an inflection point

India crossed **505 GW of total installed power capacity** in September 2025, with non-fossil fuel sources exceeding 50% of installed capacity for the first time — **five years ahead** of the Paris Agreement NDC target. Solar capacity reached **133 GW** (crossing 100 GW in January 2025), and a record 34.4 GW of solar and wind was added in the first nine months of 2025 alone.

Yet coal remains dominant in actual generation at approximately **44% of electricity** — a gap between installed capacity and generation that reflects renewable intermittency. The government plans 100 GW of new coal capacity over seven years, even as analyses show existing and under-construction coal assets already exceed 2030 requirements. This contradictory posture — world-leading renewable deployment alongside continued coal expansion — captures India's energy dilemma.

**Nuclear power** contributes modestly (24-25 reactors, ~8.8 GW, ~3% of generation) but is positioned for significant expansion. The December 2025 SHANTI Bill enables private sector participation for the first time, and the Nuclear Energy Mission targets **100 GW by 2047**. India's strategic petroleum reserves total only 5.33 million metric tonnes (~10 days of import cover), a thin buffer for a nation importing 87% of its crude.

### Digital infrastructure as competitive advantage

Jio's September 2016 launch — offering free data that crashed prices from ~₹250/GB to under ₹10/GB — represents one of the most disruptive infrastructure events in any developing country's history. Average monthly data consumption per subscriber reached **24 GB** (June 2025), up from 62 MB in 2014 — a 399-fold increase. India's 5G rollout covers 99.6% of districts with 486,000+ base stations deployed. Over **1 billion internet subscribers** are connected, with rural internet users growing from 115 million (2016) to 405 million (2024).

New submarine cables (SEA-ME-WE-6, 2Africa Pearls, Google's Blue-Raman, Meta's ~$10 billion cable, Jio-backed IAX and IEX) are expected to **quadruple** India's international bandwidth. BharatNet has laid 694,711 km of fiber to connect rural gram panchayats, though only 62% of connected households receive reliable year-round service.

### Border infrastructure as strategic reorientation

India's most revealing infrastructure investments are along its contested borders with China. The **India-China Border Roads program** now encompasses 177 roads totaling 10,023+ km in three phases, with 104 roads (6,700+ km) approved in Phase III after the 2020 Galwan clash. Strategic tunnels have transformed connectivity: the **Atal Tunnel** (9.02 km, inaugurated 2020) provides all-weather access to Leh; the **Sela Tunnel** (inaugurated March 2024) conceals troop movements to Tawang from Chinese surveillance; and the **Shinkun La Tunnel** (under construction, 15,800 ft) will be the world's highest. The **Mudh-Nyoma airfield** in eastern Ladakh — India's highest at 13,710 ft — is being upgraded to accommodate C-17 strategic transport aircraft.

The **India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC)**, announced at the September 2023 G20 summit, represents India's bid for an alternative to China's Belt and Road Initiative. Encompassing rail, maritime, energy pipeline, and digital cable links from India through the Gulf to Europe, IMEC aims to reduce India-Europe transit time by 40% and logistics costs by 30%. However, the Gaza conflict delayed Saudi-Israeli normalization critical to the rail segment, and key infrastructure exists "largely on paper" with no implementing body or dedicated funding mechanism yet established.

India's infrastructure trajectory reveals a clear strategic reorientation: from colonial extraction patterns to domestic integration (dedicated freight corridors, Bharatmala economic corridors); from China-avoidance to China-deterrence (massive LAC infrastructure push post-2020); from fossil dependency to diversified energy (solar records, green hydrogen, nuclear expansion); and from single-corridor to multi-modal connectivity (IMEC as BRI alternative, Chabahar as Pakistan bypass, Northeast connectivity under Act East).

## Conclusion: Structural tensions that will shape the next decade

Five structural tensions define India's trajectory. First, the **centralization-federalism tension**: Modi's unprecedented concentration of power at the center collides with linguistic, caste, and regional diversities that resist homogenization — the 2024 election result was partly a reassertion of this pluralism. Second, the **demographic clock**: India's 50-year dividend window is one-third spent, and the mismatch between 12 million annual labor market entrants and an economy generating insufficient formal employment represents the country's single greatest structural risk. Third, the **two-front security dilemma**: managing nuclear-armed Pakistan and a rising China simultaneously, with a defense budget that has tripled in 12 years but remains stretched across an enormous conventional force structure and a maturing nuclear triad. Fourth, the **digital-informal paradox**: the world's most advanced digital public infrastructure sits atop an economy where 90% of the workforce operates informally, creating a governance architecture capable of distributing welfare digitally but unable to create the industrial employment that powered East Asian developmental states. Fifth, the **civilizational rebranding gamble**: whether the BJP's project of redefining India as a Hindu civilizational state consolidates national identity or fractures it along the caste and communal lines that the reservation system and constitutional secularism were designed to manage.

India's strategic position is stronger than at any point since independence — nuclear triad maturity, $700 billion in forex reserves, digital infrastructure rivaling any developed nation, and a median age 12 years younger than China's. But the window in which these advantages translate into durable power is finite, and the institutional quality required to seize it remains the fundamental unknown.
