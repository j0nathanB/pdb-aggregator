**Prepared:** 2026-03-13
**Primary languages of political discourse:** English, French

---

## MEDIA LANDSCAPE SUMMARY

Canada's media ecosystem is dominated by a handful of large conglomerates -- CBC/Radio-Canada (public broadcaster), Bell Media (CTV, BNN Bloomberg, CP24), and Postmedia Network (National Post, Ottawa Citizen, and 130+ regional papers, now majority-owned by U.S.-based Chatham Asset Management). The Globe and Mail and Toronto Star remain independently significant English-language broadsheets, while La Presse (now a nonprofit) and Le Devoir anchor French-language political journalism from Montreal. Trust in legacy brands remains relatively high compared to digital-native sources, particularly among French-speaking audiences, though overall trust levels are roughly 20 points below 2018 peaks. For foreign policy and defence coverage specifically, the ecosystem is thin: original reporting concentrates in CBC, the Globe and Mail, and The Hill Times, supplemented by think-tank publishing from CIGI, the Macdonald-Laurier Institute, and the Canadian International Council's OpenCanada. The 2025 federal election and the escalating Canada-U.S. tensions under the second Trump administration have sharpened coverage of sovereignty, defence spending, and trade statecraft.

## COVERAGE GAP ASSESSMENT

The most significant gap is the absence of a dedicated Canadian outlet for intelligence and national-security reporting comparable to The Intercept, Bellingcat, or the UK's RUSI. Intelligence-related stories surface sporadically in the Globe and Mail and Global News but are not systematically covered. Arctic sovereignty and Northern affairs receive thin coverage outside of CBC North and occasional Globe features, despite the Arctic's growing strategic salience. French-language coverage of defence and security matters is notably weaker than English-language coverage -- Radio-Canada and Le Devoir address these topics episodically rather than through dedicated beats.
