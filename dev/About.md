From the Editor
On January 20, 2026, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney named a rupture in the rules-based international order: That although the old world order is dead, the middle powers have the capacity to build a new order that encompasses certain values, such as respect for human rights, sustainable development, solidarity, sovereignty and territorial integrity of the various states. 

It's from this reality that The Middle Powers Monitor emerges, as another voice in an otherwise cacophonous media ecosystem. However, the Monitor also makes use of a compelling element of this reality, Large Language Models. Given that LLMs are uniquely good at reading and synthesizing large amounts of text, it made sense to employ them in the service of people who like to stay on top of geopolitics, but find there to be too many media outlets, each competing voraciously for the reader's attention. 

To that end, the Monitor publishes weekly, and uses a technical documentation layout to present lots of information cleanly. At the same time, AI Agents are used to skim the week's news across the wires (AP, Reuters, BBC, etc.) and do deep dives using domestic and government sources for each country. Each week's stories is then analyzed by another Agent, this one with substantial context into a country's current reality. Additional agents determine whether or not there are any common threads across countries or regions. Finally, the analysis is edited into readable prose. 

The Middle Powers Monitor is a weekly publication tracking 28 countries across five regions. It uses AI agents to read the week's news about a country and analyze a state's positioning through five analytical dimensions: diplomatic alignment, security posture, economic statecraft, institutional engagement, and domestic constraints.

Coverage
The Monitor tracks countries that occupy the strategic space between great powers and smaller states — nations whose positioning decisions carry outsized consequences for regional and global order.

Frontline and Eastern Europe
Ukraine, Poland, Finland, Estonia, Lithuania, Latvia, Czech Republic, Romania
Western Europe
France, Germany, United Kingdom, Italy, Spain, Norway, Sweden
Asia-Pacific
Taiwan, Japan, South Korea, Australia, Indonesia
Near East and South Asia
Turkey, Saudi Arabia, UAE, India
The Americas
Canada, Mexico, Brazil, Chile

The Middle Powers Monitor tracks 28 countries whose strategic decisions will determine whether the liberal international order survives the withdrawal of its traditional guarantor. The selection is not driven by GDP, G20 membership, or geographic representation for its own sake — though the list covers 14 of 20 G20 members and spans five regions. Each country earns its slot by generating active analytical signal on the Monitor's core question: is this country structurally sustaining the institutional architecture of the LIO, or merely identifying with it rhetorically? The European theater is covered in depth because that's where the question is being answered kinetically, from the strategic core that must replace American defense capacity to the frontline states whose security calculus is existential. The Indo-Pacific is included because it's where the next test of deterrence credibility is already being priced in. The swing states — India, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Indonesia — are tracked because their hedging behavior collectively determines whether the order contracts into a Western bloc or retains global reach. The Americas are represented thinly but deliberately: Canada as the sole committed North American defender, Brazil as the Global South's diplomatic heavyweight, and Mexico and Chile as tests of whether the LIO has any structural purchase in Latin America at all. Hegemons (the US, China), systemic disruptors (Russia, Iran, North Korea), and sui generis cases (Israel) are excluded because their gravitational pull warps middle-power analysis. Countries whose trajectories are already settled — Argentina under Milei, for instance — are deferred in favor of countries where the rhetorical-structural alignment question remains genuinely open.

Analytical Framework
Each country is assessed weekly across five signal categories:

    Diplomatic alignment — bilateral and multilateral positioning, alliance management, hedging behavior
    Security & defense — military posture, procurement, operational deployments, intelligence cooperation
    Economic statecraft — trade policy, sanctions compliance, investment flows, energy strategy
    Institutional engagement — participation in international organizations, treaty behavior, norm-setting
    Domestic constraints — regime stability, public opinion, electoral dynamics, civil-military relations

​
Methodology
The Monitor uses a tiered analytical pipeline. Countries are triaged weekly based on signal activity, with high-activity countries receiving full deep-dive treatment and quieter countries held at maintenance. Regional synthesis identifies cross-cutting dynamics, and an executive layer surfaces system-level patterns. Country dossiers provide the structural context that informs weekly analysis — the institutional landscape, key actors, blind spots, and historical patterns that shape how signals should be interpreted.
