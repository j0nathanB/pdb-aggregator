# Structural Country Dossier — Seed Prompt v3

## Usage
Replace `{{COUNTRY}}` with the target country. This prompt generates a seed dossier designed to be periodically updated with current events analysis. Each section is self-contained for targeted refresh.

Generate in two passes to manage context window limits:
- **Pass 1:** Metadata through Section 9 (Illicit Networks & Shadow Governance)
- **Pass 2:** Sections 10–20, with Pass 1 output provided as context

---

## Prompt

You are producing a **Structural Country Dossier** for **{{COUNTRY}}**. This dossier is not a chronological history. It is an analytical reference document that explains why this country behaves the way it does today by identifying the historical structures, traumas, dependencies, and constraints that continue to exert gravitational pull on its decision-makers and population.

### Primary Consumer: Automated Intelligence Pipeline

This dossier will be ingested as persistent context by a large language model that generates weekly intelligence briefs. The LLM will receive this dossier alongside incoming news events and must use it to:
- Determine whether an event represents continuity or deviation from structural patterns
- Generate grounded analytical observations rather than contextless commentary
- Identify when a leader's actions are constrained by structural factors versus representing genuine policy choice
- Detect when surface-level reporting obscures deeper structural dynamics

Write accordingly:
- Every analytical claim should be **concrete and pattern-matchable** — the consuming LLM needs to be able to compare incoming events against specific structural features described here
- Favor **falsifiable assertions** over hedged generalities: "Poland's judiciary crisis creates a persistent vulnerability to EU institutional pressure" is more useful than "Poland has complex relations with the EU"
- Define regional terms, acronyms, and institutional names in-line on first use (e.g., "the IRGC (Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, the ideological military branch parallel to the conventional army)")
- When referencing political figures, briefly identify their role and significance rather than assuming name recognition
- Where evidence is thin or contested, flag it explicitly with a confidence marker: **[HIGH CONFIDENCE]**, **[MODERATE CONFIDENCE]**, or **[ASSESSED]** (for analytical inference rather than established fact)

Each section must conclude with a **`STRUCTURAL CLAIMS`** block: a set of discrete, numbered assertions that distill the section's core analytical content into extractable statements. These are the claims the pipeline LLM will pattern-match against incoming events. Format:

```
STRUCTURAL CLAIMS:
1. [Concrete, falsifiable assertion about a persistent structural feature]
2. [Another assertion]
...
```

#### 0. KEY ACTORS & INSTITUTIONS PRIMER

Provide a concise reference guide to the political actors, institutions, and organizations that the analyst will encounter in current reporting and that recur throughout this dossier. For each entry, explain not just what it is but *why it matters structurally* — what role it plays in the country's power dynamics. Organize into categories (e.g., state institutions and bureaucratic agencies, political parties/movements, security actors — distinguishing between military and internal security services where they are institutionally separate, judiciary and judicial councils where they function as autonomous political actors, economic actors and key conglomerates, religious authorities where they hold constitutional or de facto political power, royal courts or monarchies where they function as parallel power structures, tribal/clan/customary governance leaders where sub-state governance structures carry political weight, non-state and civil society actors, and external actors with persistent domestic influence). Adapt these categories to the country — not every category will be relevant, and some countries will require categories not listed here.

For each actor or institution, explicitly distinguish between its **formal mandate** and its **actual political function** where these diverge. A constitutional court may formally exist to adjudicate law but functionally serve as a regime legitimation tool, an opposition veto point, or a factional weapon. A ministry of defense may nominally control military policy while actual authority resides in a parallel security body or the presidential palace. The pipeline needs to know what an institution *actually does in the power structure*, not what its charter says it should do.

This section should function as a glossary the consuming LLM can reference when processing news articles that mention these actors without explanation. Include standard abbreviations and alternate names used in English-language and domestic media.

For each entry, flag whether the actor is **institutionally stable** (will persist in substantially the same form across changes of government — e.g., the armed forces, the central bank, established courts, entrenched bureaucracies) or **regime-specific** (tied to the current leader or ruling coalition and likely to be restructured, purged, or dissolved after a transition — e.g., a president's personal advisory council, a newly created security body, a ruling party's youth wing). This distinction is essential because this glossary will be used over an extended period that may span political transitions.

#### 1. FORMATION TRAUMA & FOUNDING MYTHOLOGY

How did this state come into being? Was it forged through revolution, decolonization, partition, unification, secession, or the collapse of a predecessor state? What is the founding narrative the state tells itself — and how does it diverge from the historical record? Identify specifically what the official narrative suppresses, rewrites, or mythologizes, and analyze what those distortions reveal about current political anxieties and legitimacy requirements. The gap between the event and the narrative is often more analytically revealing than either one alone.

Identify the specific historical wounds or triumphs that continue to shape elite threat perception, national identity, and political legitimacy. For countries with multiple formation traumas layered on top of each other — post-colonial states with subsequent civil wars, countries that have been founded, dissolved, and reconstituted, or states where a revolutionary founding was followed by a counter-revolutionary restoration — analyze how these layered traumas interact rather than treating only the most recent one as definitive. Often, an earlier trauma creates the institutional and psychological template through which later traumas are experienced and narrated.

Explain how this origin story is actively maintained, contested, or instrumentalized today. Where multiple competing founding narratives exist, map which political forces, parties, or factions champion which version of the story. Identify what is at stake in the contest between narratives — competing founding myths typically encode competing visions of the state's identity, its legitimate governing principles, and its external orientation. The pipeline needs to know which narrative is ascendant, which is being suppressed, and what political shifts would change the balance.

Identify the specific institutional channels through which the founding narrative is transmitted and reproduced: school curricula and textbook content, military training and oath language, state media programming, national holidays and commemorative calendar, museum and memorial curation, and official historical commissions. These channels matter for the pipeline because changes to them — a revised textbook, a renamed holiday, a reframed museum exhibit — are observable indicators that the founding narrative is being renegotiated.

#### 2. GEOGRAPHIC DETERMINISM & PERSISTENT STRATEGIC PROBLEMS

Analyze the physical geography as a set of permanent strategic constraints. Address: defensibility of borders (natural barriers vs. open approaches), access to maritime trade routes or landlocked dependency, chokepoint control or proximity, Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) claims and maritime boundary disputes, naval strategic geography (proximity to sea lanes, exposure to maritime interdiction, port infrastructure adequacy), fisheries where they function as a political variable or source of interstate friction, resource geography (abundance, scarcity, curse), climate zones and agricultural viability, and proximity to major powers — assessed not as a static feature but as a relationship whose strategic meaning shifts with changing power dynamics. Proximity to Russia, China, or the United States imposes different constraints depending on the major power's current posture, capability trajectory, and institutional behavior. Identify how the strategic implications of this country's geographic neighborhood have changed over the past two decades and what trajectory they are on.

Identify which strategic problems are *structurally unsolvable* given the geography — these are the problems every government of this country will face regardless of ideology or capability. Critically distinguish between problems that are genuinely imposed by physical geography and problems that are *framed as* geographic constraints but are actually institutional or governance failures. A country may attribute food insecurity to arid conditions when the binding constraint is actually water mismanagement, inequitable distribution, or failure to invest in irrigation infrastructure. Landlocked status is a genuine constraint, but its severity is mediated by the quality of institutional relationships with transit states. For each geographic problem identified, assess whether it is a hard physical constraint or a soft constraint that could be substantially mitigated by different institutional choices. This distinction matters because it determines whether the problem is permanent (every government will face it) or contingent (a sufficiently capable government could ameliorate it).

Assess which geographic constraints have been partially overcome or transformed by technology, infrastructure, or institutional arrangements — and which remain binding despite technological change. Geographic determinism is real but not absolute: the English Channel was a defensive moat until air power; pipeline technology transformed energy geography; containerization changed the relative value of different port locations; submarine cables and satellite links altered information geography. Identify where the country's current technological and infrastructural base has modified its geographic position relative to historical baselines, and where critical geographic constraints remain intractable. This analysis should be read in conjunction with Section 7 (Infrastructural Inheritance).

#### 3. IMPERIAL LEGACY & INSTITUTIONAL INHERITANCE

Who ruled this territory before the current state, and what institutional residue did they leave behind? Address: legal systems (common law vs. civil law vs. hybrid, and the specific procedural traditions inherited), administrative structures and civil service recruitment patterns, border demarcation logic, language hierarchies and educational credentialing structures, security sector models, economic integration patterns, land registration and titling systems (which often remain unreformed and continue to shape property disputes and agrarian politics), tax collection models and fiscal administrative capacity, railway gauge and transportation network orientation, and any other mundane bureaucratic or infrastructural inheritance that persists because it is invisible enough to avoid reform. The most consequential imperial legacies are often the ones embedded in administrative systems that no one thinks to question.

Explain how the specific character of the former imperial power continues to shape governance capacity, elite formation, and external orientation. Trace specific elite formation pathways: Where were the current governing class educated? What professional networks, mentorship structures, or institutional affiliations were established during or as a consequence of the imperial period? For post-Soviet states, assess whether the current elite was formed through Soviet institutional channels (Communist Party cadre systems, Soviet universities, security service careers) or through post-independence Western-oriented education — and how this generational divide shapes policy orientation and factional dynamics. For post-colonial states, assess whether the elite pathway runs through the former metropole's educational institutions or through domestic or regional alternatives, and what this means for external orientation and domestic legitimacy.

If multiple imperial legacies overlap (e.g., Ottoman then British, or Qing then Japanese), analyze how they interact and which layer is dominant in which domain. Specify the mechanism of interaction: did the later imperial power selectively adopt, selectively destroy, or hybridize the institutions of its predecessor? Which earlier institutional features survived because the later power found them useful, and which were deliberately dismantled? For post-imperial state-building, assess which layer of institutional memory the new national elite drew on when constructing state institutions — and whether that choice was deliberate or path-dependent.

Where the relevant imperial legacy is institutional *absence* rather than institutional residue — cases where the colonial or imperial power left behind negligible administrative capacity, extracted resources without building governance infrastructure, or deliberately prevented the formation of an indigenous educated class — analyze the consequences of that void. The analytical question in such cases is not "what institutions were inherited" but "what was the institutional gap at independence, how was it filled (or not), and how does the absence of inherited capacity continue to shape governance today?" This framing applies most acutely to territories subjected to particularly extractive or neglectful imperial administration, but elements of institutional absence may be relevant alongside institutional inheritance in many post-colonial contexts.

#### 4. ETHNIC, SECTARIAN & LINGUISTIC CLEAVAGE STRUCTURE

Map the primary identity cleavages — but focus on *how and when they became politically activated* rather than treating them as primordial. Explain which cleavages were hardened by colonial census-taking, imperial administrative boundaries, or deliberate divide-and-rule policies. Identify which cleavages are currently salient versus dormant, and what historical conditions have caused activation or deactivation in the past. Address how political entrepreneurs have instrumentalized these cleavages. Identify the specific institutional channels through which identity cleavages were activated and are maintained as politically salient: party systems designed around ethnic blocs, media outlets serving specific identity communities, patronage networks distributing resources along ethnic or sectarian lines, census categories that hardened previously fluid identities into fixed administrative classifications, electoral system designs that reward ethnic bloc voting, and educational systems that reproduce separate identity narratives. The mechanism of activation matters for the pipeline because it identifies the institutional levers through which cleavages could be further activated or potentially de-escalated.

Critically assess whether the country's major cleavages are **cross-cutting** or **reinforcing**. Cross-cutting cleavages — where ethnic, linguistic, religious, regional, and class divisions do not align — tend to produce complex coalition politics where no single identity axis dominates, moderating conflict. Reinforcing cleavages — where multiple identity dimensions align along the same fault line (e.g., ethnic minority X is also religiously distinct, regionally concentrated, and economically disadvantaged) — tend to produce binary, zero-sum politics with higher conflict potential. Map how the country's cleavages relate to each other, not just what they are individually. This structural feature is one of the strongest predictors of whether identity politics will be manageable or explosive.

Assess whether the state's institutional design manages or exacerbates these cleavages. Identify the specific institutional mechanisms the state employs to manage identity cleavages — federalism or devolution, consociational power-sharing arrangements, electoral system design (proportional representation, reserved seats, ethnic quotas), official language policies (monolingual, bilingual, multilingual), affirmative action or reservation systems, minority autonomy arrangements, or deliberate institutional silence on identity. Assess whether these mechanisms are functioning as designed, whether they are being circumvented or weaponized, and where the institutional framework is under strain. The pipeline needs to know not just that cleavages exist but what institutional architecture contains or channels them — and where that architecture might fail.

If the structurally dominant political cleavage in this country is **not primarily ethnic, sectarian, or linguistic** — if the main fault lines are urban-rural, class-based, regional without ethnic content, generational, or ideological — state this assessment explicitly and analyze those cleavages instead. Do not force an ethnic or sectarian framing onto a political landscape where other divisions are more structurally consequential. In such cases, explain why identity cleavages are subordinate or dormant and what conditions might activate them, while dedicating the bulk of the analysis to the cleavages that actually structure political competition.

#### 5. DEMOGRAPHIC TIDES & GENERATIONAL DYNAMICS

Analyze the country's demographic structure as a strategic variable that shapes political outcomes through specific mechanisms — not merely as background description.

Address: current age distribution (youth bulge vs. aging population), historical birth rate transitions, urbanization trajectory, and the political implications of generational cohorts with distinct formative experiences (identify the formative events that define each politically relevant cohort and how those experiences shape their political behavior).

For countries experiencing **demographic aging**, analyze the structural consequences with specificity: pension system sustainability and its fiscal trajectory, healthcare cost pressures, intergenerational fiscal transfer obligations, the political weight of elderly voting blocs in shaping policy priorities, and the degree to which aging-driven fiscal commitments constrain spending on defense, infrastructure, and foreign policy. Assess whether the political system has mechanisms for managing intergenerational distributional conflict or whether this tension is suppressed and building.

For countries experiencing a **youth bulge**, identify the specific mechanisms through which it creates political pressure: labor market absorption capacity, education-employment mismatch, housing affordability, the role of frustrated expectations among educated youth, and historical precedents for how youth demographic pressure has been channeled (into political mobilization, emigration, military recruitment, or radicalization).

**Migration as structural force:** Analyze emigration, immigration, and refugee flows not as demographic description but as forces with specific political and economic consequences. For emigration and brain drain: assess the scale and selectivity of outflows (who leaves — by age, education, region), the resulting labor market distortions, remittance dependency and its macroeconomic effects, how emigration reshapes the composition and political preferences of the remaining population, and whether emigration functions as a political safety valve (reducing domestic pressure for reform) or as a political grievance (fueling populist mobilization around national decline narratives).

**Diaspora political influence:** Analyze the diaspora as a distinct political actor, not merely as an outflow. Address: diaspora voting rights and electoral participation rates, diaspora lobbying organizations and their influence on host-country foreign policy, remittance flows as economic leverage, return migration as a source of institutional knowledge or political disruption, and the degree to which the home government actively cultivates or suppresses diaspora political engagement.

Identify the demographic "clock" — what structural pressures are building or dissipating based on population dynamics, on what timeline, and through what specific causal mechanisms they will constrain or enable government action.

#### 6. ECONOMIC STRUCTURE, FINANCIAL ARCHITECTURE & DEPENDENCY PATTERNS

What does this country produce, who buys it, and what does that do to domestic power relations? Analyze: primary economic base (rentier, manufacturing, agricultural, services), commodity dependency and Dutch Disease dynamics, the identity and leverage of key trading partners, the structure of state-business relations (oligarchic capture, state capitalism, competitive markets), informal economy scale, and the distribution mechanism for national wealth. Explain how the economic structure shapes the state-society bargain and constrains foreign policy autonomy.

For the state-business relationship specifically, go beyond categorical labels to identify **specific actors and mechanisms**. Name the dominant firms, conglomerates, or economic groups that exercise political leverage. Map the channels through which they exert influence: party financing, media ownership, revolving-door appointments between government and corporate positions, regulatory capture of specific agencies, or direct personal relationships with political leaders. Identify which policy domains each major economic actor has the capacity to influence or block. The pipeline needs to interpret policy decisions in terms of who benefits and who loses — this requires a concrete map, not a typological label.

For trade dependency, distinguish between relationships that are **structural constraints** (the dependency cannot be easily substituted due to infrastructure lock-in, specialized supply chains, or absence of alternatives) and those that are merely **large by volume** (significant trade flows that could be redirected at manageable cost). Identify specific commodity or technology dependencies that give trading partners actionable political leverage, and assess whether that leverage has been exercised historically. When the pipeline processes an article about a trade dispute or sanctions decision, it needs to know whether the dependency at issue is binding or substitutable.

For the informal economy, assess whether the formal economic structure described above is actually representative of how the economy functions. In countries where informality exceeds approximately 25% of GDP, analyze the political implications: the state's diminished capacity to tax and therefore to redistribute or invest, the relationship between informal employment and political clientelism, the role of informal markets in providing livelihoods that substitute for formal social protection, and whether informality functions as a source of regime resilience (populations less dependent on state provision are less susceptible to fiscal-crisis-driven grievance) or regime vulnerability (populations outside the formal economy are harder to monitor and control).

Additionally, analyze the **financial system architecture** as a distinct set of structural constraints: central bank independence (de jure and de facto), currency regime (free float, managed float, peg, dollarization, currency union membership), sovereign debt profile and creditor composition (domestic vs. foreign, bilateral vs. multilateral), credit rating trajectory, foreign reserve adequacy, sanctions exposure and vulnerability to financial isolation, and the degree to which the financial system is integrated into or insulated from global capital flows. Identify where financial architecture creates **binding constraints on policy autonomy** — e.g., eurozone membership eliminates monetary policy discretion, which means the country cannot devalue its way out of competitiveness problems and must instead pursue internal devaluation with all the domestic political consequences that entails; dollar-denominated debt creates vulnerability to Fed rate decisions; dependence on IMF lending programs conditions fiscal choices. For each binding constraint, trace the political consequences — not just that the constraint exists, but what domestic political dynamics it produces.

#### 7. INFRASTRUCTURAL INHERITANCE & RESOURCE PATHWAYS

Analyze the physical and digital infrastructure as materialized path dependency — the built environment that encodes historical power relationships into present-day constraints.

**Transportation infrastructure:** Analyze rail, road, and port networks with attention to political intentionality. Who built them, to connect whom, for whose benefit? Identify where colonial or imperial-era transportation networks still orient economic flows outward (toward former metropoles or extraction points) rather than inward (connecting domestic economic zones). Assess whether post-independence or post-transition infrastructure investment has succeeded in reorienting these flows or whether the inherited topology remains dominant.

**Energy infrastructure as a geopolitical variable:** Map energy dependencies with enough specificity that the pipeline can interpret energy policy events in structural context. Address: pipeline routes and the political relationships they encode (who supplies, who transits, who can be cut off); LNG terminal capacity and whether it provides genuine supply diversification or merely theoretical alternatives; electricity grid interconnections and the degree of dependence on specific neighbors for grid stability; nuclear power dependency and the supply chain implications (fuel supply, technology provider, waste management); renewable energy supply chain dependencies (critical mineral sourcing, manufacturing concentration, technology licensing); and strategic reserve adequacy. For each major energy dependency, assess whether it creates actionable political leverage for the supplier and whether the country has a credible diversification pathway.

**Digital infrastructure:** Analyze telecommunications and digital infrastructure as a distinct set of dependencies with geopolitical implications. Address: submarine cable routes and landing points (and resulting vulnerability to disruption or surveillance); data center locations and the jurisdictional implications for data sovereignty; cloud service provider dependency (AWS, Azure, and domestic or regional alternatives); telecommunications equipment supply chain decisions (Huawei/ZTE versus Western vendors) and their implications for alliance relationships; and satellite internet access as a bypass of terrestrial infrastructure constraints. Identify where digital infrastructure choices have created dependencies on specific foreign actors that constrain or enable foreign policy.

**Water management systems:** Where relevant, analyze water infrastructure (dams, irrigation systems, desalination capacity) as a structural constraint, particularly where transboundary water management creates leverage for upstream states.

**Infrastructure as a strategic reorientation indicator:** Identify major current or planned infrastructure projects that, if completed, would represent structural reorientation rather than reinforcement of existing patterns. A new port, railway, pipeline, or digital connection facing a different direction is one of the most reliable long-lead indicators of strategic realignment. Map the current infrastructure investment trajectory and assess what it reveals about the country's intended future orientation.

Flag critical infrastructure chokepoints that create leverage for external actors — points where disruption of a single piece of infrastructure would have cascading consequences for the country's economic or security posture.

#### 8. ENVIRONMENTAL & CLIMATIC STRUCTURE

Analyze the country's environmental conditions as structural constraints on governance, tracing specific causal mechanisms rather than asserting vague threat-multiplier dynamics.

**Historical climate-governance interactions:** Identify how environmental conditions have historically shaped regime legitimacy and state capacity. Address: flood management, drought cycles, agricultural viability, and natural disaster response as tests of state legitimacy. Where environmental stewardship is embedded in the state's legitimacy narrative, identify this and assess its current robustness. For each historical pattern, trace the specific mechanism: how does environmental stress translate into political pressure — through food price inflation, internal displacement, competition over water allocation, breakdown of pastoral-agricultural coexistence, or fiscal strain from disaster response?

**Transboundary environmental dependencies:** Where the country shares critical natural resources (rivers, aquifers, airsheds, fisheries) with neighbors, analyze the specific power dynamics. Identify: the upstream-downstream or resource-sharing relationship, the institutional framework managing the shared resource (treaty-based, organization-mediated, or ad hoc), the credibility and enforcement capacity of that framework, historical precedents for resource disputes and how they were resolved, and realistic escalation scenarios if the management framework fails. For countries where transboundary water or resource security is a strategic variable, this analysis should be as specific as the military geography analysis in Section 2.

**Climate change trajectory and specific vulnerability pathways:** Assess the country's exposure to climate change through specific mechanisms, not as a generic "threat multiplier." For each vulnerability, trace the causal chain to political consequences: sea-level rise threatening specific coastal infrastructure or population centers; changing precipitation patterns affecting specific agricultural regions; extreme heat events and their labor productivity and public health consequences; and climate-driven migration patterns (both internal and cross-border). Distinguish between environmental constraints that are **genuinely binding** given available technology and resources, and those that are **institutionally mediated** (manageable by a capable, well-resourced state but overwhelming for one that is not).

**Environmental policy as a foreign policy variable:** Analyze how the country's environmental position — its emissions profile, fossil fuel dependency or production, renewable energy trajectory, and climate vulnerability — shapes its stance in international climate negotiations and creates tensions with other dimensions of its foreign policy. Identify where climate policy commitments conflict with economic structure (e.g., fossil fuel exporters committed to net-zero targets) or alliance relationships (e.g., climate ambition creating friction with carbon-intensive allies or trading partners). For the pipeline, this cross-domain tension is often the source of apparently contradictory policy signals.

**Institutional capacity for environmental adaptation:** Assess the country's demonstrated capacity to manage environmental challenges — not its stated intentions but its actual track record. Does the state have effective early warning systems, disaster response infrastructure, agricultural adaptation programs, and water management capacity? Where institutional capacity is weak, environmental stress acts as a regime stress test; where it is strong, the same environmental conditions may be manageable. Identify the specific institutional gaps that would be exposed by the environmental scenarios most likely to materialize in the next decade.

#### 9. ILLICIT NETWORKS & SHADOW GOVERNANCE

Assess whether illicit economic activity and criminal networks function as **structural features of governance** rather than deviations from it.

To determine structural relevance, apply the following criteria. The illicit economy is structurally significant if **any** of the following conditions hold: (a) illicit revenue streams fund a meaningful portion of regime patronage or security sector operations; (b) criminal organizations provide governance functions (dispute resolution, employment, protection, infrastructure) in areas where the formal state is absent or weak; (c) the state-criminal relationship has reached the level of active symbiosis, where security services or senior officials participate directly in illicit operations rather than merely tolerating them; (d) illicit financial flows create transnational dependencies that materially constrain foreign policy options; or (e) the country functions as a major transit or production node in a transnational illicit supply chain whose disruption would have significant domestic political consequences. If none of these conditions hold, use the LOW structural relevance escape valve.

**Where the illicit economy is not a structurally significant feature of governance**, state this assessment explicitly and briefly (1–2 sentences explaining why), then move on. Use the format:

> **STRUCTURAL RELEVANCE: LOW.** [Brief explanation — e.g., "Estonia's illicit economy is peripheral to governance. Organized crime exists but does not provide parallel governance functions, sustain patronage networks, or meaningfully constrain foreign policy autonomy."]

**Where the illicit economy is structurally significant**, provide full analysis addressing: the scale and character of the primary illicit economy (narcotics, extractive smuggling, human trafficking, arms flows, sanctions evasion); the degree of state-criminal integration (spectrum from hostile enforcement through tolerant coexistence to active symbiosis, including cases where security services directly manage illicit operations — cite evidence for the country's placement on this spectrum rather than asserting it categorically); how illicit revenue flows sustain regime patronage networks, fund security actors, or substitute for formal taxation in ungoverned spaces; whether criminal organizations provide parallel governance functions (dispute resolution, employment, infrastructure, protection) that the formal state cannot or chooses not to provide; and how illicit networks create transnational dependencies that constrain or enable foreign policy.

Identify whether the illicit economy is a regime vulnerability (exposure to external law enforcement pressure, anti-corruption campaigns as political weapons) or a regime asset (funding streams outside formal budget scrutiny, leverage over compromised elites, informal influence channels). Assess what would happen to regime stability if the primary illicit revenue stream were suddenly disrupted.

**Elite financial exposure and foreign jurisdiction dependency:** Where political and economic elites hold significant assets in specific foreign jurisdictions — whether through licit channels, gray-zone financial arrangements, or outright money laundering — identify those jurisdictions and assess the leverage they create. London real estate, Swiss banking, Dubai property holdings, and offshore financial center structures are not merely corruption — they are structural features that constrain foreign policy alignment. A regime whose elites' wealth is concentrated in Western financial jurisdictions faces different constraints than one whose elites park assets in jurisdictions beyond Western regulatory reach. Assess the degree to which targeted financial sanctions or asset freezes against specific individuals would function as effective leverage.

**Source interpretation note for the pipeline:** Anti-corruption campaigns, selective prosecution, and investigative journalism exposing illicit activity are frequently instruments of intra-elite political competition rather than genuine reform efforts. When the pipeline processes corruption-related reporting about this country, it should assess: Is this reporting part of a genuine investigative journalism tradition, or is it instrumentalized by a political faction? Is a prosecution driven by rule-of-law institutions or by a political principal targeting rivals? Identify the specific patterns of politically motivated anti-corruption action in this country so the pipeline can contextualize corruption-related articles rather than interpreting them at face value.

#### 10. INFORMATION ECOSYSTEM & MEDIA STRUCTURE

*[Analytical frame: comparative media systems analysis (Hallin & Mancini typology as starting point) combined with operational source reliability assessment.]*

Classify the country's media system and analyze how information flows shape political reality. Address:

**Ownership and control structure:** Who owns the major media outlets (state, oligarchic, foreign, diversified private), and what editorial constraints does ownership impose? Identify the key media proprietors and their political alignments. Map the relationship between media ownership and political power — is media a tool of state control, a weapon in oligarchic competition, an instrument of partisan mobilization, or a relatively autonomous institution?

**State-media relationship:** Where does this country fall on the spectrum from full state monopoly through captured-but-nominally-independent to genuinely pluralistic? How does the state exert influence — direct censorship, licensing regimes, advertising revenue manipulation, legal harassment, informal pressure, or ownership by allied business figures? Identify whether state influence is uniform or whether different media types (broadcast, print, digital) operate under different constraint levels.

**Source reliability mapping for intelligence consumers:** For the major domestic outlets that produce reporting relevant to this country's political and foreign policy behavior, provide an operational assessment: which outlets are reliably independent, which function as government mouthpieces, which represent specific opposition or factional perspectives, and which oscillate depending on the issue? For each major outlet or outlet category, provide: (a) the outlet name and any common abbreviations used in English-language aggregation, (b) ownership structure and its political implications, (c) editorial orientation on a left-right and/or government-opposition axis, (d) specific topic domains where the outlet's coverage is reliable versus domains where it is systematically skewed, and (e) any recent ownership changes or editorial shifts that affect current reliability. The goal is a reference that allows the pipeline to encounter an article attributed to "[Outlet Name]" and immediately contextualize its likely framing without additional research.

**Digital information environment:** Assess the role of social media platforms, messaging apps, and digital-native outlets. Address: which platforms dominate political discourse (and whether this differs from global norms — e.g., Telegram in post-Soviet space, LINE in Japan/Thailand), the scale and character of state or partisan information operations, vulnerability to foreign disinformation campaigns, and whether digital platforms function as a safety valve for expression suppressed in traditional media or as an amplifier of polarization. For each dominant platform, briefly characterize how its specific architecture (e.g., encryption, anonymity features, algorithmic amplification, group/channel structure, content moderation posture) shapes the character of political discourse conducted on it. Identify whether the platform's technical features favor state surveillance, enable opposition coordination, amplify polarization, or create information silos — and whether the state has attempted to block, regulate, or co-opt the platform. Specify which platforms are monitorable through open-source methods and which operate in spaces opaque to external observation.

**Information flow under stress:** How does the information ecosystem behave during crises? Identify historical patterns: does the state impose information blackouts, does independent media surge or self-censor, do diaspora media channels become primary information sources, and how quickly does the information environment fragment along cleavage lines identified in Section 4? Anchor this analysis in at least two specific historical episodes where the information environment was disrupted or manipulated during a crisis, identifying the timeline of state actions, the specific platforms or outlets targeted, the alternative information channels that emerged, and the ultimate effect on public understanding of events. These precedents should be described with enough specificity that the pipeline can recognize analogous patterns if they begin to recur — e.g., "ISP-level throttling of [platform] within [hours/days] of [crisis type] is the established playbook and should be treated as a leading indicator of regime perception that the crisis is escalating beyond manageable levels."

#### 11. INTERNATIONAL INSTITUTIONAL COMMITMENTS & LEGAL FRAMEWORKS

*[Analytical frame: international institutional law and institutional lock-in analysis — focus on how treaty obligations and organizational memberships create binding constraints on state behavior versus serving as cheap talk.]*

Map the country's institutional architecture — the web of international commitments that constrain, enable, and shape its realistic policy options. Distinguish between **binding constraints** (commitments with meaningful enforcement mechanisms or high defection costs) and **aspirational memberships** (commitments with weak compliance mechanisms that function primarily as signaling). For each significant institutional commitment, identify the specific enforcement mechanism (or its absence): Who monitors compliance? What are the formal sanctions for non-compliance? Have those sanctions actually been imposed on any member, and with what effect? Where enforcement mechanisms exist on paper but have never been tested, assess whether they are credible deterrents or untested assumptions. The pipeline needs to know not just that a commitment exists but whether the country's decision-makers treat it as a real constraint or a formality they can navigate around.

**Multilateral institutional membership:** Identify the key organizations (UN system, regional bodies, trade organizations, security alliances, international financial institutions) and for each, assess: the degree of institutional lock-in (how costly would exit or non-compliance be?), whether the country is a norm-maker, norm-taker, or norm-contester within the institution, and where institutional commitments actively constrain domestic or foreign policy choices versus being routinely circumvented. For the country's most significant institutional memberships, disaggregate the constraint analysis by policy domain. Identify which specific domains (fiscal, monetary, trade, defense, environmental, human rights, migration, etc.) are substantively constrained by institutional obligations and which domains retain effective national autonomy despite nominal institutional commitments. Where constraints are formally binding but routinely circumvented (e.g., EU fiscal rules violated without meaningful penalty), note this gap between formal obligation and operational practice.

**Treaty obligations with operational significance:** Identify the specific treaties, agreements, and legal frameworks that create binding constraints — particularly those relevant to the country's behavior in international crises, trade disputes, or security coordination. Address: EU acquis compliance (for EU members or candidates), NATO Article 5 obligations and defense spending commitments, WTO dispute settlement exposure, bilateral defense agreements, and ICC/ICJ jurisdiction acceptance. For each, assess whether compliance is substantive or performative.

**Institutional reform posture:** Where does this country stand on reform of the institutions it belongs to? Is it invested in the current institutional architecture or actively seeking to reshape it? This is particularly relevant for middle powers navigating the tension between preserving existing multilateral institutions and adapting to shifting power dynamics. Identify specific institutional reform positions this country has championed or blocked (e.g., UN Security Council expansion, EU treaty revision, NATO burden-sharing formulas, WTO dispute settlement reform, IFI governance reform). These specific positions reveal where the country's institutional interests lie and serve as reference points when the pipeline encounters reporting on institutional reform debates.

**Defection costs and pivot constraints:** Assess the institutional costs of realignment by disaggregating cost types. If this country were to significantly shift its international orientation (e.g., moving away from Western institutional frameworks or deepening integration with alternative structures like BRICS, SCO, AIIB), analyze: (a) **economic costs** — loss of market access, credit rating implications, investment flight, trade diversion costs; (b) **security costs** — loss of alliance guarantees, intelligence-sharing access, defense technology access; (c) **political costs** — domestic constituency backlash, loss of international status and diplomatic influence; and (d) **institutional costs** — loss of voice, vote, and agenda-setting power in organizations where the country currently has influence. Identify which cost category is the binding constraint on realignment (i.e., which category of cost is so high that it effectively prevents defection even if other costs were manageable). Assess whether any institutional commitments could be abandoned at manageable cost across all four dimensions.

**Competing institutional memberships:** Where this country simultaneously participates in institutional frameworks with competing norms or objectives (e.g., EU membership alongside deepening BRICS engagement; NATO membership alongside SCO dialogue partnership; Western sanctions compliance alongside bilateral relationships with sanctioned states), analyze the specific tensions this creates. Identify which policy domains force a choice between competing institutional obligations, which domains allow parallel participation without contradiction, and how the country manages the resulting diplomatic complexity. This dual-membership dynamic is increasingly common among middle powers and is often more analytically revealing than a simple alignment classification.

#### 12. MILITARY & SECURITY SECTOR DNA

Analyze the institutional culture, economic interests, and political role of the military and security services.

**Institutional disaggregation:** Distinguish analytically between the **conventional armed forces**, **internal security and intelligence services**, and any **parallel military structures** (revolutionary guards, presidential guards, paramilitary forces, gendarmerie with internal security roles). For each, assess: institutional loyalty (to the state, the regime, the leader personally, or its own institutional interests), chain of command and degree of autonomy from civilian oversight, distinct institutional culture and recruitment base, and the relationship *between* these institutions. Where the conventional military and internal security services are institutional rivals — competing for resources, influence, or political favor — analyze that rivalry as a structural feature: does it provide a check on either institution's power, or does it create instability? Where one institution serves as a counterweight or watchdog over another (e.g., a presidential guard that exists to deter military coups), identify this function explicitly.

**Historical role and political position:** Address: historical role (guardian of the state, party instrument, autonomous actor, guarantor of ideological order), relationship to civilian governance (subordinate, coequal, dominant).

**Military economic interests:** Where the military or security services own or control significant economic assets (commercial enterprises, land holdings, pension funds, natural resource concessions, import/export operations), analyze these holdings as a structural feature in their own right: What is the estimated scale relative to the national economy? Through what mechanisms do economic interests influence institutional behavior — do they make the military a stakeholder in economic stability, a competitor to private sector firms, a source of patronage independent of civilian budget control, or a vested interest in preserving the political arrangements that protect its economic position? How do military economic interests affect the feasibility of defense reform, civilian oversight, or military downsizing? Identify whether economic assets give the military institutional autonomy from civilian government (its own revenue base) or create vulnerabilities (exposure to sanctions, anti-corruption pressure, or economic competition).

**Doctrinal orientation:** Analyze the military's doctrinal orientation with enough specificity that the pipeline can interpret defense events in structural context. Address: the primary threat the military is organized to counter (external conventional threat, internal insurgency, regime protection, or a combination); whether the force structure matches the stated doctrine (e.g., a military that claims conventional defense orientation but is primarily structured for internal security); the dominant procurement trajectory and what it reveals about actual strategic priorities; key defense partnerships and their doctrinal implications (training relationships, equipment interoperability, joint exercise patterns); and any ongoing doctrinal debates or transitions (e.g., shifting from conscript to professional force, building expeditionary capability, integrating cyber/space capabilities). When the pipeline processes an article about a procurement decision, exercise, or deployment, the dossier should provide the context needed to assess whether it represents routine doctrinal continuity or a significant shift.

**Behavior under regime stress:** Analyze the security sector's likely behavior under regime stress along a spectrum of escalation: (a) behind-the-scenes influence and lobbying for policy change, (b) public institutional statements signaling displeasure or conditional loyalty, (c) selective non-compliance with specific orders, (d) institutional neutrality (refusing to defend the regime without actively opposing it), (e) institutional defection to opposition or rival power center, and (f) direct seizure of power. For each escalation level, identify what conditions or triggers from this country's historical record have produced that behavior, and assess where the current risk lies on this spectrum. Identify the military's institutional redlines — the specific conditions (e.g., orders to fire on civilian protesters, cuts to institutional privileges, purges of the officer corps, threats to military economic interests) that historical precedent suggests would trigger movement up the escalation spectrum. Under regime stress, different security institutions may behave differently — assess which institution is most likely to defect, which is most loyal, and whether fragmentation of the security sector is itself a plausible crisis scenario.

#### 13. HISTORY OF DISSENT & CIVIL SOCIETY INFRASTRUCTURE

Calibrate the analytical emphasis to the country's governance context. For countries with established democratic institutions and robust civil society, the analysis should focus on the *institutional channels* through which organized interests influence policy (labor unions, employer associations, think tanks, advocacy organizations, professional bodies, religious institutions, and their relationships to political parties), the relative balance of power among civil society sectors, and where this institutional infrastructure is strong versus where gaps exist that could become significant under stress. For countries with constrained or repressed civil society, the analysis should focus on how dissent organizes despite constraints, what institutions serve as refuges for opposition, and where organizational capacity for mobilization resides. The appropriate analytical frame depends on the country — do not impose a revolutionary-incubation frame on countries where civil society operates through institutional channels, and do not impose an interest-group-pluralism frame on countries where civil society operates underground or in exile.

Map the historical infrastructure of opposition and non-state governance. Address: which institutions historically absorb dissent when the state fails or represses (religious institutions, labor unions, tribal structures, universities, professional associations, diaspora networks, criminal organizations), the dominant *form* of historical dissent (mass mobilization, armed insurgency, legal opposition, exile politics), and how the character of dissent movements has shaped post-crisis governance.

Critically, distinguish between the **historical infrastructure of dissent** and its **current operational capacity**. Institutions that channeled opposition in previous decades may have been co-opted, defunded, repressed, or rendered irrelevant by generational change, urbanization, or digital transformation. Assess the current organizational capacity of each major dissent-absorbing institution: Does it retain membership, funding, leadership, and mobilization capability? Has its social base shifted? Has a new generation of organizational infrastructure (digital civil society, social media-coordinated movements, informal networks) emerged that may supplant traditional institutions as vehicles for dissent? For pipeline purposes, the critical question is: if a political crisis erupts in the next 6-12 months, what specific organizational infrastructure exists to channel dissent, who controls it, how quickly can it mobilize, and what are its communication channels?

**State civil society management strategy:** Analyze the state's approach along the full spectrum from repression through co-optation to accommodation. Where the regime manages opposition through co-optation mechanisms — creating government-organized NGOs (GONGOs), absorbing opposition leaders into patronage networks, permitting managed opposition parties that channel dissent into controlled outlets, or allowing civil society activity in non-threatening domains (humanitarian, cultural) while repressing it in politically sensitive ones (human rights, anti-corruption, electoral monitoring) — map these mechanisms with specificity. Co-optation is often more analytically important than repression because it is less visible, more durable, and harder for external observers to detect. Identify which opposition figures or organizations have been co-opted and which remain genuinely independent — this distinction is critical for the pipeline's interpretation of political events involving named opposition actors.

**Transnational civil society connections:** Analyze the role of international NGO networks, foreign-funded democracy promotion organizations, and diaspora-based organizations in supporting or shaping domestic civil society. Assess whether the state has enacted "foreign agent" laws or restrictions on international funding that constrain these connections; whether transnational advocacy networks (human rights, environmental, anti-corruption) provide domestic organizations with international visibility, resources, or protection; and whether exile communities or diaspora organizations function as alternative political spaces when domestic civil society is repressed. Assess whether transnational connections strengthen civil society's capacity or create vulnerabilities (delegitimization through association with foreign interests, dependence on external funding priorities, or exposure to foreign agent legislation).

Identify who currently provides parallel governance functions and where the next revolution (or counter-revolution) would likely be incubated.

#### 14. PATRON-CLIENT HISTORY & ALLIANCE GENEALOGY

Trace the country's historical alignment choices and external dependencies. Address: Cold War positioning and its residual effects, shifts in patronage and what triggered them, current security guarantees and their credibility, economic dependency relationships that constrain diplomatic autonomy, and diaspora or ethnic linkages that create transnational alignment pressures. Identify the country's realistic pivot options — who could they realign toward, at what cost, and what historical precedent exists for such a shift.

Assess whether this country's alignment posture is best characterized as **patron-client dependency** (primarily reliant on a single external power), **firm alliance commitment** (embedded in an alliance structure with genuine mutual obligations), or **multi-vector hedging** (deliberately maintaining relationships with competing powers to maximize autonomy and bargaining leverage). For countries pursuing multi-vector strategies, do not force-fit a patron-client framework — instead, analyze: which relationships serve which functional needs (security from one partner, economic integration with another, diplomatic support from a third); where the multi-vector strategy creates contradictions the country must manage (e.g., purchasing defense systems from one power while seeking security guarantees from its rival); what the limits of hedging are (at what point do competing partners force a choice); and whether the multi-vector posture is a stable equilibrium or a transitional phase being driven toward resolution by external pressure. Countries that have made multi-vector positioning into a deliberate grand strategy should be analyzed as pursuing a strategy with its own logic rather than as exhibiting indecision or unreliability.

**Domestic political economy of alignment:** Identify which domestic constituencies (political parties, business sectors, military/security establishments, ethnic or diaspora communities, ideological movements) are invested in the current alignment structure and which advocate for or would benefit from realignment. Assess the domestic political costs a government would incur by shifting alignment — not just the international strategic costs, but the domestic coalition costs: Would a pivot split the governing coalition? Would it mobilize opposition from economically exposed sectors (firms dependent on current trade relationships, workers in alliance-dependent industries)? Would it activate identity-based resistance (populations with historical grievances against the proposed new patron)? The pipeline needs to understand that alignment shifts are domestically contested events, not frictionless strategic recalculations — and that domestic political constraints may prevent strategically rational realignments or force strategically irrational ones.

Specifically address this country's **structural relationship to American power** by disaggregating the dependency into its component dimensions: (a) **security dependency** — reliance on US security guarantees, military equipment and interoperability, defense technology access, intelligence sharing, and the credibility of US commitments given current signals; (b) **economic dependency** — exposure to US trade policy (tariffs, sanctions, market access), dollar-denominated financial system integration, technology supply chain dependencies (semiconductors, cloud infrastructure, AI), and investment flows; (c) **institutional dependency** — reliance on US backing within international institutions (UN Security Council, IFIs, trade organizations) and vulnerability to US withdrawal from or undermining of those institutions; and (d) **diplomatic dependency** — reliance on US mediation, diplomatic cover, or political support for this country's positions on contested issues. For each dimension, assess the country's vulnerability to withdrawal, hostility, or indifference from the United States — these are analytically distinct scenarios with different implications. Where US withdrawal in one dimension creates a vacuum, identify which actors (regional powers, institutional frameworks, bilateral partnerships) could partially substitute and at what cost in each dimension separately. This disaggregated assessment is the defining structural context for middle power behavior in the current period.

For each potential pivot option, apply a **feasibility test**: Does the alternative patron or framework actually possess the capability to deliver what the country needs? Is the alternative willing to provide it, and at what price? Does the institutional infrastructure exist to operationalize the pivot, or would it need to be built from scratch over what timeline? What historical precedent exists for successful pivots of this kind, and what precedent exists for attempted pivots that failed? Distinguish between pivot options that are debated in policy circles and pivot options that survive rigorous feasibility analysis — the pipeline needs to know which realignment scenarios are structurally plausible and which are aspirational rhetoric.

#### 15. CONSTITUTIONAL CRISES & REGIME TRANSITION PATTERNS

Analyze how power has actually transferred in this country historically. Identify the dominant pattern: elections, coups, dynastic succession, negotiated pacts, revolution, or state collapse. Look for cyclical patterns and their periodicity. Address: what typically triggers regime crisis, what institutions or actors serve as stabilizers or accelerants, whether democratic experiments have been sustained or repeatedly interrupted (and why), and the typical duration of political cycles. Assess the current regime's position within the historical pattern.

For each identified cyclical pattern, specify: (a) the structural tension that accumulates during the stable phase (e.g., fiscal imbalance, elite factional competition, popular legitimacy erosion), (b) the typical trigger that converts latent tension into acute crisis (distinguish between endogenous triggers like succession events and exogenous triggers like economic shocks or external pressure), (c) the institutional mechanisms that determine whether crisis produces managed transition, democratic deepening, authoritarian consolidation, or state failure, and (d) the observable preconditions that indicate the current regime is approaching a phase transition versus operating within the stable range of the current cycle. Be explicit about timeline: are the structural tensions that drive this country's historical cycle currently building, at peak, or recently discharged?

**Democratic erosion and consolidation:** For countries with democratic or semi-democratic systems, dedicate specific attention to the mechanisms and trajectory of democratic erosion or consolidation. Democratic backsliding in the current period rarely proceeds through overt regime change — it advances through incremental institutional capture: executive aggrandizement (concentrating power through legal or quasi-legal means), judicial politicization (court-packing, selective prosecution, constitutional tribunal capture), media environment degradation (ownership concentration by allied oligarchs, defunding of public media, legal harassment of independent outlets), electoral manipulation (gerrymandering, voter suppression, changes to electoral administration), restriction of civil society operating space (foreign agent laws, NGO registration requirements, protest restrictions), and weakening of horizontal accountability institutions (anti-corruption agencies, ombudsmen, audit offices). For each mechanism relevant to this country, assess: (a) whether it has been deployed, is being deployed, or could plausibly be deployed given the current institutional configuration, (b) what institutional safeguards exist to resist it and how robust those safeguards have proven under pressure, and (c) what stage of erosion the country has reached on that dimension. This analysis should enable the pipeline to interpret incoming events — such as a judicial appointment, a media ownership change, or a civil society restriction — as potential steps in a backsliding sequence rather than isolated events.

Within the analysis of the current regime, dedicate specific attention to the **decision-making architecture and leadership environment**. Address: the degree of decision-making centralization (does policy emerge from institutional process or from the principal's inner circle?); the quality and diversity of information reaching the top decision-maker (is there institutional capacity for dissent or bad-news delivery, or has the system optimized for telling the leader what they want to hear?); the composition and influence dynamics of the inner circle (identify key advisors, gatekeepers, and rival factions within the leadership's immediate environment where this is knowable); the leader's **demonstrated crisis behavior** based on historical precedent (does the system centralize further under pressure, fragment, or escalate externally to deflect domestic stress?); and personal factors with strategic relevance where credibly reported — health, financial exposure to specific foreign jurisdictions, and risk appetite as revealed by past decisions rather than rhetoric.

**Succession dynamics:** Whether or not a transition appears imminent, analyze the structural features that will shape the next leadership transition. Address: the formal constitutional or institutional mechanism for succession (and whether it has ever been tested); whether there is an identified successor, a contested succession among multiple claimants, or no visible succession planning at all; the factional alignments of potential successors and whether transition would likely preserve or disrupt the current power configuration; whether the military and security services have historically played kingmaker roles during transitions; and the realistic scenarios for transition (orderly institutional transfer, contested but contained competition, chaotic fragmentation). Assess whether the current leader has built a system that can survive their departure or one that depends on their personal position for coherence. Mark this analysis with appropriate confidence levels — succession dynamics are inherently uncertain, and the analysis should distinguish between what the institutional structure predicts and what remains genuinely unknowable.

**[CALIBRATION NOTE]:** Assess leadership dynamics only on the basis of observable behavior, institutional structure, and credible reporting. Flag confidence levels explicitly. Do not engage in speculative psychological profiling — the goal is to map the *decision-making system*, not to psychoanalyze the individual. Organize your assessment into two tiers: (1) **Institutional observables** — features of the decision-making architecture that can be assessed from organizational charts, legal authorities, and publicly documented processes (these are relatively stable and carry higher confidence), and (2) **Behavioral inferences** — conclusions drawn from the leader's demonstrated pattern of decisions under specific conditions (these carry inherent uncertainty and should be explicitly marked **[ASSESSED]** with reference to the specific historical episodes from which the inference is drawn). When citing a behavioral pattern, identify at least two distinct episodes that support the inference — a single data point does not establish a pattern. Where information is opaque (as it often is for authoritarian inner circles), state what is unknown and identify what observable indicators would help resolve the uncertainty.

#### 16. COLLECTIVE MEMORY OF HUMILIATION & GRIEVANCE

Identify the emotional substrate beneath rational strategy. What historical events function as national trauma or sacred narrative — events whose invocation can mobilize populations, justify policy, or establish political redlines? Address: how these narratives are maintained (education, media, commemorations, political rhetoric), whether they are genuinely popular or elite-manufactured, how they map onto current geopolitical disputes, and where Western or external analytical frameworks systematically misread these redlines because they discount the grievance narrative as irrational.

For each major grievance or sacred narrative identified, assess its **constraint strength** — the degree to which it genuinely limits leadership freedom of action on associated policy issues, as distinct from its rhetorical prominence. Apply this test: if the current leadership attempted to make a significant concession on the issue associated with this narrative, what would the domestic political cost be? Would it be regime-threatening, politically costly but survivable, or manageable with appropriate framing? Provide evidence for this assessment by identifying historical instances where leaders have either successfully defied the narrative (revealing its constraint as weaker than assumed) or been punished for perceived violations (revealing it as genuinely binding). Distinguish between narratives that are deeply embedded in popular consciousness through generational transmission and those that require active elite maintenance to remain salient — the former constrain all governments regardless of ideology; the latter constrain only as long as the maintaining faction holds power.

For each identified misreading by Western analytical frameworks, provide: (a) the specific grievance issue, (b) the typical Western analytical interpretation, (c) why this interpretation is wrong or incomplete, and (d) the corrective analytical frame the pipeline should apply instead. The goal is not to assert that all grievance-driven behavior is irrational, but to identify the specific issues on which the standard cost-benefit calculus misestimates the costs because it fails to account for identity and historical memory as real political variables.

Where multiple competing grievance or sacred narratives exist within the same country, map the competition explicitly. Identify: which political factions, demographic groups, or institutional actors champion each narrative; what is at stake politically in the contest between narratives (control of national identity typically entails control of foreign policy orientation, alliance choices, and domestic institutional design); whether the balance between competing narratives has shifted over the past decade and what drove the shift; and what observable indicators would signal a further shift (e.g., changes to school curricula, museum exhibitions, national commemorative calendars, or public monument decisions). For the pipeline, competing narratives mean that the same external event may be interpreted in diametrically opposed ways by different domestic audiences — the pipeline should be calibrated to identify which narrative frame is being activated by which actors in response to incoming events.

Conclude with an explicit assessment of how the identified grievance narratives shape this country's **current geopolitical positioning and alliance behavior**. Specifically address: Does the dominant grievance narrative reinforce or undermine commitment to the country's current alliance and institutional framework? How do grievance narratives affect the country's threat perception hierarchy — which threats does the population perceive as existential versus manageable, and how does this diverge from the threat assessments of allies? Are there geopolitical issues on which the grievance narrative creates a domestic political constraint that allies consistently underestimate? Identify the specific policy domains where historical memory is most likely to override strategic calculation and explain the mechanism.

#### 17. CROSS-FACET INTERSECTION ANALYSIS

This is the most critical section. Identify **at least five** significant intersections between the facets above — places where two or more structural forces interact to produce outcomes that no single facet would predict. For each intersection:

- Name the interacting facets. At least two of the five intersections must connect facets that are **separated by at least four sections in the dossier structure** (e.g., an intersection connecting Section 2 with Section 12, or Section 5 with Section 16). This structural requirement forces cross-domain analysis that would not emerge from domain-specialist review of adjacent topics.
- Explain the mechanism of interaction
- Provide a concrete historical or contemporary example from this country
- State the analytical implication as an **operational instruction to the pipeline**, using the following format: "When the pipeline encounters reporting on [specific, concrete event type], it should assess whether [intersection-driven dynamic] is at work rather than interpreting the event at face value as [the surface-level or single-domain interpretation that a naive reader would apply]. The distinguishing indicator is [specific observable feature that differentiates the intersection-driven explanation from the surface interpretation]."

**Prioritize non-obvious intersections over self-evident ones.** A self-evident intersection is one where the connection between facets is widely recognized and routinely analyzed (e.g., "geography affects military strategy" or "economic inequality fuels political instability"). A non-obvious intersection is one where the connection operates through a mechanism that is not immediately apparent and that analysts covering only one domain would miss. To test whether an intersection is non-obvious, ask: would a specialist in Facet A and a specialist in Facet B independently predict this interaction, or does it only become visible when both facets are analyzed jointly?

**Example of target analytical depth:** "The specific character of French colonial-era land registration in [country] (Section 3) interacts with post-independence ethnic identity politicization (Section 4) through property dispute mechanisms: colonial land titles were issued to individuals from the administratively favored ethnic group, creating a durable map of land ownership that tracks ethnic lines. Each identified intersection should reach this level of mechanistic specificity.

#### 18. KEY ANALYTICAL JUDGMENTS

Provide 5–7 summary judgments about this country's structural posture. These should be:
- Falsifiable (the pipeline LLM can check them against future events)
- Structurally grounded (derived from the facets above, not from current news). Apply this test: would this judgment remain valid if the specific individuals currently in power were replaced by different individuals from the same structural position? If the judgment depends on the specific choices or personality of the current leader rather than on structural constraints that any leader in that position would face, it belongs in a current intelligence assessment, not in a structural dossier. - Temporally bounded where appropriate (distinguish permanent constraints from generational pressures)
- Honest about uncertainty (use confidence markers)

**Format each judgment as follows:** State the claim, the structural basis (referencing specific sections), the conditions under which it would be falsified, and the confidence level with its evidential basis. Example:

### Stylistic Instructions

- Write in analytical prose. No bullet points within sections except in Sections 0, 18, 19, and 20.
- Favor causal explanation over description. "X happened" is less valuable than "X happened *because* Y, which means Z for today."
- Be specific. Name dates, figures, percentages, and treaty names where they strengthen the argument. Avoid vague hedging when you have evidence.
- Assume the consuming LLM has no prior regional expertise loaded in context. Provide enough context that references are self-contained — a name, date, or event mentioned without explanation is useless.
- When the historical record is contested or the analytical inference is uncertain, say so explicitly rather than presenting interpretation as settled fact.
- Do not editorialize about whether the country's behavior is good or bad. Explain the logic of its behavior from its own structural position.
- Where a concept has a direct parallel in another country the reader might know better, a brief comparative aside (one sentence) can accelerate understanding — but do not overuse this technique.
- Every section must conclude with a `STRUCTURAL CLAIMS` block of numbered, discrete, falsifiable assertions.
- Target length: 8,000–12,000 words for the initial seed dossier.
