# Seventeen Thousand Islands, No Anchor

Indonesia is the world's fourth most populous nation, a sprawling archipelago of 17,380 islands that controls four of the nine major maritime chokepoints on which global trade depends. Under President Prabowo Subianto, a former special forces commander who lost two presidential bids before winning in 2024, the country is undergoing its deepest remilitarization since the Suharto era. A governing coalition that holds 81% of parliament has neutralized institutional opposition, co-opted the two largest Islamic mass organizations, and placed roughly \$570 billion in state assets under a politically managed sovereign wealth fund. Indonesia's nickel gambit has made it indispensable to the global battery supply chain, but at the cost of acute dependence on Chinese capital and demand. Prabowo's "omnidirectional diplomacy" — joining BRICS while applying for OECD membership, buying French fighters and Turkish jets while hosting the largest-ever joint exercise with the United States — is the physical expression of a hedging strategy that American retrenchment has made existentially necessary. Five major protest waves in 2025, including the deadliest since 1998, reveal the fragility beneath the surface.

## I. Key actors and institutions primer

This section maps Indonesia's power structure as of March 2026, distinguishing formal mandates from actual political functions. Prabowo Subianto's presidency represents the deepest remilitarization of Indonesian governance since the New Order, operating through a coalition so dominant it has effectively eliminated institutional opposition.

<strong>The presidency</strong>

- **President Prabowo Subianto** (b. 1951, inaugurated 20 October 2024). Former lieutenant general, Kopassus commander (1995–98), dismissed from the military in 1998 for involvement in the kidnapping of pro-democracy activists. Lost presidential bids in 2014 and 2019, served as Jokowi's Defense Minister (2019–2024), won 2024 election with \~58% of the vote (\~96.2 million votes). Oldest first-term president in post-independence history at 73. Governing style is military-oriented: held cabinet retreat at the Military Academy in Magelang, appointed the largest cabinet in post-Reformasi history (48 ministers, 54 deputy ministers). Signature programs include free nutritious meals for children, food self-sufficiency, and the **Danantara** sovereign wealth fund (launched 24 February 2025, targeting US\$900 billion in managed assets). Aims for 8% GDP growth (actual \~5%). [HIGH CONFIDENCE]
- **Vice President Gibran Rakabuming Raka** (b. 1987). Youngest-ever VP at 37. Son of former President Joko Widodo. His candidacy was enabled by a controversial Constitutional Court ruling (Decision No. 90/PUU-XXI/2023), in which Chief Justice Anwar Usman—Jokowi's brother-in-law—cast the deciding vote to create an age exception. Anwar was subsequently found guilty of serious ethical violations. Gibran has limited independent political base; his position is regime-specific, dependent on the Prabowo-Jokowi alliance.
- **Jokowi's ongoing influence** [ASSESSED]: Former president retains substantial power through his son (VP), son-in-law Bobby Nasution (Mayor of Medan), loyalists in cabinet (Bahlil Lahadalia, Tito Karnavian, Pratikno), the TNI Commander (Gen. Agus Subiyanto, a Jokowi appointee), and the Police Chief (Gen. Listyo Sigit Prabowo, also a Jokowi appointee). Prabowo's September 2025 cabinet reshuffle—removing Sri Mulyani and Budi Gunawan—signals gradual consolidation away from Jokowi networks.

<strong>Legislature and judiciary</strong>

- **DPR (House of Representatives)**: 580 seats. 2024 results: PDI-P 110, Golkar 102, Gerindra 86, NasDem 69, PKB 68, PKS 53, PAN 48, Demokrat 44. PPP failed the 4% threshold for the first time since 1977. Speaker: Puan Maharani (PDI-P, daughter of Megawati).
- **Constitutional Court (Mahkamah Konstitusi)**: Formally the guardian of the constitution, but institutional credibility deeply damaged by the Gibran candidacy ruling. Increasingly perceived as politically captured. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]
- **Supreme Court (Mahkamah Agung)**: Historically plagued by corruption. Less politically prominent than the MK in recent years.

<strong>Political parties and coalition structure</strong>

The **Koalisi Indonesia Maju Plus (KIM Plus)** governing coalition controls \~470 of 580 DPR seats (\~81%), creating an effective political cartel with minimal institutional opposition. Prabowo has proposed making it permanent.

- **PDI-P** (110 seats) — Chairman: Megawati Sukarnoputri. Secular nationalist. The **only party outside the coalition**, calling itself a "balancer" (penyeimbang). Weakened by Jokowi's defection and the February 2025 arrest of Secretary-General Hasto Kristiyanto by KPK on bribery charges widely seen as politically motivated. *Institutionally stable; currently regime-opposed.*
- **Golkar** (102 seats) — Power held by Airlangga Hartarto (Coordinating Minister for Economic Affairs). Centrist, pragmatic, New Order legacy party. *Institutionally stable; perennial coalition partner.*
- **Gerindra** (86 seats) — Prabowo's personal vehicle, founded 2008. Nationalist-populist. *Regime-specific.*
- **PKB** (68 seats) — Chairman: Muhaimin Iskandar. Linked to Nahdlatul Ulama. *Institutionally grounded in NU networks.*
- **NasDem** (69 seats) — Chairman: Surya Paloh (media mogul). Personal vehicle. *Regime-adjacent.*
- **PKS** (53 seats) — Islamist, Muslim Brotherhood-influenced. Historically oppositional but joined KIM Plus post-election, representing erosion of checks and balances. *Institutionally stable; ideologically flexible in practice.*
- **PAN** (48 seats) — Linked to Muhammadiyah. Chairman Zulkifli Hasan serves as Coordinating Minister. *Institutionally grounded in Muhammadiyah networks.*
- **Demokrat** (44 seats) — Chairman: Agus Harimurti Yudhoyono (AHY), son of former President SBY. AHY holds Coordinating Minister for Infrastructure. *Dynastic vehicle.*

<strong>Security actors</strong>

- **TNI Commander**: General Agus Subiyanto (Jokowi appointee, retained by Prabowo). Conducted 300+ officer rotations in late 2024.
- **TNI structure**: Army (TNI-AD) is the largest and most politically influential branch. Navy (TNI-AL) and Air Force (TNI-AU) are chronically underfunded relative to the archipelagic state's maritime defense needs. Key special forces: **Kopassus** (Army, Prabowo's former unit—many cabinet members have Kopassus backgrounds), **Kopaskhas** (Air Force), **Densus 88** (counter-terrorism unit under POLRI, formed 2003 with US/Australian assistance).
- **BIN** (State Intelligence Agency): Head is Lt. Gen. (Ret.) Muhammad Herindra, former Kopassus commander. Formally strategic intelligence; functions as political instrument.
- **POLRI** (National Police): Chief is Gen. Listyo Sigit Prabowo (Jokowi appointee). \~400,000+ personnel. Separated from military in 1999.
- **New TNI Law (2025)**: Controversial amendments allow active military officers to hold civilian positions, reversing post-Reformasi civilian supremacy norms. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

<strong>Economic actors</strong>

- **Hartono brothers** (Djarum Group/BCA): Combined net worth \~\$48 billion, Indonesia's wealthiest. Control Bank Central Asia, Indonesia's largest private bank.
- **Anthony Salim** (Salim Group): Indofood (Indomie), Indomaret (19,280+ stores), palm oil. Net worth \~\$7.3 billion.
- **Sinar Mas** (Widjaja family): Paper/pulp (APP), palm oil, real estate. Net worth \~\$10.8 billion.
- **Oligarchic-political nexus**: 45% of 2019-2024 DPR legislators were businesspeople. Jeffrey Winters's "Material Power Index" shows the wealth gap between Indonesia's top 40 oligarchs and average citizens exceeds **630,000 to 1**.[^1] Key bridge figures: Hashim Djojohadikusumo (Prabowo's brother), Erick Thohir (media conglomerate), Rosan Roeslani (ran Prabowo's campaign). Chinese-Indonesian conglomerates (\~3% of population, estimated to control \~70% of private economy) maintain connections across administrations but avoid direct political office.
- **Danantara** (Daya Anagata Nusantara): Sovereign wealth fund launched February 2025, absorbing the "Magnificent Seven" SOEs: Bank Mandiri, BRI, BNI, Pertamina, PLN, Telkom, MIND ID. Combined assets \~Rp 8.9 quadrillion (\~\$570 billion). Managed by political appointees; governance concerns are acute.

<strong>Religious authorities</strong>

- **Nahdlatul Ulama (NU)**: Largest Islamic organization globally (\~80–120 million affiliates). Chairman: Yahya Cholil Staquf (Gus Yahya). Traditionalist Sunni, tolerant, Sufi-influenced. Currently in severe internal crisis over a government-granted 26,000-hectare coal mining concession in East Kalimantan; Supreme Council dismissed Gus Yahya in December 2025. Linked to PKB. *Institutionally massive but internally fractured.*
- **Muhammadiyah**: \~60 million affiliates. Chairman: Prof. Haedar Nashir. Modernist/reformist, operates 120+ universities and hospitals. Maintains formal distance from politics. *Institutionally stable.*
- **MUI** (Indonesian Ulema Council): Semi-governmental, created 1975 under Suharto. Issues fatwas; functions as regime-legitimizing body rather than independent religious authority. *Regime-aligned.*

<strong>Civil society</strong>

- **KPK** (Corruption Eradication Commission): Established 2003, systematically weakened by the 2019 KPK Law revision (Law 19/2019) which reclassified it as an executive agency, created a Supervisory Board requiring pre-approval for wiretaps, and reclassified employees as civil servants (75 failed a "nationalism test"; 51 senior investigators fired). Under Prabowo, KPK has been weaponized against PDI-P (arrest of Hasto Kristiyanto). *Institutionally captured.* [HIGH CONFIDENCE]
- **KontraS, ICW, LBH**: Active but under increasing pressure. Student protests (February 2025 "Indonesia Gelap" movement; August 2025 mass protests) show persistent mobilization capacity, but organizations face legal harassment and co-optation.

The concentration of roughly 81% of parliamentary seats under the governing coalition has produced the weakest institutional opposition in the post-Reformasi era, while the presidency itself has become the most militarized civilian government since the New Order, with former military and police officers occupying more cabinet positions than at any point since 1998. The KPK's independence has been structurally destroyed rather than merely weakened, transforming it from a corruption watchdog into a prosecutorial instrument available to the executive — as the politically charged arrest of PDI-P's Hasto Kristiyanto illustrates. Jokowi retains residual influence over security appointments through loyalists at the helm of the TNI and POLRI, but this leverage is decaying as Prabowo consolidates power through reshuffles. Underlying all of these dynamics, the oligarchic structure of Indonesian politics has deepened rather than moderated under democratic governance, with the Danantara sovereign wealth fund placing roughly $570 billion in state assets under political management with minimal independent oversight — concentrating economic and political power to a degree unseen since the New Order itself.

## II. Formation trauma and founding mythology

Indonesia's national identity rests on multiple layered traumas, each generating competing narratives that remain politically live. The state deploys a carefully curated mythology through institutional channels, but this mythology contains internal contradictions that resurface in every political generation. Understanding which narrative a political actor invokes reveals their coalition logic.

<strong>Independence as sacred origin and the anti-colonial reflex</strong>

The proclamation of 17 August 1945 is Indonesia's foundational sacred event. Sukarno read the declaration at 10:00 AM at Jalan Pegangsaan Timur 56 in Jakarta, two days after Japan's surrender, after radical *pemuda* (youth groups) kidnapped him and Hatta to force a declaration before the Dutch could return. The subsequent four-year war against the Netherlands—in which the Dutch launched two military offensives euphemistically called "police actions" (*Agresi Militer* I and II, in 1947 and 1948)—cemented foreign military intervention as the foundational threat in Indonesian strategic consciousness.[^2]

US pressure proved decisive in forcing Dutch withdrawal: the threat to withhold Marshall Plan funds compelled the Netherlands to the Round Table Conference (August–November 1949), with formal sovereignty transfer on 27 December 1949. This creates an ambivalent American presence in Indonesian founding memory—the US helped secure independence, but Indonesian elites remember that this assistance was strategic, not principled. The "350 years of colonization" narrative (dating from VOC arrival circa 1600) generates deep structural suspicion of foreign powers that shapes the *bebas-aktif* (free and active) foreign policy doctrine. Indonesian airports are named after anti-Dutch heroes; every August 17 ceremony renews the anti-colonial commitment.

<strong>Pancasila as both glue and cage</strong>

Pancasila—the five principles articulated in Sukarno's 1 June 1945 BPUPK speech (belief in God, just humanity, national unity, deliberative democracy, social justice)—is simultaneously a genuine pluralist compromise and a political control mechanism. Its genius lies in preventing Indonesia from becoming an Islamic state while acknowledging monotheism, holding together a nation of 700+ languages and six recognized religions.[^3]

Under Suharto's New Order, Pancasila became an instrument of containment. The P4 program (1978) mandated 40+ hours of ideological indoctrination for all civil servants and students, emphasizing rote memorization of 36 normative precepts. In 1983, Pancasila became the mandatory "sole basis" (*asas tunggal*) for all organizations—critics were labeled "anti-Pancasila" and suppressed. Post-Reformasi, P4 was formally revoked (1998), but Pancasila education returned under Jokowi through BPIP (Pancasila Ideology Development Agency, 2018), headed by Megawati Sukarnoputri. The current Merdeka Curriculum incorporates a "Pancasila Student Profile" framework. The pattern is consistent: each regime instrumentalizes Pancasila to discipline the political spectrum while claiming to honor the founding compromise.

<strong>The Jakarta Charter wound</strong>

The original 22 June 1945 draft preamble—the Jakarta Charter (*Piagam Jakarta*)—included seven words obligating Muslims to follow sharia law. These words were removed on 18 August 1945, reportedly under pressure from Christian representatives of eastern Indonesia (notably Johannes Latuharhary of Ambon) who threatened secession if the formula remained. This removal functions as a **foundational wound in Islamic political memory**. Every generation of Islamist politicians has attempted to reinstate the seven words—in 1959, 1968, 2000, and 2002—and every attempt has been defeated. Aceh's special autonomy with sharia implementation represents a localized compromise. The Jakarta Charter debate is the original fault line that produces every subsequent secular-Islamic political contest. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

<strong>The 1965 black hole</strong>

The September 30th Movement, the murder of six army generals, and the subsequent mass killings of 500,000 to over 1 million people remain Indonesia's central unresolved trauma. The CIA privately called it "one of the worst mass murders of the 20th century."[^4] The army organized civilian groups—including NU's Ansor youth wing—to carry out killings targeting PKI members, ethnic Chinese, trade unionists, and feminists. Western governments, including the US and UK, provided material support and lists of suspected communists.

The New Order's official narrative—propagated through the annual broadcast of the *Pengkhianatan G30S/PKI* film (1984) and the Pancasila Sakti monument at Lubang Buaya (managed by the TNI)—cast the military as national savior and the PKI as barbaric traitors. This narrative remains politically dominant. School textbooks continue to reproduce it. In 2017, Jokowi attended a public screening and declared "don't let PKI cruelty happen again." The 2012 documentary *The Act of Killing* and the 2015 International People's Tribunal in The Hague have created counter-narratives, but the 2016 national symposium—Indonesia's first official discussion—was immediately constrained: Coordinating Minister Luhut Panjaitan opened it by ruling out any official apology. The election of Prabowo—implicated in 1998 disappearances—as president in 2024 represents what scholars call a "critical juncture" testing whether democratic substance can survive democratic procedure.

<strong>Reformasi as second founding</strong>

The 1998 Asian Financial Crisis, which collapsed the rupiah from Rp 2,600/USD to Rp 15,000/USD and contracted GDP by 13.1%, triggered student protests, the Trisakti shootings (12 May 1998, four students killed), and the May 1998 riots (over 1,000 killed, at least 168 cases of rape targeting ethnic Chinese women). Suharto resigned on 21 May 1998. The subsequent constitutional amendments (1999–2002) effectively rewrote the constitution—from 71 articles and 1,393 words to 199 articles and 4,559 words—establishing presidential term limits, direct elections, the Constitutional Court, and human rights provisions.[^5] Critically, the Preamble containing Pancasila was preserved unchanged, maintaining the founding compromise.

Indonesia's founding traumas continue to exert gravitational force on contemporary politics. The "350 years" anti-colonial narrative generates a suspicion of foreign powers that cuts across the ideological spectrum, constraining any government from appearing subordinate to external actors whether Western or Chinese. Pancasila, meanwhile, has proven endlessly adaptable as a legitimation instrument — each successive regime repurposes it to discipline opponents while claiming continuity with the founding compromise, and any actor labeled "anti-Pancasila" faces existential political risk. The 1965 massacres remain the most consequential unresolved trauma: the TNI's institutional identity depends on the New Order narrative of those events, making any serious truth-and-reconciliation process a threat to military legitimacy and therefore extremely unlikely under a president with Prabowo's background. The Jakarta Charter debate, in turn, is the generative fault line behind every secular-Islamic political contest, irresolvable because settling it in either direction would fracture the founding coalition. Reformasi built the institutional architecture of democracy — elections, the Constitutional Court, press freedom, decentralization — but left untouched the underlying power structures of oligarchy, military influence, and historical violence. Prabowo's presidency now tests whether those institutions can genuinely constrain power or whether they merely decorate it.

## III. Geographic determinism and persistent strategic problems

Indonesia's geography is its most immutable structural feature: **17,380 islands spanning 5,120 kilometers east-to-west across three time zones**, with a maritime domain of 5.8 million square kilometers encompassing four of the world's nine major maritime chokepoints.[^6] This geography creates three permanent strategic problems that no government can escape: controlling the archipelago internally, defending it externally, and leveraging its chokepoint position internationally.

<strong>The chokepoint premium</strong>

The Strait of Malacca carries about **25–30% of global maritime trade** (\~\$3.5 trillion annually), including 80% of China's oil imports and 40% of Japan's maritime trade.[^7] Indonesia also controls the Sunda Strait (connecting the Java Sea to the Indian Ocean), the Lombok Strait (deeper alternative accommodating supertankers), and the Makassar Strait (critical north-south route between Kalimantan and Sulawesi). Indonesia is the first and only archipelagic state to formally designate Archipelagic Sea Lanes under UNCLOS Article 53, establishing three north-south corridors (ALKI I, II, III) through Government Regulation No. 37 of 2002. Critically, Indonesia has not designated east-west routes, preserving sovereignty over lateral transit and creating persistent tension with the US and other maritime powers seeking broader transit rights.

China's "Malacca Dilemma"—the vulnerability of 80% of its oil imports to interdiction through Indonesian-controlled waters—is arguably the single most important geostrategic fact shaping Sino-Indonesian relations. It drives China's investment in alternatives (Pakistan's CPEC, Myanmar pipelines) while incentivizing Beijing to maintain workable relations with Jakarta regardless of other frictions.

<strong>The Natuna friction zone</strong>

Indonesia's EEZ around the Natuna Islands is partially overlapped by China's nine-dash line. Indonesia officially maintains it is "not a claimant" in the South China Sea dispute—a legal fiction that preserves its ASEAN mediator role. In practice, confrontations are escalating. In December 2019–January 2020, about 50 Chinese fishing vessels accompanied by Coast Guard ships entered Indonesia's EEZ, prompting deployment of 10 naval warships and F-16 fighters; President Jokowi personally visited the Natunas and declared "no compromise."[^8] In October 2024, Chinese Coast Guard vessels entered Indonesian waters barely a day after Prabowo's inauguration. Under Prabowo, Indonesia has shifted toward "assertive transparency"—publicly releasing confrontation video—while institutionalizing a new 2+2 dialogue mechanism with China (April 2025).

<strong>The defensibility paradox</strong>

Indonesia must defend 5.8 million square kilometers of maritime territory with a military whose budget allocation is fundamentally misaligned with its geography. Of the TNI's \~395,500 active personnel, **75% are Army**—a legacy of decades focused on internal threats (separatism, counter-insurgency) rather than maritime defense.[^9] The Navy operates \~326 vessels, but only 22 are suitable for EEZ enforcement across 2.7 million square kilometers. The maritime security agency Bakamla has just 10 ships versus an estimated need of 90. A "Trident Shield" rebalancing plan would cost an estimated US\$125 billion over 25 years—resources Indonesia does not have.

<strong>Resource geography as strategic asset and curse</strong>

Indonesia's resource endowment is extraordinary: the world's largest palm oil producer (\~60% of global supply), holder of **42% of global nickel reserves** and \~51% of global mine production, the world's largest thermal coal exporter, plus significant tin, natural gas, copper, and gold deposits.[^10] Nickel is concentrated in Sulawesi and Halmahera; coal in East and South Kalimantan; palm oil across Sumatra and Kalimantan; copper and gold at the Grasberg mine in Papua. This resource geography creates a fundamental center-periphery tension: Java holds the population and political power, while outer islands hold the extractable wealth—a pattern directly inherited from colonial dynamics.

<strong>Climate vulnerability as structural constraint</strong>

Jakarta's subsidence crisis exemplifies how environmental factors constrain strategic choice. Parts of North Jakarta are sinking **10–30 centimeters per year** due to groundwater over-extraction; 40% of the city now lies below sea level.[^11] Without intervention, more than a third of the capital could be submerged by 2050. This crisis was the primary stated justification for the Nusantara capital relocation. Across the archipelago, Indonesia sits entirely within the Ring of Fire (\~127 active volcanoes), lost \~32 million hectares of tree cover from 2001–2024, and suffered \$28 billion in economic losses from the 2015 peatland fires alone.

Indonesia's control of four major maritime chokepoints gives it permanent structural leverage over any great power dependent on Asian sea lanes — leverage that is geographic rather than military and therefore persists regardless of the TNI's capability gaps. The Natuna friction zone will almost certainly intensify because China's nine-dash line and Indonesia's EEZ are legally irreconcilable under UNCLOS, and the diplomatic fiction of "non-claimant" status holds only so long as confrontations stay below the threshold that would force formal legal proceedings. The TNI's army-centric force structure, meanwhile, represents a persistent misallocation for an archipelagic state, driven by decades of institutional path dependency around internal security rather than by any strategic logic oriented toward maritime defense — a rebalancing that no post-Reformasi government has managed to fund. Domestically, Java-centrism is the most politically consequential geographic fact: 56% of the population concentrated on 7% of the land area and generating roughly 59% of GDP, producing separatist pressures in resource-rich outer islands and constraining equitable development. Jakarta's accelerating subsidence makes some form of capital function relocation structurally necessary within the next 15 to 25 years, regardless of which government holds power or what becomes of the Nusantara project.

## IV. Imperial legacy and institutional inheritance

Indonesia's institutional DNA is substantially colonial. The civil law system, land tenure framework, administrative boundaries, center-periphery fiscal relations, and ethnic economic stratification all trace directly to Dutch and Japanese colonial governance. This is not merely historical background—it is the operating system on which post-independence politics runs.

<strong>The extraction template</strong>

The VOC (chartered 1602, dissolved 1800) and the subsequent Cultivation System (*Cultuurstelsel*, 1830–1870) established the foundational logic of Indonesian economic governance: **centralized extraction of peripheral resources for the benefit of the center**. Under the Cultivation System, 20% of village land was allocated to government export crops, generating revenues that at peak accounted for over one-third of Dutch government revenue.[^12] Harvard research (Dell and Olken) shows that villages near historical sugar factories still show different economic outcomes today—the extraction pattern left measurable scars persisting nearly two centuries. The Ethical Policy (1901) was chronically underfunded—by 1930, only 6% of Indonesians were literate—but its critical unintended consequence was educating the generation (Sukarno, Hatta, Sjahrir) that led the independence movement.

<strong>Legal and land systems</strong>

Indonesia's civil law tradition, inherited from the Dutch *Burgerlijk Wetboek* (promulgated 1847), fundamentally differentiates it from common-law neighbors Malaysia and Singapore. Article 2 of the 1945 Constitution's transitional provisions stipulated that all existing laws remain in force until replaced, allowing the entire Dutch legal framework to persist. A new Criminal Code (Law No. 1/2023) was enacted only in 2022 with a three-year transition period—meaning Indonesia operated under colonial-era criminal law for 77 years after independence.

The land tenure system is perhaps the most consequential colonial inheritance. The 1870 *Domeinverklaring* (Domain Declaration) established that "all land not held under proven ownership shall be deemed the domain of the state"—effectively dispossessing communities that held land under customary (*adat*) law without Western-style documentation. The Basic Agrarian Law of 1960 (UUPA) formally revoked the *Domeinverklaring*, but Suharto's 1967 Basic Forestry Law effectively reinstated it by designating **63% of Indonesia's land area as state forest**. About **71% of land parcels still lack formal title**, generating persistent conflicts—the Agrarian Reform Consortium documented 410 active land conflicts affecting \~87,000 residents as of recent data.[^13]

<strong>The Japanese acceleration</strong>

Japan's 3.5-year occupation (1942–1945) was disproportionately transformative. Japan dissolved the Dutch administrative upper layer, replacing it with Indonesians in mid-level positions—providing governance experience denied under colonial rule. PETA (*Pembela Tanah Air*), established October 1943, trained 37,000 men in Java and 20,000 in Sumatra, becoming the core military force of the revolution and the foundation of the TNI. Most remarkably, Japan's *Tonarigumi* neighborhood surveillance system, established January 1944, was repurposed after independence as the *Rukun Tetangga/Rukun Warga* (RT/RW) system—the lowest administrative unit in Indonesia today. The obligation for guests staying over 24 hours to report to the local RT head originates directly from Japanese occupation-era surveillance infrastructure.[^14]

<strong>The language decision</strong>

The 1928 *Sumpah Pemuda* (Youth Pledge) chose Malay—a trade lingua franca—over Javanese (spoken by the largest ethnic group) as the national language. This was arguably the most successful language policy in the postcolonial world. Indonesia achieved over 90% comprehension of the national language across 700+ indigenous languages. The choice was politically significant: Javanese has complex hierarchical speech levels (*krama, madya, ngoko*) reflecting feudal social stratification, making it inherently anti-egalitarian. Bahasa Indonesia's flat register was a conscious rejection of Javanese hierarchy—and a signal that national identity would not be ethnic Javanese identity.

<strong>The Chinese intermediary class</strong>

Dutch racial classification divided colonial society into three tiers: Europeans, Foreign Orientals (Chinese, Arabs, Indians), and Natives (*Inlanders*). Chinese were positioned as commercial intermediaries—tax collectors, moneylenders, small-to-medium traders—while the *Domeinverklaring* and *Wijkenstelsel* (forced residential segregation) constrained their land ownership and mobility. This colonial economic architecture created the ethnic economic stratification that persists today: Chinese-Indonesians (\~3% of population) controlling a disproportionate share of private wealth, producing recurring scapegoating during crises (1965, 1998).

The colonial extraction template — centralized harvesting of peripheral resources for the benefit of the center — has never been fundamentally reversed; post-independence fiscal architecture merely replaced Dutch extraction with Jakartan redistribution, preserving the structural dependency in which outer islands produce and Java allocates. The *Domeinverklaring* principle of state ownership over untitled land persists in practice through the 1967 Forestry Law and its successors, ensuring that land conflict remains a permanent feature of Indonesian politics so long as 71% of parcels lack formal title. More broadly, Indonesia's civil law system, plural legal structure combining statutory law with *adat* and Islamic law, and administrative hierarchy are all direct institutional inheritances from Dutch colonial governance, creating path dependencies that constrain reform efforts decades after independence. Against these continuities, the adoption of Bahasa Indonesia stands out as the single most successful nation-building instrument in the country's history, creating cross-ethnic communication capacity across 700-plus linguistic lines that has prevented the archipelago from fragmenting. The Chinese-Indonesian economic intermediary role, by contrast, is a colonial construction that continues to produce cyclical scapegoating whenever economic crisis or political competition requires an identifiable target — a pattern that is structural rather than contingent, recurring with grim regularity from 1965 to 1998 to the present.

## V. Ethnic, sectarian, and linguistic cleavage structure

Indonesia's extraordinary diversity—over 1,300 coded ethnicities, 700+ languages, six recognized religions—has not produced state failure because its cleavage structure is predominantly cross-cutting rather than reinforcing. Where cleavages reinforce (Papua, historical Aceh), violence and separatism result. Where they cross-cut (Java's santri-abangan divide, the NU-Muhammadiyah rivalry within Islam), they prevent any single identity bloc from achieving monopoly power. Pancasila functions as an engineered cross-cutting mechanism. The greatest structural risk is cleavage realignment—the transformation of cross-cutting divisions into reinforcing ones.

<strong>Javanese cultural hegemony</strong>

The Javanese comprise \~40% of Indonesia's population and exert cultural influence disproportionate even to their demographic weight. Javanese political culture emphasizes *halus* (refined/subtle) behavioral norms, hierarchical politeness, emotional restraint, and consciousness of social stature. All Indonesian presidents have been ethnically Javanese or at least partially so (the sole exception, B.J. Habibie, was maternally Javanese). Javanese cultural expressions—gamelan, wayang, batik—were promoted as national culture under Suharto's "Javanization," generating ongoing resentment among outer-island groups.[^15] Prabowo commissioned a replica of the Majapahit palace in 2024, signaling continuity with the Javanese political-mythological tradition.

The Clifford Geertz *aliran* (streams) framework, while debated, retains analytical utility: *santri* (devout Muslims, aligned with Islamic parties), *abangan* (syncretic Muslims, aligned with nationalist-secular parties like PDI-P), and *priyayi* (aristocratic-bureaucratic class). A 2019 survey found 14% of Indonesians identify as *abangan*, 30% as *santri*, and 3% as *priyayi*, with identification far stronger in Java (66%) than outside it (22%). The critical trend is "santrinization"—the progressive adoption of *santri* religious practices by *abangan* and *priyayi* populations, driven by decades of Saudi-funded Wahhabi mosque construction and growing Islamic conservatism.

<strong>The NU-Muhammadiyah equilibrium</strong>

The rivalry between Nahdlatul Ulama (\~80–120 million affiliates, traditionalist, Sufi-influenced, strongest in East Java) and Muhammadiyah (\~60 million affiliates, modernist/reformist, more scripturalist, urban middle-class base) is the most important intra-Islamic cleavage in Indonesian politics. It prevents Muslim political unity—the single structural feature most critical to maintaining Indonesia's pluralist political order. Despite competitive tensions, the two organizations make common cause on public policy more often than not and both formally uphold Pancasila. The 2016–2017 anti-Ahok mobilization revealed how this equilibrium can be stressed: the regime aligned with NU and deployed sectarian rhetoric, while senior Muhammadiyah leaders challenged Jokowi.

<strong>Chinese-Indonesians as structural scapegoat</strong>

The ethnic Chinese minority (\~3–4% of population, widely estimated to control the majority of private-sector wealth) remains the most vulnerable cleavage point. The trajectory from colonial intermediary role through forced assimilation under Suharto (ban on Chinese characters, mandated name changes) to the May 1998 anti-Chinese riots (at least 168 rapes reported, predominantly targeting Chinese women) to the Ahok blasphemy case (2016–2017) shows a cyclical pattern.[^16] Post-Reformasi improvements are real (Wahid lifted cultural bans in 2000, Chinese New Year became a national holiday in 2002), but social prejudice persists. In 2025, Cultural Minister Fadli Zon dismissed the documented 1998 rapes as "rumor," demonstrating continued official erasure.

<strong>Reinforcing cleavages and separatism</strong>

Papua represents Indonesia's most dangerous cleavage alignment: Melanesian ethnicity plus Christian/indigenous religion plus economic marginalization plus geographical periphery plus racial discrimination. The 1969 Act of Free Choice—in which only 1,025 hand-picked individuals voted under military coercion—is the original legitimacy wound. The Free Papua Movement (OPM/TPN-PB) has intensified since declaring "war" in 2018. The government's response combines special autonomy funds (2% of national DAU), the Grasberg mine revenue sharing (government now holds \~63% after 2025 divestment), and pemekaran (splitting Papua from 2 to 6 provinces)—widely criticized as a divide-and-rule strategy. The UN has condemned "shocking abuses" including killing of children, disappearances, and torture.[^17]

Aceh's separatist conflict was resolved through the 2005 Helsinki MoU, facilitated by the 2004 tsunami's humanitarian opening. The peace settlement granted special autonomy including sharia law implementation—the only Indonesian province authorized to apply Islamic criminal law (*jinayat*). Notably, GAM initially rejected sharia, viewing it as Jakarta's imposition. Aceh's resolution shows that Indonesia's separatist pressures are negotiable when cleavages are addressable through autonomy concessions.

<strong>The Islamist mobilization challenge</strong>

The 212 movement (December 2016, 150,000–500,000+ protesters against Ahok's alleged blasphemy) showed Islamist groups' capacity to weaponize religious identity. FPI (*Front Pembela Islam*) was dissolved in December 2020; Hizb ut-Tahrir Indonesia was banned in July 2017 under Perppu No. 2/2017 for advocating an Islamic caliphate contradicting Pancasila. These actions were political as much as security-based—strengthening NU's position while countering Islamist challengers to Jokowi's coalition. Islamist parties have collectively never exceeded \~43.5% of the vote (1955 peak) and have been in long-term decline, but their mobilization capacity on specific issues remains potent.

Indonesia's stability ultimately rests on the fact that its cleavages predominantly cross-cut rather than reinforce one another, and the greatest systemic risk is any realignment that causes ethnic, religious, and economic grievances to stack along the same fault lines. The NU-Muhammadiyah rivalry within Islam is the single most important structural feature sustaining this equilibrium: by preventing Muslim political unity, it preserves the pluralist governance order, and any development that aligns these two organizations behind a single political bloc would be a critical threat indicator. Papua remains the most dangerous site of sustained political violence precisely because its cleavages are maximally reinforcing — Melanesian ethnicity, Christian and indigenous religion, economic marginalization, and racial discrimination all overlap in a territory whose legitimacy wound from the coerced 1969 Act of Free Choice has never been addressed by any autonomy package. Over the longer term, the progressive adoption of orthodox Islamic practice across formerly syncretic populations — the trend scholars call "santrinization" — is gradually narrowing the cultural space within which Pancasila pluralism operates. And the cyclical pattern of anti-Chinese scapegoating, triggered reliably by economic crisis or political competition, persists because the structural conditions that produce it — Chinese-Indonesian economic prominence combined with minority status — create a permanently available target that no amount of legal reform has yet neutralized.

## VI. Demographic tides and generational dynamics

Indonesia's **\~287 million people** make it the world's fourth most populous nation. It is inside a demographic dividend window—median age 30.7, working-age population at 68.2%, dependency ratio at a historic low of 46.8%—that will close by the late 2030s to early 2040s.[^18] Whether Indonesia capitalizes on this window or squanders it is the decisive question for the country's trajectory. Current indicators—59% informal employment, 55% functional illiteracy, youth NEET rates of 23.8%—suggest the window is being wasted.

<strong>The demographic dividend's narrow window</strong>

Indonesia entered its demographic bonus period around 2012–2015, with the window expected to last about 20–30 years. The total fertility rate has fallen to replacement level (\~2.1–2.2 children per woman, down from 5.5+ in the 1960s). The old-age dependency ratio stands at \~10% but is projected to reach **46%+ by 2100**—a five-fold increase. Indonesia faces the classic middle-income trap risk of "getting old before getting rich": aging at a low per capita income level (\~\$5,083 GDP per capita in 2025) with far less fiscal capacity than China, which faces the same structural challenge.

<strong>Generational fault lines</strong>

Three generational cohorts carry fundamentally different political imprints. The **New Order generation** (born 1950s–1970s, formed under Suharto) values stability over freedom, defers to authority, and dominates the senior military, bureaucratic, and business elite. The **Reformasi generation** (born late 1970s–1980s) is more politically assertive and pro-democracy, many having been student activists in 1998. The **digital-native generation** (millennials and Gen Z, born 1990s–2010s) comprised **over 52% of registered voters in 2024** (\~106 million under 40). Cambridge Core research found Gen Z Indonesians are the **least concerned about democratic backsliding** among all cohorts—exhibiting "democratic complacency."[^19] They voted overwhelmingly for Prabowo despite his authoritarian history, driven by his viral "Gemoy" (cute) TikTok avatar campaign.

<strong>Labor market failure</strong>

The labor market is failing to absorb Indonesia's youth bulge. Open unemployment stands at 4.76% (February 2025, BPS), but youth unemployment (ages 15–24) reaches **16.2%**, and the NEET rate among 19–24 year-olds is 26.4%.[^20] The informal sector employs **59.4%** of all workers—up from previous years, meaning formalization is moving backward. University graduates face rising unemployment, and vocational school graduates have the highest unemployment rate at 8%. PISA 2022 scores place Indonesia among the lowest globally: mathematics 366 versus OECD average 472, reading 359 versus 476. The RISE Programme estimates Indonesia will not reach OECD reading averages until about 2090 without major reform.

<strong>Migration as pressure valve</strong>

An estimated **5 million Indonesians work abroad** across 100+ countries, with remittances reaching about **US\$15 billion in 2024** (10% of total foreign exchange). Key destinations include Hong Kong, Taiwan, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, and Japan. Domestic workers constitute 36.5% of placements, and women comprise over half of migrant workers, exposing them to exploitation and abuse. The government is actively shifting toward skilled worker placement, with a 2026 target of 300,000–500,000 skilled workers. Internally, Jakarta's \~42 million metropolitan population (now the world's most populous urban agglomeration per the November 2025 UN revision) reflects massive rural-to-urban migration that continues to strain infrastructure.

Indonesia's demographic dividend window will close by the late 2030s to early 2040s, and the weight of evidence — 59% labor informality, rising graduate unemployment, 55% functional illiteracy — suggests the dividend is being squandered rather than captured. The generation that could have driven transformation instead exhibits what researchers call "democratic complacency": Gen Z voters, with no lived memory of authoritarianism, are the least concerned about democratic backsliding of any cohort, making it electorally costless for leaders like Prabowo to erode institutional norms. The informal economy's dominance creates a self-reinforcing fiscal trap in which the government cannot fund the human capital investments the dividend demands because it cannot tax the informal workforce, yet it cannot formalize that workforce without the very institutional capacity that adequate revenue would provide. Overseas worker remittances of roughly $15 billion annually serve as a critical pressure valve, absorbing labor market surplus that might otherwise translate into domestic instability — meaning any disruption to Gulf or East Asian labor markets would produce immediate political stress at home. Indonesia's urbanization trajectory, currently around 58% and projected to exceed 70% by 2045, will concentrate political and economic power still further on Java unless the capital relocation or substantial outer-island development materializes at a scale not yet in evidence.

## VII. Economic structure, financial architecture, and dependency patterns

Indonesia's economy—**\$1.44–1.51 trillion nominal GDP** (2025), the world's 16th-largest—is structured around commodity extraction, oligarchic networks, and a growing but shallow financial system. The critical structural dynamic is the deepening economic entanglement with China—which simultaneously drives growth (through nickel investment) and creates strategic vulnerability (through monopsony dependence). Prabowo's "national-capitalist" model, anchored by the Danantara sovereign wealth fund and aggressive resource nationalism, represents an attempt to capture commodity rents for state-directed development, but it operates through the same oligarchic networks it claims to discipline.

<strong>Commodity architecture and the nickel gambit</strong>

Indonesia's resource nationalism strategy is most visible in the **nickel export ban** (full ban on raw ore exports since January 2020). The ban triggered a massive downstream investment surge, overwhelmingly from Chinese firms: Tsingshan, Huayou, GEM, and CNGR control about 75% of refining capacity, concentrated at the Morowali Industrial Park (Central Sulawesi) and Weda Bay Industrial Park (North Maluku).[^21] Nickel export value surged—the sector now surpasses coal as Indonesia's top export by value, with \$16.5 billion in the first half of 2025 versus coal's \$14.4 billion. Indonesia controls **\~58–62% of global nickel production** (up from 31.5% in 2020), with projections reaching 70–74% by 2026–2035.

The strategy's structural vulnerability is acute: **82% of Indonesia's nickel exports go to China**, and 80–90% of refining machinery is Chinese-sourced. This creates what economists call monopsony risk—a single dominant buyer. Indonesian oversupply has depressed global nickel prices, shuttering competing production in Australia and elsewhere but making Indonesia's own producers dependent on Chinese demand stability. The environmental costs are significant: captive coal power plants (25.9 GW capacity, &gt;75% coal-fueled) power the smelters, and land conflicts expanded 114% in 2022.

<strong>The China dependency deepens</strong>

Bilateral trade with China reached about **\$135–150 billion in 2024**, making China Indonesia's largest trading partner for over a decade. The trade relationship is structurally asymmetric: Indonesia exports raw commodities (coal, nickel, palm oil) and imports manufactured goods—China accounts for 22.6% of non-oil exports but **40.3% of non-oil imports**.[^22] Indonesia ran a \$10.3 billion deficit with China in 2024, reversing a previous surplus. The Jakarta-Bandung High-Speed Railway (Whoosh, opened 2023) carries about \$7 billion in Belt and Road-related debt. Prabowo's November 2024 state visit to Beijing yielded \$10 billion in new commitments, signaling continued deepening.

<strong>Oligarchic capture and state-business fusion</strong>

Jeffrey Winters's framework of "collective ruling oligarchy"—where oligarchs compete openly through democratic institutions after Suharto's fall—accurately describes Indonesia's political economy. The wealth gap between the top 40 oligarchs and average citizens exceeds 630,000 to 1. Under Prabowo, the fusion of public and private power has intensified: deputy ministers serve as commissioners on major SOEs; Prabowo's brother Hashim Djojohadikusumo holds major land investments near the new capital; campaign chief Rosan Roeslani bridges business and government. The Danantara sovereign wealth fund places \~\$570 billion in state assets under management structures where political appointment, not independent governance, determines leadership.

<strong>Financial architecture</strong>

Bank Indonesia, formally independent since 1999, operates an inflation-targeting framework (2.5%±1% target) under Governor Perry Warjiyo (second term through 2028). Foreign exchange reserves stand at **\$156.5 billion** (December 2025, equivalent to 6.3 months of imports). The rupiah trades at about **IDR 16,200–16,600/USD** under a managed float. The sovereign debt-to-GDP ratio is relatively conservative at **40.46%** (2025), well below the 58% average for BBB-rated peers, with investment-grade ratings from all three major agencies (Moody's Baa2, Fitch BBB, S&P BBB).[^23]

The critical fiscal weakness is revenue collection: Indonesia's **tax-to-GDP ratio stagnates at \~10%**, far below ASEAN averages and the 21.2% median for similarly rated countries. This is a direct consequence of the informal economy's dominance. The 2025 fiscal deficit hit 2.92% of GDP, approaching the statutory 3% ceiling. Fitch has warned that revenue-to-GDP of only 14.3% leaves limited fiscal space.

The digital economy—projected at \$130–146 billion by 2025—represents the most dynamic growth sector. GoTo (merged Gojek and Tokopedia), Grab, Shopee, and Dana lead a fintech ecosystem where QRIS digital payment adoption surged from 17 million transactions (2020) to 689 million (2024). But **\~80% of Indonesians remain unbanked or underbanked** (ISEAS 2023), and Indonesia has the world's third-largest unbanked population.

Indonesia's nickel downstream strategy has succeeded on its own terms — capturing value-added processing and vaulting the country to dominance of global supply — but at the cost of deep structural dependence on Chinese capital, technology, and demand that now constrains Jakarta's geopolitical freedom of action. The broader China trade relationship mirrors this asymmetry: Indonesia exports raw commodities and imports manufactured goods, the deficit is widening, and the trajectory is toward intensification rather than diversification. Domestically, the binding constraint on state capacity is a tax-to-GDP ratio stuck at roughly 10%, far below regional and ratings-class peers; without resolving the informal economy's resistance to taxation, the government simply cannot fund the human capital, infrastructure, and defense investments its strategic ambitions require. The Danantara sovereign wealth fund concentrates the largest pool of state economic assets under political control in post-Reformasi history, and its governance structure — driven by political appointment with limited independent oversight — creates conditions that could produce either transformative state-directed development or massive rent extraction, with the outcome hinging on regime incentives rather than institutional safeguards. Indonesia's conservative sovereign debt profile at 40.46% of GDP and investment-grade ratings provides a genuine buffer against external shocks, but the narrow revenue base beneath it means the fiscal cushion is considerably thinner than headline numbers suggest.

## VIII. Infrastructural inheritance and resource pathways

Indonesia's infrastructure reveals its strategic intentions more honestly than its diplomatic statements. The colonial pattern—extraction-oriented networks moving resources from interior to coast for export—has been overlaid but not replaced by post-independence integration projects. Jokowi's decade of infrastructure investment (2014–2024) built more toll roads than all previous administrations combined, yet 66% of those toll roads were on Java. The critical question is whether current projects signal genuine archipelagic integration or continued Java-centric development with outer-island extraction corridors.

<strong>The colonial inheritance persists in steel and concrete</strong>

Dutch railways were explicitly designed to transport cash crops—sugar, coffee, rubber—from interior plantations to coastal ports. Indonesia's current railway network of **6,945 kilometers is actually shorter than the 8,157 kilometers of track that existed under colonial administration in 1939**.[^24] The shrinkage occurred on outer islands as extraction-era lines fell into disuse without replacement. Java dominates passenger rail; Sumatra's four non-continuous networks are mainly freight-oriented (coal, pulp, palm oil). Kalimantan has only private mining lines. Papua has no operational rail.

Jokowi's infrastructure campaign was the most ambitious in Indonesian history: **2,103–2,433 kilometers of toll roads** built in 2014–2024, representing 72.7% of all toll roads ever constructed in the country (compared to 189 kilometers under the entire SBY administration, 2005–2014). The Trans-Java Toll Road's main corridor (870 km, Merak to Surabaya) was connected in December 2018. The Trans-Sumatra Toll Road has \~800 kilometers operational of 2,818 planned, with a target completion of 2029. The Jakarta-Bandung HSR (Whoosh, 148 km, opened 2023)—Southeast Asia's first high-speed rail—was financed through Belt and Road investment. Yet the geographic distribution reveals persistent Java-centrism: Java accounts for 1,716 km of toll roads versus 738 km for Sumatra, 97 km for Kalimantan, and 61 km for Sulawesi.

<strong>Port capacity as trade bottleneck</strong>

Tanjung Priok (Jakarta), Indonesia's busiest port, handled **7.6 million TEUs in 2024** against an original capacity of \~5 million TEUs.[^25] The New Priok/Kalibaru expansion (Phase 1 completed, Phase 2 broke ground November 2024) targets 18 million TEU capacity by 2030. Logistics costs remain crippling: **14.3–23% of GDP** depending on methodology, versus 8–10% in benchmark countries. Building a house in Papua costs about three times what it costs in Java due to shipping expenses. Port clearance takes \~7 days versus 1 day in Singapore. The cabotage principle (strengthened by Law No. 66 of 2024) prohibits foreign-flagged vessels from domestic shipping—increasing FDI through forced joint ventures but raising domestic costs by limiting competition.

<strong>Nusantara: Prabowo's inherited white elephant</strong>

The Nusantara capital relocation project (IKN, East Kalimantan) is about 80% complete in its Phase I executive area—the Presidential palace, ministry offices, and 47 apartment towers for civil servants are built. But only 800 of 6,600 planned hectares have been developed, and only about 1,000 authority employees are present.[^26] Prabowo has dramatically deprioritized the project: the 2026 budget allocates just **Rp 6.3 trillion (\~\$400 million)** versus Rp 43.4 trillion spent in Jokowi's final year. IKN was not included in Prabowo's "top five" strategic priorities. In September 2025, Nusantara was downgraded from "national capital" to "political capital." Prabowo has not signed the presidential decree to officially shift capital functions from Jakarta. The strategic significance—moving the political center away from Java and closer to Borneo maritime routes—is real, but execution risk is severe. An anonymous official told reporters: "Prabowo feels that this is not his legacy."

<strong>Energy: coal lock-in versus transition rhetoric</strong>

Coal generated **62% of Indonesia's electricity** in 2023 (217 TWh out of 350 TWh), with fossil fuels totaling 81%. Coal power capacity has more than doubled in a decade, from 25 GW to 52 GW. Captive coal plants for nickel processing add another 25.9 GW. Renewables contribute about 19% of the electricity mix (hydro 7%, bioenergy 6.4%, geothermal 4.8%, solar 0.2%, wind 0.1%)—far below the original 23% target for 2025, now revised downward to 17–19%.[^27]

The Just Energy Transition Partnership (JETP), signed at the November 2022 G20 Bali Summit, committed US\$20 billion from international partners. Progress has been slow: only 1.6 GW of coal considered for early retirement (down from an initial 5.5 GW), and actual disbursement lags. Prabowo has announced a target of 100% renewable energy by 2035 and the RUPTL 2025–2034 targets 61% renewables—aspirational rhetoric contradicted by ongoing coal expansion. The structural reality is coal lock-in: PLN relies on budget subsidies (21% of operating revenues, \~5% of central government spending), coal DMO provides cheap fuel, and the nickel processing boom demands massive baseload power that renewables cannot yet provide. National electrification reaches \~99% officially, but this masks severe disparities in eastern Indonesia, where \~6 million people lack access.

<strong>Digital infrastructure: the connectivity revolution</strong>

The Palapa Ring (completed 2019, 35,000 km undersea + 21,000 km land cables, \~\$1.5 billion) connecting 500+ regencies represents genuine archipelagic integration—the first infrastructure project to meaningfully connect eastern Indonesia to the national backbone. But utilization rates remain low: 69% in the west, 37% in the east. Starlink launched in Indonesia on 19 May 2024, filling connectivity gaps in remote provinces but raising sovereignty concerns. The SATRIA-1 satellite (launched 2023) underperforms: of 150,000 targeted public service connection points, only 27,805 are operational, with less than 12% in Papua/Maluku. Mobile internet penetration has reached 79.5% with 221+ million users, but 5G coverage extends to only 26.3% of the population. Data center investment is surging—projected at \$3.79 billion by 2030—concentrated in Jakarta (56.7% market share) and Batam (fastest growing, leveraging proximity to Singapore).

<strong>Infrastructure as strategic reorientation indicator</strong>

Current infrastructure patterns reveal a country attempting **strategic non-alignment through diversified dependency**. The Sea Toll program (expanded from 3 routes in 2015 to 118 by 2025) and Palapa Ring represent genuine domestic integration. Chinese-funded projects (Jakarta-Bandung HSR, nickel processing parks) orient toward China. Western-funded initiatives (JETP energy transition, Quad-supported Open RAN) create counter-dependencies. The Batam-Singapore data center corridor and RCEP-driven port modernization serve ASEAN connectivity. Danantara's priorities—nickel, bauxite, copper, data centers, renewables—target commodities serving multiple global partners simultaneously. The pattern is consistent with Indonesia's traditional *bebas-aktif* foreign policy: extracting investment from all major powers while committing to none. Under American retrenchment, this hedging strategy becomes not merely traditional but existentially necessary.

The fact that Indonesia's railway network is shorter today than it was under colonial administration in 1939 — 6,945 kilometers versus 8,157 — reveals how selectively post-independence governments maintained extraction-era infrastructure, preserving Java's networks while allowing outer-island lines to decay. Jokowi's infrastructure decade was quantitatively transformative, accounting for nearly three-quarters of all toll roads ever built in the country, yet it did not fundamentally alter the Java-centric pattern: 66% of those toll roads remain on Java. Prabowo has effectively deprioritized the Nusantara capital relocation, cutting its budget by over 85% relative to Jokowi's final year, and the project now risks becoming a partially completed monument to the previous administration unless private investment materializes at a scale not yet in evidence. Indonesia's coal dependence, meanwhile, is locked in through at least the mid-2030s by the combination of PLN's subsidy structure, the domestic market obligation for coal-fired power, and the massive captive energy demand from nickel processing — a reality with which JETP commitments and renewable energy rhetoric are structurally misaligned. Taken together, infrastructure investment patterns reveal a country engaged in strategic hedging: Chinese capital finances transport and industrial processing, Western capital finances energy transition and digital security, ASEAN connectivity serves regional trade, and domestic integration projects like the Sea Toll program and Palapa Ring serve national cohesion. This diversified dependency structure is the physical manifestation of *bebas-aktif* foreign policy, and it remains the most reliable indicator of Indonesia's strategic orientation as American retrenchment reshapes the regional order.

## IX. ENVIRONMENTAL & CLIMATIC STRUCTURE

<strong>Climate disasters as recurring tests of Indonesian state legitimacy</strong>

Indonesia ranks sixth globally in susceptibility to high-impact natural hazards. Located on the Pacific Ring of Fire with 100+ active volcanoes, exposed to El Niño/La Niña oscillation, Indian Ocean Dipole shifts, and equatorial cyclogenesis, the archipelago's environmental structure imposes a relentless cadence of governance stress-tests. **BNPB recorded 2,107 disaster incidents in 2024 alone**, claiming 547 lives and displacing 6.3 million people. The pendulum swings between drought-fire years (El Niño: 2015, 2019, 2023) and catastrophic flooding (La Niña: 2024–2025), each cycle exposing institutional capacity and political will.

Three episodes illustrate the climate-governance nexus most clearly. The **2004 Aceh tsunami** (220,000 killed) forced open a previously sealed conflict zone to international humanitarian access, catalyzing the August 2005 Helsinki Peace Agreement that ended a 30-year insurgency — and drove creation of BNPB in 2007. The **2015 peatland fire crisis** burned 2.6 million hectares, caused an estimated \$16 billion in damages, triggered transboundary haze emergencies across Southeast Asia, and exposed governance failure severe enough to establish the Peatland Restoration Agency (BRG). Most recently, **Tropical Cyclone Senyar in November 2025** — an extremely rare equatorial cyclone — devastated Aceh, North Sumatra, and West Sumatra: 961 killed, 234 missing, 156,000+ homes damaged, about one million displaced. The Prabowo government's refusal to declare a national disaster and reluctance to accept bilateral or ASEAN AHA Centre assistance became a major political controversy. Amnesty International Indonesia called it "a human rights catastrophe reflecting a deeper failure of governance and legitimacy." Scientists directly linked severity to deforestation, industrial expansion, and weak spatial planning. As of February 2026, 23 of 52 affected districts remained in emergency response, many lacking clean water, electricity, and medical supplies.

Jakarta's compound crisis anchors the vulnerability story. North Jakarta subsides at **7–25 cm per year**; roughly 40% of the city now sits below sea level. Projections suggest a third of Jakarta could be underwater by 2050. This drove President Jokowi's 2019 decision to relocate the capital to **Nusantara** in East Kalimantan — officially inaugurated August 17, 2024, with a projected cost of \$30–35 billion. Yet construction has destroyed 14,000+ hectares of forest, investor participation has disappointed, and the 28 million Jakartans left behind face an uncertain future of escalating flood risk with no comprehensive mitigation plan. Semarang and Surabaya face analogous coastal subsidence trajectories at smaller scales.

<strong>Transboundary haze diplomacy remains structurally unresolved</strong>

The haze-palm oil nexus is Indonesia's most persistent transboundary environmental friction. The 2002 ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution (AATHP) — the bloc's first binding environmental agreement — was ratified by Indonesia only in January 2015, twelve years after other members. The Lowy Institute characterizes it as "toothless" given ASEAN's non-interference principle. Singapore enacted its own Transboundary Haze Pollution Act in 2014 as a unilateral response. The 2023 El Niño-driven haze spike (API readings exceeding 300 in South Sumatra) renewed bilateral tension, with Malaysia's Environment Minister formally urging Indonesian action. Malaysian and Singaporean companies own 18–30% of Indonesian palm oil operations, making the issue transboundary in causation as well as effect.

The palm oil sector generates **4.5% of Indonesia's GDP**, creating powerful vested interests resistant to environmental regulation. The Second ASEAN Haze-Free Roadmap (2023–2030) focuses on peatland management but inadequately addresses illegal deforestation and weak enforcement — a structural constraint that no amount of summit diplomacy has altered.

<strong>Indonesia's emissions profile reveals deep contradictions</strong>

Indonesia is the **world's largest thermal coal exporter by tonnage and sixth-largest CO₂ emitter**. Coal provides about 60% of electricity generation; solar PV and wind contribute less than 1%. Its Enhanced NDC (2022) committed to 29% unconditional and 41% conditional emissions reductions below business-as-usual by 2030. The Second NDC (submitted October 2025) shifts to absolute targets, with peak emissions projected at 1,345,707–1,491,474 Gg CO₂e by 2030 — requiring an estimated **\$472.6 billion in investment**. Climate Action Tracker rates Indonesia's trajectory "Critically Insufficient."

The **Just Energy Transition Partnership (JETP)**, a \$20 billion commitment announced at the 2022 G20 Bali summit, has become a case study in implementation failure. The US withdrew from all JETPs in early 2025; Germany assumed co-lead. Other partners raised pledges to \$21.4 billion, but of this, only **\$3.1 billion (14.5%) has been approved**, and only 1.5% of public funding was in grants rather than loans. The JETP Progress Report 2025 shows zero reduction in grid-connected coal power capacity through 2035. Captive coal power nearly quadrupled from 5.5 GW (2019) to nearly 20 GW (2025). The Cirebon-1 early retirement plan — the first major Energy Transition Mechanism project — was cancelled in late 2025. Indonesia's RUPTL 2025–2034 delays over 70% of planned 42.6 GW renewable capacity to post-2030 while frontloading 12.7 GW of new fossil capacity by 2029.

The carbon tax, legislated in 2021 at a minimum rate of IDR 30,000/tCO₂e (\~\$2/tCO₂e), has been **repeatedly delayed since its original April 2022 start date and remains unimplemented as of early 2026**. The IDX Carbon exchange, launched in September 2023, achieved only IDR 78 billion (\~\$4.9 million) in total transactions over its first two years, with just 8 projects listed.

<strong>Institutional capacity is eroding under Prabowo</strong>

The **Peatland and Mangrove Restoration Agency (BRGM) was dissolved in December 2024** when the Prabowo administration chose not to renew its mandate. Over 2016–2024, the agency restored 1.6 million hectares of peatland and 84,396 hectares of mangroves, falling short of targets (2.6 million and 600,000 hectares respectively). Functions were transferred to a directorate within the Ministry of Forestry. A World Bank mangrove project was rated "Moderately Unsatisfactory" as of June 2025, with only 10.97% disbursement. Political appointees with no environmental expertise were placed in FOLU program management positions in March 2025, prompting alarm about institutional continuity.

Indonesia holds the world's largest tropical peatland (\~36% of global total), storing an estimated **46–57 billion tons of carbon** and contributing up to 74% of Indonesia's total natural climate solution mitigation potential. The dissolution of the dedicated agency responsible for managing this asset represents a major strategic risk.

<strong>Deforestation is declining but fragile, with new drivers emerging</strong>

Indonesian government data shows net deforestation fell from **1.9 million ha/year (1990–1996) to \~133,000 ha (2022–2023)** — a 90% reduction confirmed by FAO review. Global Forest Watch data shows a 64% drop in primary forest loss between 2015–2017 and 2020–2022. But2023 saw a 27% uptick from the prior year (El Niño-driven), and a critical structural shift has occurred: **97% of 2024 deforestation occurred within legal concessions**, reflecting a move from illegal to quasi-legal destruction. Nickel mining is now a major emerging driver, particularly in Sulawesi, Maluku, and Kalimantan; a 2022 study found Indonesia accounted for 58% of mining-driven forest loss across 26 tropical countries. Law No. 2 of 2025 (Fourth Amendment to the Mining Law, enacted March 2025) expanded permit access and offered mining licenses to religious organizations, while the 2020 Omnibus Law had already weakened environmental impact assessment requirements.

## X. ILLICIT NETWORKS & SHADOW GOVERNANCE

<strong>A shadow economy worth nearly a quarter of GDP</strong>

Indonesia's illicit and informal economy operates at scale. The **Global Shadow Economy EY Report 2025 valued it at \$326 billion, equivalent to 23.8% of GDP**. Indonesia's Financial Intelligence Unit (PPATK) estimates a wider 30–40% range. Informal employment encompasses 60–70% of the workforce, with 99% of all businesses classified as informal MSMEs — 66 million units employing about 117 million workers. The tax-to-GDP ratio remains chronically low at roughly 10–11%, reflecting the state's limited reach into this parallel economy.

The principal illicit sectors operate with distinct geographic and commodity signatures. **Illegal mining (PETI)** operates at about 2,741 identified locations (Ministry of ESDM, December 2023), with President Prabowo citing estimated state losses of **Rp 300 trillion (\~\$19 billion) annually** in his August 2025 State of the Nation address. Gold operations concentrate in Central Kalimantan, Riau, West Sumatra, and Papua; tin in Bangka Belitung (where refined production fell 30.7% in 2024 to its lowest in 20+ years after crackdowns); coal in South and East Kalimantan; and nickel expanding into Sulawesi and Raja Ampat. The **2.1-ton crystal methamphetamine seizure** near Batam in May 2025 — the largest in Indonesian history, valued at \$300+ million and linked to Golden Triangle syndicates — punctuated the scale of drug trafficking through the archipelago. UNODC reported 236 tons of methamphetamine seized across East and Southeast Asia in 2024, a 24% increase. **Illegal fishing** costs Indonesia an estimated \$1.85–4 billion annually, with the Arafura Sea, North Natuna Sea, and Malacca Strait as primary hotspots.

<strong>State-criminal integration is structural, not aberrant</strong>

The most analytically significant feature of Indonesia's illicit economy is its entanglement with security institutions. WikiLeaks cables from 2006–2007 documented Indonesian officials telling US diplomats that the TNI had "far more troops in Papua than it is willing to admit, chiefly to protect and facilitate TNI's interests in illegal logging operations" and operated "as a virtually autonomous governmental entity." Freeport's Senior Vice President admitted that "main allegations about direct payments by the company to the military are true." In September 2025, Papuan lawmaker Yan Mandenas publicly accused rogue TNI and police members of protecting unlicensed gold and nickel mining in Yahukimo, Nabire, and Raja Ampat.

Police involvement is equally systematic. Former PT Timah executives testified that illegal tin mining in Bangka Belitung was "unstoppable because it enjoyed police protection." In Central Kalimantan, police "crackdowns" function as theater — word leaks from district level, miners hide equipment temporarily, and payments of Rp 500,000–1 million settle unannounced patrols. As PWYP Indonesia concluded in 2025: **"Institutions tasked with upholding the law, including the police, military and regulatory bodies, are themselves implicated in shielding, facilitating and profiting from illicit mining."**

Indonesia's official defense budget covers only one-third to one-half of actual military spending, with contributions from private companies, military-run businesses, and illicit economic activities filling the gap — a structural dependency that incentivizes ongoing security-sector involvement in extractive economies.

<strong>The KPK has been systematically neutralized</strong>

The Corruption Eradication Commission (KPK), once Indonesia's most effective anti-corruption institution, has been progressively defanged since 2019. Law 19/2019, passed in just 12 days, created a Supervisory Council controlling operations, reclassified employees as civil servants, and required board approval for surveillance. The TWK (National Insight Test) was used to purge personnel. Between January and November 2020, 38 personnel left KPK due to disappointment with organizational changes. Public trust fell from 83.1% (2017) to 72.7% (2022). Indonesia's Corruption Perceptions Index score stands at **37 (ranked 99th of 180 countries)** on TI's 2024 index.

The **Hasto Kristiyanto case** crystallizes how anti-corruption has become a political weapon. PDI-P Secretary General Hasto — the most prominent critic of Jokowi and the sole effective opposition figure — was named as a suspect in December 2024, detained in February 2025, and sentenced in July 2025 to three years and six months for bribing a former election commissioner with Rp 600 million. Hasto publicly alleged: "This is just a political issue because of my criticism of Jokowi." KPK leadership was handpicked by Jokowi. The subsequent July 2025 Prabowo clemency — extending to Hasto, Thomas Lembong, and 1,000+ other convicts — indicated that the case served its political purpose and was resolved through dealmaking. This pattern of selective prosecution recurs across administrations: under SBY, the KPK-police "gecko vs. crocodile" conflict; under Jokowi, the systematic legislative weakening; under Prabowo, clemency for graft convicts who return stolen assets.

<strong>Elite financial exposure is large but strategically buffered</strong>

Indonesian oligarch wealth is heavily offshored. A **Dartmouth study (2025) found 70–90% of offshore assets** by Indonesian elites are in "blacklisted jurisdictions" — among the highest globally. An estimated \$93 billion was held in Singapore by 2010 (Capgemini/Merrill Lynch data). ICIJ investigations found nine of Indonesia's 11 richest families held 190+ offshore trusts and companies. Global Financial Integrity estimated Indonesia lost \$10 billion annually in illicit financial outflows between 2001 and 2010.

Bloomberg reported in April 2025 that wealthy Indonesians were shifting "hundreds of millions of dollars offshore" amid concerns over Prabowo's fiscal discipline, with USDT stablecoins gaining favor for moving \$100,000+ undetected and the Middle East emerging as an alternative to Singapore's tighter post-scandal due diligence. Heavy reliance on Singapore's financial infrastructure creates theoretical vulnerability to Western sanctions architecture, though direct targeting remains unlikely absent a major geopolitical rupture.

## XI. INFORMATION ECOSYSTEM & MEDIA STRUCTURE

<strong>Ten conglomerates control Indonesian media, most with direct political party ties</strong>

Indonesia's mainstream media landscape is defined by the **intersection of ownership, party affiliation, and oligarchic interest**. Three of the major conglomerate owners are active political party leaders: **Hary Tanoesoedibjo** (MNC Group — RCTI, MNC TV, GTV, iNews; chairs Perindo Party); **Surya Paloh** (Media Group — Metro TV, Media Indonesia; chairs NasDem Party); and **Aburizal Bakrie** (Viva Group — tvOne, ANTV; former Golkar chairman). **Chairul Tanjung** (Trans Media — Trans TV, Trans7, CNN Indonesia, Detik.com) has no formal party role but actively campaigned for both Jokowi and Prabowo. **Kompas Gramedia Group** retains the most editorial independence among major conglomerates but faces increasing economic pressure and self-censorship. Only **Tempo** (uniquely employee-owned), **Tirto.id**, and **Narasi** (founded by journalist Najwa Shihab) operate as genuinely independent outlets. All major conglomerates have been part of governing coalitions, creating a system where media functions as a bargaining chip for political access rather than a public accountability mechanism.

RSF's assessment is stark: "Around ten large media groups share most of the mainstream media market... the media oligarchy linked to political interests has grown stronger." The Press Council counted 5,019 active media companies in 2024, with 77.4% being online outlets — but the concentration of broadcast media (which reaches the rural majority) in oligarch hands is the structural constraint that matters.

<strong>Press freedom is declining sharply under Prabowo</strong>

Indonesia **dropped 16 places on the RSF World Press Freedom Index in 2025, falling from 111th to 127th** — its lowest ranking since 2016. The classification shifted from "Problematic" to "Difficult." The steepest deterioration was in the security indicator (down 38 positions to 125th) and economic indicator (142nd). RSF noted that journalists reporting on corruption and protests "often faced multiple forms of intimidation by police and military personnel." Freedom House scored Indonesia 49/100 (Partly Free) on its Freedom on the Net 2024 index, down from 56/100 in 2016.

Specific incidents illuminate the trajectory. In March 2024, three navy officers abducted journalist **Sukandi Ali**, interrogated and physically assaulted him for reporting on a navy barge seizure, and forced him to sign papers ceasing journalism practice. During the August 2025 protests, music creators behind a protest anthem were coerced by police into revealing their identities and pulling the song from Spotify — Amnesty International called for investigation.

<strong>The digital repression toolkit has become sophisticated</strong>

Indonesia's 212 million internet users (74.6% penetration) and 143 million social media accounts make the digital space the primary arena for political contestation. WhatsApp leads with about 187 million monthly active users; Facebook reaches 208 million; Instagram 103–119 million; TikTok 100+ million.

The **buzzer (buzzer politik) phenomenon** is a defining feature of Indonesia's information ecosystem. Oxford University identified Indonesia as one of 70 countries using "cyber troops." Buzzer networks, which emerged in the 2012 Jakarta gubernatorial election and have since professionalized, operate year-round — shaping narratives through anonymous accounts, hashtag spamming, mass retweeting, doxxing, and character assassination. During the 2024 election, buzzers helped sanitize Prabowo's image from accused human rights violator to "gemoy" (cute) figure. In January 2020, Reuters discovered the military was funding a network of 10 news sites publishing pro-government propaganda. The Lowy Institute documented Chinese Spamouflage networks amplifying anti-Rafale narratives in Indonesian social media using the hashtag #HentikanRafaleDeal — an instance of foreign actors exploiting Indonesia's buzzer infrastructure.

The legal architecture for speech control rests on the **Electronic Information and Transactions Law (UU ITE)**, under which Amnesty International documented at least 530 cases of criminalization affecting 563 victims between 2019 and 2024, with 146 cases in 2024 alone. The **new Criminal Code (KUHP), effective January 2, 2026**, adds provisions criminalizing insults to the president (up to 3 years), blasphemy (up to 5 years), and spreading communist ideology (4–10 years). Amnesty International's Executive Director called it "a significant blow to civil liberties."

<strong>August 2025 showed a new model of digital-age repression</strong>

The August 2025 protests — triggered by announcement that each of 580 DPR members would receive Rp 50 million monthly housing allowances (\~10x Jakarta minimum wage) — produced the most comprehensive digital repression operation to date. At least **10 civilians were killed**, hundreds injured, and 1,200+ arrested in Jakarta alone. SAFEnet documented the full toolkit: ITE Law criminal charges against student activists for posting satirical memes; doxxing of civil society leaders; TikTok Live suspended by ByteDance under government pressure; Meta removing video of the Police Chief ordering subordinates to shoot trespassers; multiple pro-democracy Instagram accounts suspended; police confiscation and forced data extraction from protesters' phones; and the Broadcasting Commission instructing outlets not to air scenes of brutality.

As analysis in Magdalene.co concluded: **"The most striking innovation was not state censorship, but how the government persuaded platforms to do its bidding... Jakarta outsourced repression to platforms, maintaining plausible deniability."** This represents a model adaptable to future crises — and one that operates with minimal great-power pushback in an era of American retrenchment from democracy promotion. Indonesia has six IFCN-certified fact-checking organizations (including Mafindo, Tirto.id, Tempo.co, Kompas.com), but they operate against a structural tide where 68% of Indonesians get news primarily from social media and only 39% trust news media overall.

## XII. INTERNATIONAL INSTITUTIONAL COMMITMENTS & LEGAL FRAMEWORKS

<strong>Indonesia is pursuing multi-alignment, not non-alignment</strong>

The central finding across Indonesia's international institutional engagements is a shift from traditional "free and active" non-alignment toward what the Lowy Institute terms **"multi-alignment"** — building partnerships issue by issue, shifting with material interest. Under Prabowo, Indonesia simultaneously joined BRICS (January 2025), concluded the EU-Indonesia CEPA (September 2025), signed a new security treaty with Australia (November 2025), maintained US defense cooperation (Super Garuda Shield 2025 was the largest ever iteration), deepened French defense ties (\$8+ billion Rafale program), and attended Beijing's September 2025 military parade alongside Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin before appearing at UNGA and a Gaza peace summit. This is additive hedging, not pivot — but it operates on the assumption that American attention to Southeast Asia is declining.

**BRICS accession on January 6, 2025** made Indonesia the first Southeast Asian full member. The Jokowi government had hesitated after the 2023 Johannesburg Summit endorsement, fearing damage to non-aligned credibility. Prabowo moved swiftly: FM Sugiono attended the October 2024 Kazan Summit and secured consensus within three months. Indonesia frames membership through its "free and active" doctrine and simultaneously pursues OECD membership as a counterbalance. BRICS provides access to the New Development Bank (NDB), alternative economic frameworks, and rhetorical alignment with Global South priorities. In March 2025, Prabowo announced plans to join the NDB, though required share purchases create fiscal pressure. Critically, Indonesia is part of BRICS's push for local currency trade settlements — which could trigger friction with the Trump administration.

<strong>ASEAN centrality is eroding as Prabowo looks beyond the region</strong>

Multiple authoritative sources document a sharp downgrading of ASEAN in Indonesia's foreign policy hierarchy. The Lowy Institute assessed in 2025: "ASEAN, once Indonesia's anchor, now serves mainly as a ceremony. Prabowo's government participates but rarely leads." ISEAS noted that "ASEAN is now overlooked as a cornerstone" of foreign policy. Foreign Minister Sugiono skipped an ASEAN Foreign Ministers meeting in Thailand (December 2024) focused on Myanmar. Prabowo explicitly argued: "If we want to be truly non-bloc, if we want to befriend everyone, that means we are on our own. If we are threatened or attacked, nobody is going to help us" — a remarkably transactional formulation that departs from decades of ASEAN institutionalism.

Indonesia's UN engagement remains substantial. It was elected **President of the UN Human Rights Council for 2026** and ranks **5th globally in peacekeeping contributions** with 2,752 uniformed personnel (January 2025), deployed primarily to UNIFIL in Lebanon. Indonesia has served four UNSC non-permanent terms, most recently in 2019–2020.

<strong>Trade agreements reflect institutional hedging</strong>

The **Indonesia-EU CEPA**, concluded September 23, 2025 after nearly a decade of negotiations, eliminates tariffs on 98.5% of tariff lines. Bilateral trade stood at €27.3 billion in 2024. The deal includes a Protocol on Palm Oil — the most contentious issue — and an Investment Protection Agreement. Ratification targets January 1, 2027. In parallel, Indonesia won a WTO panel ruling against EU palm oil biofuel restrictions (DS593, January 2025) and requested authorization to suspend EU trade concessions. US-Indonesia bilateral goods trade reached \$45.8 billion in 2025, and in February 2026 the two countries signed an Agreement on Reciprocal Trade cutting US tariffs on Indonesian goods to 19%.

Indonesia participates in RCEP and IPEF, though the latter's future under the second Trump administration is uncertain — making BRICS membership partly a hedge against IPEF's potential discontinuation.

<strong>Climate commitments are conditional and increasingly fragile</strong>

Prabowo's brother and special envoy **Hashim Djojohadikusumo** questioned Indonesia's continued Paris Agreement participation in early 2025: "If the United States does not want to comply... why should a country like Indonesia comply with it?" Energy Minister Bahlil Lahadalia described staying in Paris as "a dilemma." Indonesia ultimately submitted its 2035 NDC in October 2025, but Hashim called the JETP a "failed program," noting: "The promised \$5 billion out of \$20 billion has not materialized." As of December 2024, donors had disbursed only \$230 million in grants for 44 projects. Germany signed a €200 million policy-based loan in June 2025, and the JETP CIPP is being revised with coal phase-out deprioritized from second to fifth priority. American withdrawal from the JETP directly weakened the architecture — a concrete instance of the retrenchment dynamic at work.

Indonesia has not ratified the Rome Statute and is unlikely to do so. Military resistance is a primary factor; international law expert Hikmahanto Juwana testified to the Constitutional Court in 2023 that there is hesitancy "if anyone who is deemed a hero to the nation is declared a loser someplace else."

<strong>Defection costs are high but declining as alternatives mature</strong>

Indonesia's deep integration into Western trade (\$45.8 billion US bilateral goods trade; €27.3 billion EU bilateral trade), financial systems (regular IMF Article IV consultations; World Bank/ADB as primary development finance sources), and defense ecosystems (F-15EX, Super Garuda Shield, Australia security treaty) makes a sharp pivot costly. But the growing availability of BRICS/NDB/AIIB alternatives, China's position as Indonesia's top trading partner, and deliberate defense diversification across French, Turkish, South Korean, and potentially Chinese platforms provide genuine optionality. The BRICS Contingent Reserve Arrangement ironically reinforces IMF dependency (requiring IMF programs for access beyond 30% of quotas), but NDB lending and AIIB membership offer supplementary financing channels. Indonesia's critical minerals (nickel, cobalt, bauxite), demographic weight (280+ million), and strategic geography at the intersection of Indian and Pacific oceans mean that Western partners also face steep costs from losing Indonesian cooperation — giving Jakarta leverage in both directions.

## XIII. MILITARY & SECURITY SECTOR DNA

<strong>Prabowo is executing the largest military restructuring since Suharto</strong>

The 2025 TNI Law (Law No. 3 of 2025), passed unanimously by the DPR on March 20, 2025, expanded the civilian institutions where active-duty military can serve from 10 to 14, adding the Attorney General's Office, BNPB (Disaster Mitigation), BNPT (Counterterrorism), Bakamla (Maritime Security), and BNPP (Border Management). Article 7 expanded non-war military operations to include cyber threat defense and protection of citizens abroad. Retirement ages were raised to 58 for enlisted personnel, 60 for generals, and 63 for four-star generals with presidential extension. The law was rushed through in less than two months, with deliberations held in a luxury hotel rather than the parliamentary complex. **186 civil society organizations** launched a petition opposing it, mass student protests erupted, and multiple constitutional challenges were filed.

But Imparsial, a Jakarta-based watchdog, had already documented that **at least 2,569 active-duty officers were serving in civilian roles** before the amendments — making the law less an expansion than a legalization of existing practice. As scholars at IACL-AIDC noted: "It merely strengthens the legal foundation for these practices."

In August 2025, Prabowo inaugurated **six new Army Kodams** (bringing the total to 21), **14 naval base commands, three Air Force commands, six Kopassus groups, 20 territorial brigades, 100 territorial development battalions**, five Marine infantry battalions, and five Air Force commando battalions — the largest military restructuring since the Suharto era. The new territorial development battalions contain agriculture, health, and construction companies, explicitly tasked with economic development alongside combat roles. The stated goal is one territorial battalion per regency/city and one Kodam per province. The army chief of staff openly stated the new Kodams were intended to **"balance" the police**, which already has provincial-level command structures.

<strong>The Kopassus network runs the government</strong>

New Mandala analysis found **9 of 16 ex-army appointees in Prabowo's cabinet have Kopassus backgrounds**. Defense Minister Sjafrie Sjamsoeddin — Prabowo's military academy classmate, who commanded Kodam Jaya during the 1998 crisis and served as Suharto's military adjutant — also serves as ad interim Coordinating Minister for Political and Security Affairs. Foreign Minister Sugiono is a former Kopassus first lieutenant. BIN (National Intelligence) chief Muhammad Herindra is ex-Kopassus. Most remarkably, Cabinet Secretary Teddy Indra Wijaya is an **active-duty Kopassus officer** who served as Prabowo's personal assistant. Kopassus commanders have been elevated from two-star to three-star general positions. The cabinet held a military-style retreat at the Military Academy in Magelang with ministers in camouflage fatigues, 4 AM wake-ups, and marching exercises.

<strong>Force structure and defense spending remain mismatched with ambitions</strong>

Indonesia fields about **400,000 active-duty personnel** (300,400 Army, remainder Navy and Air Force), 400,000 reserves, and 250,000 paramilitary — ranking 13th globally on the Global Firepower index. The Navy operates three fleet commands with Martadinata-class frigates, PKR SIGMA frigates, about four submarines (Type 209-class), and 326 total vessels. The Air Force flies F-16C/D and aging Su-27/Su-30 variants, with the first three Rafale B jets arriving at Roesmin Nurjadin Air Base on January 23, 2026.

Defense spending reached **Rp 335.3 trillion (\~\$23.4 billion) for 2026** — a 37% increase — though ASPI assessed the MoD-specific allocation of Rp 187.1T as "more of a political-fiscal compromise than a strategic breakthrough." At about **0.8% of GDP**, Indonesia's defense spending remains among the lowest in Southeast Asia, compared to Singapore's 3.0% and Vietnam's 2.3%. Prabowo's campaign promise to reach 1.5% of GDP by 2029 would require roughly doubling spending. Only about 25% of the current budget goes to capital spending; the rest is consumed by operational costs.

<strong>Equipment diversification is the signature procurement strategy</strong>

Indonesia is deliberately acquiring military platforms from **France, South Korea, Turkey, the United States, China, Italy, and Japan** — a multi-source strategy designed to avoid dependence on any single supplier. The flagship acquisition is the **42 Dassault Rafale fighter jets (\~\$8.1 billion)**, with potential for 8–24 more under a May 2025 Letter of Intent. During President Macron's state visit, over 20 agreements worth \$11+ billion were signed, including **two Scorpène Evolved submarines (\~\$2 billion)** to be built at PT PAL in Surabaya and CAESAR self-propelled howitzers. Indonesia contracted **48 Turkish KAAN fifth-generation fighters** in July 2025. The **KF-21 Boramae** program with South Korea was restructured, with Indonesia's cost share reduced from \~\$1.15 billion to \$438 million, retaining rights to 48 aircraft. An F-15EX MoU with Boeing covers up to 24 jets. Chinese J-10C fighters have also been confirmed.

Asian Military Review warns this "bewildering fighter smorgasbord" creates severe interoperability, logistics, and training burdens. **85% of defense procurement is funded through overseas loans**, further constraining flexibility.

<strong>TNI-POLRI rivalry is intensifying under Prabowo</strong>

SETARA Institute documented at least **37 instances of military-police conflict between 2014 and 2024**. Under Jokowi, police budgets soared from Rp 44 trillion (2014) to Rp 126 trillion (2024) as he used police as a power base. Under Prabowo, power has shifted decisively toward the military. In March 2025, three police officers were shot dead by two soldiers during a cockfighting raid in Lampung. In February 2025, dozens of army troops stormed Tarakan Police HQ in North Kalimantan after a nightclub brawl escalated. During the August-September 2025 protests, TNI intelligence (Bais) personnel were spotted at protest sites, and allegations emerged that TNI provoked violence to discredit police. The TNI remains **Indonesia's most trusted institution at 95.8%** (Indikator Politik Indonesia survey, May 2025), compared to POLRI at 72.2% — a gap Prabowo is exploiting institutionally.

<strong>Doctrinal orientation is shifting toward maritime deterrence and cyber</strong>

The traditional Sishankamrata (Total People's Defense) doctrine, emphasizing comprehensive mobilization of national resources across Main (TNI), Reserve, and Supporting components, is being supplemented by a new maritime and A2/AD focus. The **Perisai Trisula Nusantara (Nusantara Trident Shield)** concept replaces the Minimum Essential Force framework, emphasizing integrated deterrence across land, naval, air, space, and cyber domains. The Air Force's IAADS ("Cakra") blueprint covers posture through 2044. The 2025 TNI Law explicitly adds cyber threat defense as a military operation, with a planned dedicated cyber force. Relocation of the capital to Nusantara in East Kalimantan positions strategic assets near ALKI II (the vital sea lane of communication) and maritime flashpoints in the North Natuna Sea.

<strong>Defense partnerships span all major power centers</strong>

**Super Garuda Shield 2025** (August 25–September 4, 2025), the largest iteration yet, involved 6,500 troops from 13 nations including HIMARS live fire, amphibious operations, cyber defense, and airborne operations. Indonesia conducted **115 bilateral/multilateral exercises in 2023–24** — more than any other Southeast Asian nation. The **Australia-Indonesia Common Security Treaty**, announced November 2025 during Prabowo's first state visit to Australia, commits both governments to leader- and ministerial-level consultations when security is threatened — stopping short of automatic mutual defense but representing potentially the most significant security partnership under Prime Minister Albanese. A December 2025 Australia-PNG-Indonesia trilateral defense ministers' meeting agreed to meet annually.

Simultaneously, Indonesia resumed joint military exercises with China after a decade-long hiatus (August 2024). Prabowo visited Moscow in June 2025 and signed a "declaration on strategic partnership" with Putin. Reports of potential revival of the stalled Su-35 deal circulate despite sanctions risks. The pattern is unmistakable: Indonesia is diversifying its defense relationships across every available power center, treating military partnerships as a portfolio to be optimized rather than an alliance to be maintained — a strategy that American retrenchment both enables and incentivizes.

---

<strong>Five structural dynamics shaping Indonesia's trajectory</strong>

First, **environmental governance is deteriorating precisely as climate risks accelerate**. The dissolution of BRGM, stalled JETP, unimplemented carbon tax, and expanding coal capacity create a widening gap between international commitments and domestic policy that American withdrawal from climate leadership has made politically easier to sustain.

Second, **illicit networks are embedded in the security architecture**, not peripheral to it. The TNI's structural funding gap, police protection rackets, and systematic neutralization of the KPK ensure that state-criminal integration will persist as a governance feature rather than a bug — particularly as territorial development battalions create new vectors for military economic involvement.

Third, **the information ecosystem has evolved from oligarchic media capture to sophisticated digital repression**. The August 2025 prototype — outsourcing censorship to platforms while maintaining plausible deniability — represents a scalable model for controlling dissent without the crude internet shutdowns that draw international attention.

Fourth, **multi-alignment is Indonesia's institutional hedging strategy**, adding BRICS to existing Western frameworks rather than substituting one for the other. The defection costs remain high in both directions, giving Jakarta genuine leverage — but the balance is shifting as alternative institutions mature and American institutional commitment fades.

Fifth, **the re-militarization under Prabowo is the deepest structural shift**, with the 2025 TNI Law, massive force restructuring, Kopassus network in government, and expanded civilian mandates representing the sharpest rollback of post-1998 democratic reforms. This is occurring with minimal international pushback — arguably the clearest single indicator of what American retrenchment means in practice for Indonesian democracy.

## XIV. HISTORY OF DISSENT AND CIVIL SOCIETY INFRASTRUCTURE

<strong>13.1 — Civil society faces a double squeeze unprecedented since Reformasi</strong>

Indonesia's civil society, once the most vibrant in Southeast Asia, operates under a **dual constriction**: domestic repression escalating under Prabowo and collapse of international funding after the elimination of USAID. The operational space for independent organizations has narrowed faster in 2025–2026 than at any point since the late New Order period. V-Dem's 2025 democracy report places Indonesia in the "grey zone," no longer meeting minimum standards of electoral democracy. Amnesty International documented **104 attacks against human rights defenders** in the first half of 2025 alone, spanning criminalization, intimidation, physical violence, and arbitrary arrest.

The major civil society organizations remain operational but under severe pressure. **KontraS** (Commission for the Disappeared and Victims of Violence), under coordinator Dimas Bagus Arya, documented 10 deaths, 451 injured civilians, and 4,145 arbitrary arrests during the August 2025 protests and their aftermath. KontraS's February 2026 Fact-Finding Commission (KPF) report, produced with YLBHI and LBH Jakarta, traced events across 8 provinces and 18 cities, identifying **6,719 total arrests** — described as "the biggest hunt for activists since 1998." KontraS itself faces systematic intimidation: in March 2025, unidentified motorcyclists surveilled their office, members received calls linked to intelligence officials, and Indonesian Army vehicles were documented on CCTV driving past or stopping at KontraS headquarters on four occasions.

**YLBHI/LBH** (Legal Aid Foundation) continues providing legal defense for detained protesters through the Tim Advokasi untuk Demokrasi (TAUD). LBH Jakarta's TikTok account was permanently banned during the August 2025 protests. **ICW** (Indonesia Corruption Watch) maintains anti-corruption monitoring from Jakarta but faces harassment from pro-government groups. **Walhi** (Friends of the Earth Indonesia), under Executive Director Zenzi Suhadi, filed a Constitutional Court challenge on World Environment Day 2025 against 13 articles of the Job Creation Law's environmental provisions.

The legal architecture constraining civil society has multiple layers. The **Ormas Law** (2013, amended 2017) allows the government to dissolve organizations without judicial oversight — a single ministerial written warning can trigger dissolution within seven working days. The revised **TNI Law** (March 2025) enables military crackdowns on activists under "military operations other than war." The **ITE Law** criminalizes online expression. Most ominously, a proposed **Foreign Propaganda and Disinformation Bill**, confirmed by Coordinating Minister Yusril Ihza Mahendra as a direct presidential initiative, includes a 67-page academic memorandum proposing "foreign capital restrictions" targeting organizations receiving foreign grants. Though not yet on the 2026 legislative priority list, presidential pressure could accelerate its passage.

Prabowo's rhetoric reinforces this framework. At a July 2025 PSI Congress, he accused the Indonesia Gelap protests and #KaburAjaDulu movement of being "fabricated" and "paid for by corruptors." After the August 2025 protests, he publicly used the phrase *"Hai, antek-antek asing"* (Hey, foreign lackeys) — a direct echo of New Order-era delegitimization of dissent through allegations of foreign manipulation.

<strong>13.2 — The student movement showed surprising resilience but structural fragmentation</strong>

The 2025 protest cycle produced the most sustained mass mobilization since 1998, unfolding in three distinct waves. The **"Indonesia Gelap" (Dark Indonesia) protests** erupted on February 17, 2025, triggered by Presidential Instruction No. 1/2025 cutting Rp 306.7 trillion (\$19 billion) from education and health budgets to fund the Free Nutritious Meal program. BEM SI (All-Indonesian Students' Union) coordinated simultaneous protests in Jakarta, Bandung, Surabaya, Semarang, Bali, and Yogyakarta, with an estimated 5,000 student participants and additional civil society mobilization. Nine demands were presented, including reviewing budget cuts, rejecting TNI dual-function, and ratifying an Asset Confiscation Law. The movement adopted a punk aesthetic and was accompanied by the viral hashtag **#KaburAjaDulu** (Just Run Away First), expressing youth disillusionment. A protest song by duo Sukatani went viral before police intimidated the artists into retracting it.

The **second wave** followed passage of the revised TNI Law on March 20, 2025, themed "Indonesia Gelap Jilid 2." Police opened fire during Jakarta protests, injuring five university students. Over 5,000 joint security personnel deployed against protesters.

The **August 2025 mass protests** represented a qualitative escalation. Triggered by a proposed Rp 50 million (\$3,057) monthly housing allowance for 580 House members — ten times Jakarta's minimum wage — amid mass layoffs exceeding 70,000 workers in the first four months of 2025. Beginning August 25 outside the DPR building, protests spread to nearly **50 cities nationwide**. The August 28 killing of **Affan Kurniawan**, a 21-year-old Gojek driver run over by a Brimob police tactical vehicle, went viral and catalyzed dramatic escalation. Government buildings were torched in Makassar and Mataram. MPs' homes were looted. The Straw Hat Pirates' Jolly Roger flag from *One Piece* became a prominent opposition symbol. KontraS documented **10 deaths, 451 injured civilians, and 6,719 arrests** — including 196 minors detained on the first night alone. Forty-four people were reported missing; two remained unaccounted for as of late 2025. Post-protest mass arrests continued through October, with 1,623 detained between September 1 and October 1. Key detained activists face up to 12 years in prison, and a Political Prisoners' Union was formed inside detention.

Key universities active throughout: UI (University of Indonesia), UGM (Gadjah Mada), ITB (Bandung Institute of Technology), Diponegoro, Airlangga, and Mulawarman. BEM SI served as the central coordinating body, developing a "17+8 People's Demands" platform. But the movement differs structurally from 1998. East Asia Forum analysis notes it "shows early signs of expanding beyond spontaneous anger towards more organised and durable action," but remains more fragmented and horizontal than the organized student-civil society-mass organization networks of Reformasi. Social media influencers have "overshadowed NGO activists and grassroots vocational students." Indonesian diaspora communities mobilized far more actively than in 1998 or 2019.

<strong>13.3 — Labor is divided between co-optation and opposition</strong>

The labor movement reflects a deep structural split. **KSPI** (Confederation of Indonesian Trade Unions), under President Said Iqbal, maintains a dual strategy of supporting Prabowo's government while pressing economic demands. On May Day 2025, KSPI joined the pro-government rally at the National Monument, with Prabowo as featured speaker. Yet KSPI rallied an estimated 10,000 workers on August 28, 2025, and organized December 2025 protests against the 2026 minimum wage formula.

The opposition camp centers on **GEBRAK** (Gerakan Buruh Bersama Rakyat), a coalition of 35 critical civil society groups, with **KASBI** (Indonesian Trade Union Congress Alliance) as its largest member. GEBRAK's demands include repeal of the TNI Law. **KSBSI**, under President Elly Rosita, maintains independence from both camps, declining to participate in KSPI's December 2025 actions.

Key labor pressure points: 70,000+ layoffs in the first four months of 2025; Jakarta's 2026 minimum wage set at Rp 5.73 million (\$341), below the Decent Living Needs threshold; Government Regulation 49/2025 on wages rejected by KSPI as suppressing wages; and an ongoing 2026 Workforce Law revision included in the National Legislative Program that could prove decisive for workers' rights.

<strong>13.4 — Religious mass organizations have been neutralized as democratic counterweights</strong>

The co-optation of **Nahdlatul Ulama** and **Muhammadiyah** — with a combined membership base exceeding **100 million** — represents perhaps the most consequential structural shift in Indonesia's civil society landscape. Per ISEAS FULCRUM (February 2026), Prabowo uses "both carrots and sticks" to secure "complete loyalty," resulting in "a deepening co-option of the two Islamic organisations that have long been considered a bastion of 'civil Islam.'"

NU secured 5 ministerial seats, while Muhammadiyah received 7 ministerial/deputy ministerial seats. Both organizations received **mining concessions** — NU's East Kalimantan concession was pressured to switch its business partner from a Garibaldi Thohir-linked company to one connected to Hashim Djojohadikusumo, Prabowo's brother. Simultaneously, KPK investigations target relatives of NU and Muhammadiyah leaders — NU chairman Yahya Cholil Staquf's brother Yaqut was charged in a hajj corruption scandal, while Muhammadiyah executives face corruption investigations. Prabowo pardoned a convicted Muhammadiyah executive. Both organizations endorsed Prabowo's controversial decision to join Trump's "Board of Peace" in February 2026.

The **212 movement** and its successor organizations, including hardline groups like Wahdah Islamiyah, now have friendly relations with Prabowo — a marked contrast from Jokowi's suppression of the FPI. The Islamist mobilization capacity that produced massive 2016–2019 protests has been effectively absorbed. As RSIS confirmed: "By 2025, major Islamic organisations and parties... all have become members of Prabowo's large political coalition."

<strong>13.5 — Digital civil society operates in an expanding surveillance environment</strong>

With over **167 million active social media users** and TikTok and Instagram each exceeding 100 million accounts, digital platforms have become the primary terrain of political contestation. Key hashtag campaigns — #IndonesiaGelap, #KaburAjaDulu, #TolakRUUTNI — organized and documented the 2025 protest cycle. TikTok became critical for protest documentation through live-streaming; 80% of TikTok Shop Indonesia transactions occur through live format.

Government response has been aggressive. TikTok Live was suspended during the August protests, and TikTok's operating license was temporarily suspended in October 2025 for refusing to provide user data on protest activity — restored only after the company complied. Meta and TikTok were summoned by the Ministry of Communication for content removal discussions. SAFEnet documented criminalization of online expression, doxxing, gender-based online violence, internet disruptions, and suspected sabotage of server optic cables. A student was arrested in May 2025 for posting a fabricated meme; a transgender TikTok user was sentenced to 34 months for a satirical video. The government employs paid political "buzzers" to manipulate online narratives.

A rare positive development: the Constitutional Court's April 2025 ruling restricted ITE Law defamation provisions to natural persons only, excluding government agencies and corporations, and narrowed "public unrest" provisions to exclude online spaces.

<strong>13.6 — International funding collapse creates existential threat to independent media and CSOs</strong>

The elimination of USAID — which provided **\$153.52 million to Indonesia in 2024** and had invested about \$800 million in welfare programs — combined with the suspension of NED's worldwide sponsorship of about 2,000 democracy groups, has created what amounts to a funding crisis for Indonesian civil society. Specific casualties include Remotivi (media NGO, lost USAID-linked grants), New Naratif (lost NED funding), and the \$38.5 million ERAT governance program for anti-corruption and transparency. Independent media outlets are accelerating diversification toward membership fees, social entrepreneurship, and European donors. The proposed Foreign Propaganda Bill would further restrict foreign funding channels. Freedom House's Freedom on the Net 2025 scored Indonesia **48/100** (Partly Free, declining).

## XV. PATRON-CLIENT HISTORY AND ALLIANCE GENEALOGY

<strong>14.1 — Indonesia's alliance structure reflects seven decades of strategic hedging</strong>

Indonesia's external relationships have followed a consistent structural logic since independence: maximizing autonomy through diversified dependencies while avoiding formal alliance commitments. The *bebas dan aktif* (free and active) doctrine, articulated by Vice President Hatta in 1948, has been reinterpreted by each successive leader but never abandoned. Under Prabowo, it has mutated into what he calls **"omnidirectional diplomacy"** — a more transactional, leader-driven variant that simultaneously deepens engagement with the United States, China, Russia, and emerging middle powers.

**Cold War positioning** established the template. Sukarno's Indonesia hosted the Bandung Conference (1955), co-founded the Non-Aligned Movement, and pursued confrontation with Western-backed Malaysia. The PRRI/Permesta rebellion (1957–58), involving covert CIA support for regional separatists, left deep institutional distrust of American intentions. After 1965, Suharto's Western tilt embedded Indonesia in the IGGI/CGI aid architecture, with Japan as its largest ODA donor and the IMF/World Bank playing central roles. Post-1998, Western arms embargoes after the East Timor crisis forced defense diversification — the structural origin of Indonesia's current multi-sourcing approach.

<strong>14.2 — The US relationship is stagnating as Trump-era transactionalism replaces strategic investment</strong>

The US-Indonesia defense relationship, symbolized by **Super Garuda Shield** (the largest iteration in 2025 with 6,500+ personnel from 13 nations), retains institutional depth but lacks strategic momentum. The most significant development is negative: Boeing confirmed at the **Singapore Airshow 2026** that the **F-15EX campaign is "no longer active."** The \$13.9 billion package — 24 aircraft with weapons, logistics, and training — never progressed beyond MoU stage. The deal's collapse reflects both fiscal mismatch (the package exceeded Indonesia's entire \~\$9.5 billion defense budget in 2025) and competing procurement from France and Turkey.

Trump's "Liberation Day" tariffs hit Indonesia with a **32% country-specific tariff** in April 2025. Prabowo traveled to Mar-a-Lago to negotiate directly, agreeing to buy \$15 billion in US energy, \$4.5 billion in agriculture, and 50 Boeing aircraft, bringing the tariff down to **19%**. The full Agreement on Reciprocal Trade was signed February 19, 2026, with Indonesia eliminating tariffs on 99% of US products, removing restrictions on critical mineral exports, and adopting forced labor import bans. Trump demanded Indonesia publicly announce a US surveillance drone buy; Indonesia declined. Analyst assessment from The Diplomat (January 2026): "US-Indonesia Relations Stagnated in 2025" — the USAID closure gutted American Indonesia expertise and institutional relationships.

<strong>14.3 — China is Indonesia's indispensable economic partner and primary strategic dilemma</strong>

Bilateral trade reached a record **\$135.15 billion in 2024**, making China Indonesia's largest trading partner by a wide margin. **Chinese firms control about 75% of Indonesia's nickel refining capacity**, with Tsingshan Holding Group and Jiangsu Delong alone accounting for over 70%. An estimated \$30 billion in Chinese investment has flowed into downstream nickel, while Chinese state-led development finance to Indonesia totaled **\$70 billion from 2000–2023**, over 90% as debt at market rates, with an additional \$94.1 billion in private Chinese FDI.

The Natuna Sea remains the friction point. In October 2024, China sent coast guard vessels to disrupt Indonesian exploratory drilling. Prabowo's controversial November 2024 joint statement with Beijing — calling for "joint maritime development" in areas of "overlapping claims" — was widely interpreted as implicitly recognizing China's nine-dash line, prompting rapid Foreign Ministry clarification. In April 2025, Indonesia became China's first **Comprehensive Strategic Dialogue** partner in a 2+2 format.

Indonesia manages this asymmetry through three mechanisms: imposing tariffs of up to 200% on certain Chinese goods, attempting to restructure nickel deals to position Chinese companies as minority shareholders (to qualify for US IRA tax incentives), and maintaining robust Western defense ties as a counterbalance.

<strong>14.4 — France has emerged as Indonesia's most consequential new defense partner</strong>

The France-Indonesia defense partnership represents the most substantial structural shift in Indonesia's alliance map. **Three Rafale aircraft were delivered on January 27, 2026**, the first of an eventual 42 contracted in phased tranches worth about **\$8.1 billion**. During Macron's May 28, 2025 state visit to Jakarta, a Letter of Intent was signed for an additional 12–24 Rafale jets, plus Scorpène submarines and light frigates. Two **Scorpène Evolved submarines** (valued at about \$2 billion) are under contract with Naval Group, to be built at PT PAL shipyard in Surabaya with full technology transfer — Indonesia will be the test case for Naval Group's lithium-ion battery system. Naval Group set up a subsidiary, PT Naval Group Nusantara, in July 2025. France also participates in Super Garuda Shield and Prabowo attended the Bastille Day parade in July 2025.

<strong>14.5 — The Australia-Indonesia security treaty marks a structural upgrade with inherent limits</strong>

The **Jakarta Treaty 2026**, substantively concluded during Prabowo's first state visit to Australia in November 2025 and formally signed in January 2026, commits both parties to "consult at Leader and Ministerial level on a regular basis about matters affecting their common security." It is explicitly **not** a mutual defense pact — if either country's security is threatened, they will "consult and consider what measures may be taken either individually or jointly." The treaty replaces the 1995 Keating-Soeharto security agreement torn up after Australia's role in East Timor's independence. Analyst assessment from ASPI: Australia should not overestimate closeness — the relationship is "not akin to those it has with close non-allied friends as Japan or Canada."

<strong>14.6 — Russia and BRICS signal Prabowo's structural reorientation toward multipolarity</strong>

Prabowo's **June 2025 visit to St. Petersburg** — where he met Putin, signed a Declaration on Strategic Partnership, and keynoted the SPIEF while skipping the G7 Summit in Canada — represents the clearest signal of reorientation. The partnership covers agriculture, space exploration, energy, and military-technical cooperation. A **€2.29 billion joint investment fund** links Danantara with Russia's RDIF. Prabowo returned to Moscow in December 2025, inviting Putin to visit Indonesia. Reports of Russia seeking airbase access at Biak Island (Papua) were denied by Indonesia but alarmed Australia.

Indonesia became a **full BRICS member on January 6, 2025** — the first Southeast Asian nation admitted. Prabowo simultaneously pursues OECD membership (application submitted June 2025) and CPTPP accession (applied September 2024), embodying his dictum: "Indonesia must be everywhere — in the North, in the South, in all platforms."

<strong>14.7 — Emerging middle power partnerships reflect defense diversification logic</strong>

**Turkey** has emerged as a major new defense partner. At Indo Defence 2025 (June 2025), Indonesia signed for **48 KAAN fifth-generation fighters** (valued at about \$10 billion) and **60 Bayraktar TB3 drones**, becoming the first international customer for the Khan tactical ballistic missile. The KAAN deal carries a significant caveat: Indonesia stipulated aircraft must be "completely free of US export controls" — challenging given US-supplied engines, with Turkey's domestic replacement not expected before 2036.

**South Korea's** KF-21 Boramae program was restructured in June 2025, reducing Indonesia's cost share from 1.6 trillion won to **600 billion won (\~\$438 million)** for 48 aircraft, with barter arrangements including commodities. The partnership was strained by a January 2024 incident involving Indonesian engineers caught attempting to steal classified KF-21 technical data.

**India** confirmed a **BrahMos missile deal** in March 2026 — three coastal batteries valued at about \$300–450 million, making Indonesia the second foreign BrahMos buyer after the Philippines. Prabowo was Chief Guest at India's 75th Republic Day (January 2025).

**Japan** remains Indonesia's largest cumulative aid donor with about 2,000 companies employing 7.2 million Indonesians. Security cooperation is expanding through discussions on joint Mogami-class frigate development and Official Security Assistance grants.

<strong>14.8 — Prabowo's diplomatic style is hyperactive, personalized, and strategically ambiguous</strong>

Prabowo conducted **32 foreign visits to 22 countries** in his first year — more than any recent predecessor. His first trip as president was to Beijing (November 2024), followed immediately by Washington — a deliberate signal sequence. He describes foreign leaders as "close friends" and "brothers," employing the Chinese proverb "a thousand friends are too few, one enemy is too many." His worldview centers on realist maxims: "The strong will rule and the weak will suffer." Lowy Institute characterizes the approach as "diplomacy as a search for position rather than purpose... selective alignment, building partnerships issue by issue." Critics describe a foreign policy that is "hyperactive" but lacks coherence — "visibility without vision" — with institutional coordination gaps exposed by joint statement controversies with China and unilateral diplomatic initiatives.

## XVI. CONSTITUTIONAL CRISES AND REGIME TRANSITION PATTERNS

<strong>15.1 — Indonesia's regime transitions follow a recognizable pattern with a critical variable</strong>

Each Indonesian regime transition has featured three consistent elements: **(1)** the military as kingmaker or constraint, **(2)** mass mobilization as trigger, and **(3)** elite bargaining determining outcomes. The variable factor is whether military factions split — when they did (1998), the regime fell; when they held (1965–66), the regime was remade under military control.

The 1999–2002 constitutional amendments fundamentally transformed the system: presidential term limits, direct elections, a Constitutional Court, human rights provisions, and removal of military/police appointed legislative seats. Only 11% of the original constitution survived unchanged. The constitutional order has not been formally amended since 2002, but it is being structurally altered through ordinary legislation — particularly the TNI Law and Danantara SOE framework — that achieves structural change without constitutional amendment.

A critical 2000 bargain embedded in the reformasi settlement separated police from military but secured the military's key demand: no retroactive prosecution for past human rights violations, encoded in Article 28(1). This remains a fundamental constraint on democratic consolidation.

<strong>15.2 — The TNI Law represents the sharpest reversal of post-1998 military reform</strong>

The DPR **unanimously passed** amendments to the TNI Law on March 20, 2025, expanding the number of civilian positions open to active-duty military officers from **10 to 14 institutions** — adding the Attorney General's Office, BNPB (disaster agency), BNPT (counterterrorism), Bakamla (maritime security), and BNPP (border management). Article 7 now authorizes TNI to conduct "military operations other than war" — including cyberthreats, infrastructure security, and local government assistance — **without DPR consent**, requiring only presidential authorization. Retirement ages were raised from 53 to 58 for enlisted and 58 to 60 for generals. Deliberations were held at a luxury hotel rather than parliament. Imparsial reported at least **2,569 active-duty officers** already serving in civilian roles before the amendments, often outside legal frameworks. The law normalizes and entrenches what scholars characterize as a de facto return to Suharto-era *dwifungsi*.

<strong>15.3 — The Constitutional Court crisis destroyed the judiciary's credibility as democratic safeguard</strong>

The October 16, 2023 ruling enabling Gibran Rakabuming Raka's vice-presidential candidacy was the most consequential judicial decision since Reformasi. The Court ruled 5–4 to create an exception to the 40-year minimum age requirement for candidates who had held or been elected to public office. Chief Justice **Anwar Usman** — who had married Jokowi's sister in May 2022, making him Gibran's uncle — failed to recuse himself. The Ethics Council found Anwar guilty of "serious ethical violation" of five principles but only dismissed him as Chief Justice while allowing him to remain as ordinary justice. The ruling itself was preserved intact. Just six days after the decision, Prabowo announced Gibran as his running mate.

<strong>15.4 — The mega-coalition and centralized decision-making eliminate structural checks</strong>

The **KIM Plus coalition** holds **470 of 580 DPR seats (81%)**, with only PDI-P's 110 seats outside — and even PDI-P has not adopted formal opposition, with Speaker Puan Maharani leading passage of the TNI Law. This virtual unanimity eliminates legislative oversight.

Prabowo's cabinet of **111+ appointees** — the largest since Sukarno's 132-minister cabinet in 1966 — includes at least **23 retired military/police officers**, with 9 of 16 ex-army appointees having Kopassus backgrounds and personal links to Prabowo. His inner circle consists predominantly of Taruna Nusantara alumni (the elite semi-military high school he helped establish in 1990) and Gerindra cadres. Key figures include State Secretary Prasetyo Hadi, Defense Minister Sjafrie Sjamsoeddin (military academy classmate implicated in 1998 crackdowns), and brother Hashim Djojohadikusumo as primary financier.

Power centralization operates through multiple mechanisms: budget decree authority (the January 2025 freeze redirecting Rp 300+ trillion), **Danantara** (managing \$172+ billion in state assets with no independent audit mechanism), fiscal recentralization (regional transfers cut about one-third from 2024 levels), and the September 2025 cabinet reshuffle that removed Jokowi loyalists including Finance Minister Sri Mulyani Indrawati.

Vice President **Gibran Rakabuming Raka**, at 37 Indonesia's youngest-ever VP, has been marginalized. By May 2025, he was described as an "invisible VP" excluded from strategic meetings and cabinet reshuffles. Prabowo sent Jokowi, not Gibran, to Pope Francis's funeral — a role normally reserved for the VP. Retired military officers' Forum formally petitioned for Gibran's dismissal, though the DPR took no action.

<strong>15.5 — All major democracy indices confirm a decade of continuous decline</strong>

Marcus Mietzner (ANU) identifies a pattern where elite collusion "both enables and limits executive aggrandizement" — elites produce backsliding but set limits (they rejected Jokowi's term-extension bid), protecting from "full collapse" while producing "gradual decline, reducing chances of democratic revitalization."

The evidence supports a characterization of **accelerating democratic erosion approaching — but not yet reaching — authoritarian consolidation**. Constraining factors include: elite competition between Prabowo and Jokowi factions, civil society protest resilience (five major waves in 2025), the Constitutional Court's occasional independence (April 2025 ITE ruling), international scrutiny, and constitutional term limits.

## XVII. COLLECTIVE MEMORY OF HUMILIATION AND GRIEVANCE

<strong>16.1 — The 1965 massacres are being actively rehabilitated rather than reckoned with</strong>

The estimated 500,000–1 million killed in the 1965–66 anti-communist purges represent Indonesia's deepest unresolved trauma. Under Prabowo, the trajectory has shifted from suppression to active **historical rehabilitation of the perpetrators**. On November 10, 2025 (Heroes Day), Prabowo conferred the **national hero title on Suharto** via Presidential Decree — HRW's Usman Hamid called it "utterly absurd" given Suharto's responsibility for "one of history's greatest genocides." Culture Minister Fadli Zon commissioned a **10-volume national history series** that presents the 1965–67 mass killings simply as a "resolution" of the 30 September Movement. Made Supriatma (ISEAS) compared it to Suharto's 1975 state history project. Indonesia at Melbourne assessed that Prabowo has "politicised history to an extent not seen since the New Order."

YPKP 65 has identified **359 mass graves** across Indonesia. Communism/Marxism remain legally banned. The 2004 curriculum revision acknowledging the killings was reversed in 2006, with revised textbooks ordered burned. The International People's Tribunal (2015–16) found Indonesia guilty of crimes against humanity and concluded the massacres "could be categorised as genocide," but the verdict had no legal consequences.

Prabowo's personal stakes are direct: his former father-in-law was Suharto; his military career included Kopassus operations marked by civilian massacres in Papua; and he was dismissed from the military in 1998 for kidnapping student activists. The historian Asvi Warman Adam predicted the textbook rewrite will ensure "the New Order will only be a historical account of the successes in development... this record will also erase the cases involving Prabowo in 1998."

<strong>16.2 — The colonial narrative provides the ideological substrate for multipolarity</strong>

The "350 years of colonization" narrative — historically simplified, as full Dutch control over the entire archipelago was achieved only by the early 20th century — functions as the foundational myth of Indonesian sovereignty. Under Prabowo, this narrative directly informs BRICS membership, resource nationalism, skepticism of Western trade conditions, and multi-alignment strategy. At the 2025 SPIEF, Prabowo explicitly invoked colonialism: "For the past 30 years, we have witnessed the dominance of neoliberal economic philosophy and classical free-market capitalism." His inaugural speech centered "anti-colonialism, sovereignty, and self-sufficiency."

The colonial narrative fuels **resource nationalism** across partisan lines. Eve Warburton (Lowy Institute/Cornell) documented how it connects to "popular notions of economic sovereignty," linking land and subsoil resources to national citizens and making normative claims about who should control and benefit from resources. The EU's deforestation regulations and US tariffs are routinely characterized as neo-colonialism.

Notably absent from collective memory: Japan's WWII occupation. A Springer Nature study found the occupation "is largely absent from Indonesian historical consciousness." Japan enabled independence, trained the military, and established administrative structures — the occupation is remembered instrumentally, not as trauma.

<strong>16.3 — The May 1998 riots remain a taboo subject with latent activation potential</strong>

The May 1998 riots — 1,200+ burned to death, 8,500 buildings destroyed, 130+ documented rapes of Chinese-Indonesian women in Jakarta — have **never been criminally investigated**. No prosecutions, no redress. Weekly "Kamisan" protests by mothers of victims have continued outside the presidential palace for over 17 years. Chinese-Indonesians commemorated the 27th anniversary in May 2025 with vows to "resist forgetting."

The textbook rewrite project is expected to omit Prabowo's direct implication in the 1998 kidnapping of student activists that preceded the riots. Anti-Chinese sentiment was weaponized politically in the 2016–2017 campaign against Jakarta Governor Ahok. The military's documented role in fomenting anti-Chinese violence in 1998 — as a "frame-shifting strategy" to distract from its failure to control student protests — established a dangerous precedent that remains available for future activation during economic crises.

<strong>16.4 — Papua represents an active colonial war framed as counterinsurgency</strong>

The situation in Papua has **dramatically worsened** under Prabowo. Human Rights Monitor reported "an unusually high number of torture cases" in Q1 2025, aerial bombardments near civilian settlements in Intan Jaya, and at least **86,886 internally displaced persons**. TNI General Agus Subiyanto announced a new war doctrine in January 2025 including drones and helicopter gunships for bombing remote highlands. The Merauke Strategic National Project plans to develop 2 million hectares of customary land for agriculture without free, prior, informed consent, with implementation handed to the TNI. West Papua remains closed to international observers, UN agencies, and independent journalists.

Papua's domestic framing serves a dual function: the NKRI (Unitary State) territorial integrity narrative makes any external attention to Papua equivalent to foreign interference, while the resource extraction logic (Freeport gold/copper, Wabu Block gold reserves of 2.3 million kg) drives continued military presence. Prabowo's personal history compounds the issue — he led 1996 military operations in Papua marked by civilian massacres.

<strong>16.5 — Islamic political grievance has been domesticated but religious intolerance persists</strong>

The Jakarta Charter's "seven words" — demanding sharia obligation for Muslim adherents — were removed from the constitution on its first day. This generated a foundational grievance that drove the Darul Islam rebellion and Islamic party politics through the 20th century. By 2024, however, this grievance has been **effectively domesticated**: transactional coalition-building takes priority over ideology, NU issued a 2019 declaration explicitly rejecting the caliphate ideal, and Prabowo absorbed Islamic organizations into his patronage network rather than mobilizing them oppositionally.

<strong>16.6 — Grievance narratives constrain foreign policy through three mechanisms</strong>

**Sovereignty maximalism**: The colonial narrative, Papua framing, and East Timor experience combine to produce reflexive resistance to any external involvement in domestic affairs — constraining human rights cooperation, international monitoring, and accountability mechanisms.

**Anti-Western hedging**: Colonial memory provides domestic legitimacy for BRICS membership, Russia partnership, and multi-alignment, while making exclusive alignment with Western institutions politically costly.

**Resource nationalism**: The "350 years" narrative produces broad bipartisan support for state-led, domestically owned resource exploitation models — constraining the terms on which foreign investment (particularly Chinese investment in nickel) can operate.

## XVIII. CROSS-FACET INTERSECTION ANALYSIS

<strong>17.1 — Nickel dependency creates an environmental-sovereignty-alliance trilemma</strong>

Chinese firms control **75% of Indonesia's nickel refining capacity**, creating a structural dependency that constrains three domains simultaneously. Indonesia cannot enforce environmental regulations at Chinese smelters — where 60+ deaths/accidents were linked to Tsingshan operations in 2023 alone — without risking capital flight from the sector that generates \$38–40 billion in export revenues. It cannot pivot away from Chinese economic dependence even while hedging militarily through French, Turkish, and Indian procurement. And it cannot comply with emerging Western ESG and supply chain due diligence requirements (EU deforestation regulation, US forced labor import bans from the February 2026 trade deal) without restructuring Chinese-dominated production chains. Government Regulation No. 28/2025 froze new nickel smelter investment and raised royalties from 10% to 14–19% — a late attempt to reassert control. But the February 2026 US trade deal's requirement to remove restrictions on critical mineral exports to the United States directly contradicts the downstreaming policy that was supposed to capture value domestically. **The nickel sector is the point where Indonesia's economic nationalism, environmental governance, and alliance hedging collide irreconcilably.**

<strong>17.2 — The archipelagic information gap enables selective authoritarian deepening</strong>

Indonesia's 17,000-island geography creates structural information asymmetries that Prabowo's centralization exploits. Digital civil society and protest movements concentrate overwhelmingly on Java — BEM SI coordination operates through Java-centric university networks, while TikTok live-streaming and Instagram mobilization require bandwidth that outer island populations lack. When authorities suspended TikTok during August 2025 and implemented internet blackouts, the information impact was asymmetric: Java-based protesters could shift to alternative platforms and media coverage, while peripheral communities lost access entirely. The TNI Law's expansion of military "operations other than war" to include cyberthreats and infrastructure security acquires different meaning in Papua (where West Papua remains closed to independent journalists) than in Jakarta (where international media presence constrains repression). **Democratic erosion proceeds at different speeds across the archipelago, with the most severe repression occurring in the most information-poor zones.**

<strong>17.3 — The demographic dividend is being militarized rather than realized</strong>

With **42% of the population under 25**, real wages declining 1.1% annually over 2018–2024, and 68% of the population living near poverty by revised World Bank standards, Indonesia's youth bulge is becoming a destabilizer rather than an economic dividend. Prabowo's response has been to channel this energy through two parallel structures: the **MBG school meal program** (Rp 335 trillion allocated for 2026, absorbing 8.8% of the budget) as a patronage delivery mechanism reaching 30+ million beneficiaries, and **military expansion** — dozens of new military units, six new regional commands, defense budget rising from \$8.8 billion to \$11.8 billion — as both employment program and control infrastructure. The 73.3% cut to the Ministry of Public Works and regional transfer reductions shift resources from productive infrastructure to consumption (MBG) and coercion (military), structurally undermining the conditions for the demographic dividend to materialize. **The August 2025 protests — triggered by elite privilege amid mass layoffs — are the leading indicator of this failure.**

<strong>17.4 — Religious co-optation removes the historical circuit-breaker for authoritarian overreach</strong>

In every previous Indonesian regime crisis, religious mass organizations played a critical role in constraining or channeling dissent. NU and Muhammadiyah provided organizational infrastructure for the 1998 movement; they served as negotiation intermediaries between students and the military; their moral authority set limits on state violence. Prabowo's systematic co-optation through ministerial seats, mining concessions, and selective KPK investigations has **eliminated this circuit-breaker**. The organizations are now materially dependent on the regime and actively deployed against civil society — ISEAS describes them as "forced to challenge the progressive forces within civil society." If economic crisis generates mass mobilization on the scale of 1998, the traditional institutional intermediaries that prevented total state breakdown will be absent. **This intersection between religious co-optation and democratic erosion is the single most underappreciated structural risk.**

<strong>17.5 — The 1965 memory project simultaneously delegitimizes dissent and enables BRICS alignment</strong>

The Suharto rehabilitation project and "antek-antek asing" (foreign lackeys) rhetoric against civil society draw directly from the 1965 anti-communist playbook — external enemies corrupting domestic order through domestic proxies. This narrative framework performs double duty: it delegitimizes democratic opposition by associating it with foreign manipulation (reproducing the PKI framing), while simultaneously providing ideological cover for alignment with Russia and China, who share an institutional interest in discrediting Western-funded civil society. The €2.29 billion Danantara-RDIF joint investment fund and the proposed Foreign Propaganda Bill are two faces of the same structural logic: **strengthening non-Western economic ties while closing the space for Western-linked civil society organizations.** The textbook rewrite project literally constructs the historical foundation for this reorientation.

<strong>17.6 — Colonial land tenure patterns perpetuate the Papua conflict through resource extraction logic</strong>

Dutch colonial-era extractive frameworks persist in Papua through direct institutional inheritance. The concession model — external capital extracting resources from indigenous territories under state military protection — maps directly from VOC-era spice extraction through Suharto's Freeport contract to Prabowo's Merauke Strategic National Project. The 2 million hectares of customary Marind Anim land being developed for rice and sugarcane without FPIC, with implementation handed to the TNI, reproduces the colonial structure with different flags. The proposed amnesty for independence fighters (January 2025), conditioned on pledging loyalty to Indonesia, mirrors colonial-era "pacification" offers. **Papua is where Indonesia's anti-colonial identity narrative and its colonial-pattern resource extraction create the sharpest internal contradiction** — one that the sovereignty maximalism of the NKRI framework prevents from being acknowledged, let alone resolved.

## XIX. KEY ANALYTICAL JUDGMENTS

<strong>Judgment 1: Indonesia will not recover "Free" democracy status during Prabowo's presidency.</strong>
*Confidence: HIGH (90%)*

The structural conditions for democratic recovery are absent: the mega-coalition eliminates legislative checks, the Constitutional Court's legitimacy is compromised, religious mass organizations are co-opted, the TNI Law entrenches military civilian roles, and the proposed Foreign Propaganda Bill would further constrict civil society. The EIU, Freedom House, and V-Dem trajectories all show continuous decline over a decade. Elite competition constrains full authoritarian consolidation but does not produce democratic recovery.

*Falsification condition:* Freedom House upgrades Indonesia to "Free" (score above 72) or V-Dem Liberal Democracy Index returns above 0.50 before October 2029.

<strong>Judgment 2: The Prabowo mega-coalition will fragment before the 2029 elections, triggered by distributional conflict over Danantara resources or fiscal stress.</strong>
*Confidence: MODERATE-HIGH (75%)*

The coalition's cohesion depends on patronage distribution. Danantara's \$172+ billion in managed assets and mining concessions provide the material basis, but Fitch and Moody's negative outlook revisions (March 2026), the statutory 3% deficit ceiling, and the MBG program consuming 8.8% of the 2026 budget create fiscal pressure that will force distributional choices. The September 2025 removal of Jokowi loyalists shows that coalition management requires periodic exclusion — each exclusion creates a potential defector. If GDP growth falls below 4.5% for two consecutive quarters, the patronage base contracts below what the coalition requires.

*Falsification condition:* KIM Plus maintains &gt;75% DPR seat control through the 2029 election without a major party defection, or Danantara generates enough returns to avoid fiscal trade-offs.

<strong>Judgment 3: Chinese share of Indonesian nickel refining will exceed 80% by 2028 absent active industrial policy intervention that Prabowo is structurally unlikely to implement.</strong>
*Confidence: MODERATE (65%)*

Government Regulation 28/2025 freezing new smelter investment and the royalty increase (10% to 14–19%) represent a late effort at reassertion, but 54 operating smelters, 38 under construction, and 45 in planning are predominantly Chinese-financed. Indonesian firms own only about 10% of refined capacity. The February 2026 US trade deal's critical mineral export provisions may actually accelerate Chinese processing dominance by increasing demand for Indonesian refined nickel.

*Falsification condition:* Indonesian-owned share of refined nickel capacity rises above 20% by 2028, or government successfully restructures majority-Chinese smelters into minority-Chinese ownership.

<strong>Judgment 4: The civil society funding gap from USAID elimination will not be filled by alternative sources within three years, producing permanent organizational attrition.</strong>
*Confidence: HIGH (85%)*

USAID provided \$153.52 million annually; NED sponsored about 2,000 democracy groups globally. European donors, while willing, lack the scale and institutional infrastructure to replace these flows. The proposed Foreign Propaganda Bill would further constrain foreign funding channels. Indonesian independent media are attempting diversification to membership and social enterprise models, but these generate a fraction of previous funding. The organizational capacity destroyed — particularly in governance, anti-corruption, and media independence — takes decades to rebuild.

*Falsification condition:* Alternative funding sources (European governments, private philanthropy, membership models) reach 75% of previous USAID/NED levels within 36 months.

<strong>Judgment 5: Prabowo's defense procurement diversification will generate an interoperability crisis by 2028–2030.</strong>
*Confidence: MODERATE-HIGH (75%)*

Simultaneous procurement from France (Rafale, Scorpène), Turkey (KAAN, Bayraktar, Khan), South Korea (KF-21), India (BrahMos), and legacy Russian platforms (Su-27/30) creates a logistics, maintenance, and interoperability challenge that exceeds Indonesia's institutional capacity. There has been no updated Defense White Paper since 2015. Analysts describe the acquisition strategy as "haphazard and fragmented." The KAAN deal's stipulation that aircraft be free of US export controls — impossible before Turkey's domestic engine by 2036 at earliest — suggests some agreements function as diplomatic signaling rather than operational procurement.

*Falsification condition:* Indonesia publishes a Defense White Paper with integrated platform strategy, or successfully operates 4+ fighter types simultaneously at stated readiness levels.

<strong>Judgment 6: A major protest cycle exceeding August 2025 in scale will occur before 2028, triggered by the intersection of economic grievance and governance failure.</strong>
*Confidence: MODERATE-HIGH (70%)*

Five major protest waves in 2025 alone establish a pattern of escalating frequency. The structural triggers persist: fiscal austerity outside the MBG program, mass layoffs in manufacturing (textile sector collapse), real wage decline, elite self-dealing visible through Danantara, and the absence of institutional channels for grievance resolution. The 2025 protests showed that leaderless, social media-coordinated movements can rapidly scale. The removal of religious organization intermediaries (Judgment 4 of Section 17) increases the risk that future protests lack moderation mechanisms.

*Falsification condition:* GDP growth exceeds 6% sustained, real wages increase, and no protest wave exceeding 20 cities occurs before December 2027.

<strong>Judgment 7: Indonesia will attempt to mediate or host a major international peace initiative by 2027, leveraging BRICS membership, ASEAN chair ambitions, and Prabowo's "everywhere" doctrine.</strong>
*Confidence: MODERATE (60%)*

Prabowo's diplomatic hyperactivity — 32 visits to 22 countries in year one — and his framing of Indonesia as a bridge between Global North and South create institutional pressure for a signature diplomatic achievement. The Gaza evacuation offer (1,000 Palestinians), the Russia strategic partnership, and BRICS membership position Indonesia as a potential mediator. The risk is that "visibility without vision" produces a high-profile initiative that lacks follow-through.

*Falsification condition:* Indonesia does not host, convene, or actively mediate a major multilateral peace process by December 2027.

## XX. WATCH INDICATORS

<strong>1. Danantara transparency trajectory</strong>
*Observable:* Audit access by BPK/BPKP/KPK; publication of portfolio returns; Patriot Bond subscription rates; credit rating agency commentary on SOE governance.
*Threshold:* If Danantara operates for 18+ months without independent audit, treat as confirming authoritarian fiscal capture. Patriot Bond subscription below 50% of Rp 50 trillion target signals investor resistance.

<strong>2. Foreign Propaganda Bill legislative progress</strong>
*Observable:* Inclusion in Prolegnas priority list; DPR committee assignment; draft text circulation; civil society mobilization response.
*Threshold:* Entry into the 2026 or 2027 priority list signals acceleration. Passage would represent the gravest legal threat to civil society since the Ormas Law amendment.
*Channel:* DPR legislative tracker, Jakarta Post parliamentary coverage, ICNL regulatory tracker.

<strong>3. Constitutional Court rulings on TNI Law challenges</strong>
*Observable:* Docket assignment, oral arguments, ruling timeline, composition of panel.
*Threshold:* Upholding the law confirms judicial capitulation. Partial strike-down (e.g., limiting OMSP scope) signals residual institutional independence. Full strike-down (extremely unlikely) would indicate elite fracture.
*Channel:* MK (Constitutional Court) website, academic commentary, Tempo/Kompas reporting.

<strong>4. Student protest coordination evolution</strong>
*Observable:* BEM SI organizational changes; formation of permanent cross-university structures; labor-student alliance formalization; diaspora network institutionalization.

*Channel:* Social media monitoring (Twitter/X, TikTok, Instagram), BEM SI statements, university media.

<strong>Confirming Indicators (Signal confirms structural shift already underway)</strong>

<strong>6. Defense budget as percentage of GDP</strong>
*Observable:* Annual budget allocations, supplementary budgets, off-budget defense spending through Danantara.

*Channel:* Finance ministry budget documents, SIPRI database, Janes Defence Budgets.

<strong>7. Nickel sector ownership restructuring</strong>
*Observable:* Indonesian vs. Chinese ownership shares in operating smelters; enforcement of Regulation 28/2025; US IRA qualification negotiations; new Indonesian-led ventures.
*Threshold:* If Indonesian-owned share rises above 15%, the resource nationalism agenda is gaining traction. If Chinese share exceeds 80%, dependency deepens beyond manageable levels.
*Channel:* BKPM (Investment Ministry) data, industry publications (Mining.com, Reuters), C4ADS tracking.

<strong>8. Papua conflict intensity and access</strong>
*Observable:* IDP numbers, extrajudicial killing reports, international journalist/NGO access requests, UNGA/UNHRC statements.
*Threshold:* IDP numbers exceeding 100,000 or confirmed use of armed drones against civilian areas would signal qualitative escalation. Any grant of international media access would signal strategic softening.
*Channel:* Human Rights Monitor quarterly reports, TAPOL, HRW, ULMWP statements, satellite imagery analysis.

<strong>9. NU/Muhammadiyah leadership elections (NU congress July/August 2026)</strong>
*Observable:* Candidate platforms, level of state interference, outcome relative to Prabowo preferences.
*Threshold:* If Yahya Cholil Staquf is replaced by a more independent figure despite Prabowo's preference, this signals limits to religious co-optation. If a regime loyalist consolidates control, it confirms Section 17.4 assessment.
*Channel:* NU official statements, ISEAS analysis, Indonesian religious media.

<strong>10. OECD accession milestones vs. BRICS institutional engagement</strong>
*Observable:* OECD review committee reports, legislative compliance assessments, BRICS NDB lending volumes, attendance/initiative levels at BRICS summits.
*Threshold:* If OECD accession stalls while BRICS engagement deepens (particularly NDB lending exceeding \$5 billion), Indonesia's structural orientation toward the non-Western institutional order is confirmed. Simultaneous progress on both suggests the "everywhere" doctrine is sustainable.
*Channel:* OECD accession tracker, NDB annual reports, BRICS summit communiqués.

<strong>11. Press freedom index movements</strong>
*Observable:* RSF annual ranking, AJI violence tracking, journalist imprisonment numbers, media outlet closures.

*Channel:* RSF annual report, AJI annual survey, CPJ imprisonment tracker.

<strong>12. Rupiah exchange rate and capital flow patterns</strong>
*Observable:* IDR/USD rate, foreign portfolio flows, Bank Indonesia intervention frequency, foreign reserve levels.

## XXI. PIPELINE INTEGRATION NOTES

<strong>20.1 — Source interpretation guidance for Indonesian media</strong>

**Tier 1 (Highest analytical value):** Tempo (Indonesia's premier investigative outlet; has faced severed pig heads and dead rats as intimidation, confirming its independence), Tirto.id (data-driven journalism, digital-native), Kompas (establishment paper but maintains editorial standards), and Jakarta Post (English-language; important for international positioning signals — what the government wants external audiences to see).

**Tier 2 (Valuable with calibration):** CNN Indonesia (Trans Corp ownership creates oligarch linkage; useful for government messaging interpretation), Kumparan (digital platform, younger audience), Mongabay Indonesia (environmental reporting). Detik.com has high traffic but is aggregation-heavy with limited original analysis.

**Tier 3 (Indicator value only):** Government-aligned media (ANTARA news agency, TVRI) are useful primarily as indicators of official narrative — divergence between ANTARA framing and Tempo/Jakarta Post framing on the same event signals contested terrain. Social media monitoring of pro-government "buzzers" provides early warning of narrative campaigns.

**Key calibration:** Self-censorship affects **80% of Indonesian journalists** per AJI surveys. Absence of coverage on sensitive topics (Papua operations, Danantara finances, 1965 history) is itself an indicator. The ITE Law's criminalization of online expression means that digital sources exercise considerable self-restraint. Tempo's willingness to publish despite intimidation makes it the single most reliable domestic source.

**International analytical sources:** ISEAS-Yusof Ishak Institute (Singapore) produces the most consistent, high-quality Indonesia analysis — its Perspective series and FULCRUM platform are essential. Indonesia at Melbourne (University of Melbourne) provides rigorous academic analysis with Indonesia specialist depth. New Mandala (ANU) offers critical political analysis. East Asia Forum publishes timely Indonesia commentary. Lowy Institute provides Australian-perspective strategic assessment. CSIS Jakarta is well-connected to Indonesian policy elites but may reflect proximity to power.

<strong>20.2 — Event significance thresholds</strong>

**Routine (expected fluctuation):** Individual protest events in fewer than 5 cities; cabinet-level personnel changes among deputy ministers; bilateral MoU signings without binding commitments; quarterly GDP variation within 4.5–5.5% band; individual press freedom incidents.

**Elevated (monitor closely):** Protests spreading to 10+ cities simultaneously; credit rating outlook changes; Constitutional Court decisions on TNI Law or other structural legislation; defense procurement contract signings (vs. LoIs/MoUs); Danantara audit requests or denials; major foreign leader visits with substantive agreements; rupiah crossing 17,000/USD; any Papua access concession.

**Critical (potential structural shift):** Credit rating downgrade to sub-investment grade; mega-coalition party defection; military faction split indicators (public disagreement among senior commanders); protests exceeding August 2025 in scale (50+ cities, fatalities exceeding 15); passage of Foreign Propaganda Bill; Constitutional Court dissolution or restructuring; Prabowo health incident; sustained GDP below 4% for two quarters.

<strong>20.3 — Common misreading patterns</strong>

**Misreading 1: Treating Indonesia's multi-alignment as strategic incoherence.** Western analysts frequently characterize Prabowo's simultaneous engagement with the US, China, Russia, France, and Turkey as contradictory or evidence of foreign policy confusion. This misreads the structural logic. Multi-sourcing is Indonesia's dominant strategy precisely because it maximizes autonomy — the "thousand friends" approach is coherent within its own framework, even if it produces tactical contradictions (the joint China statement, the SPIEF-over-G7 choice). The correct frame is not "alliance choice" but "dependency avoidance."

**Misreading 2: Projecting Western democratic recession frameworks onto Indonesia.** Indonesia is not Hungary or Turkey. The mega-coalition does not rest on ideological polarization but on patronage distribution. The constraint on authoritarian consolidation is not opposition party strength but elite competition — the same oligarchic structure that enables backsliding also prevents any single actor from achieving hegemonic control. Prabowo cannot complete what Orbán completed because he must continuously buy off coalition partners who retain their own resource bases.

**Misreading 3: Underestimating protest capacity because movements lack institutional form.** The five protest waves of 2025 show that leaderless, social media-coordinated movements can achieve scale rapidly without the organizational infrastructure that Western analysts look for. The absence of formal opposition party leadership or labor union coordination does not indicate weakness — it indicates a different mobilization model that is harder to decapitate but also harder to sustain.

**Misreading 4: Assuming BRICS membership signals anti-Western alignment.** Indonesia's simultaneous OECD application undermines this reading. BRICS membership is better understood as status acquisition and institutional diversification rather than alignment choice. The risk is not that Indonesia "joins the anti-Western bloc" but that it becomes a free-rider in all institutions simultaneously, contributing to none while extracting benefits from each.

**Misreading 5: Conflating defense procurement volume with military capability.** Indonesia's \$50+ billion in announced defense deals across 7+ supplier nations will not translate proportionally into combat power. Interoperability challenges, maintenance bottleneck risks, human capital constraints, and the absence of a Defense White Paper since 2015 mean that headline procurement numbers systematically overstate actual capability acquisition. Many agreements remain at LoI/MoU stage; Indonesia's procurement history shows frequent cancellation and renegotiation.

<strong>20.4 — Cross-dossier connection points with other middle powers</strong>

**Australia:** The Jakarta Treaty 2026 is the primary structural connection. Watch for: Australian intelligence assessments of Indonesia-Russia ties (particularly Biak airbase reports), AUKUS reassurance dynamics, Australia-Indonesia economic integration depth (Danantara-Australian investment flows), shared Papua equities (Australia's historical Papua/PNG role creates friction), and the structural tension between Australia's alliance system and Indonesia's multi-alignment.

**Japan:** Indonesia's largest cumulative aid donor and third-largest trade partner with 2,000 companies employing 7.2 million Indonesians. The Mogami-class frigate joint development discussions represent a potential new structural link. Watch for: Japan's FOIP strategy integration, Official Security Assistance flows, competition with China for infrastructure investment, and Japan-Indonesia economic partnership evolution as Japan seeks supply chain diversification from China.

**South Korea:** The KF-21 Boramae program creates a 25+ year structural dependency. The espionage incident (January 2024) and cost-share renegotiation reveal the fragility of defense-industrial partnerships with Indonesia. Watch for: IF-21 delivery timelines, technology transfer depth, and whether the barter-for-commodities model becomes a template for other middle power defense relationships.

**India:** The BrahMos deal (confirmed March 2026) opens a new defense relationship vector. Both countries are BRICS members, both hedge between the US and China, and both are major commodity players. Watch for: deepening Quad-adjacent cooperation dynamics, India-Indonesia maritime security cooperation in the Indian Ocean/Malacca Strait interface, and competition/cooperation in nickel supply chains as India seeks battery material security.

**France:** The most consequential new defense partner. The Rafale and Scorpène programs create 30+ year platform dependencies with full technology transfer commitments. Watch for: France's Indo-Pacific strategy integration, Naval Group's PT Naval Group Nusantara as a local industrial footprint, and whether France displaces the US as Indonesia's preferred Western defense partner — which would mark a major structural shift in the regional security architecture.

**Turkey:** The KAAN deal's viability is the critical question. If the aircraft cannot be freed from US export controls, the agreement functions as diplomatic signaling rather than defense acquisition. Watch for: Turkish engine development timeline, Baykar drone factory construction in Indonesia (Baykar-Republikorp joint venture), and whether Turkey becomes a sustained defense partner or an episodic deal counterpart.

**Brazil:** Both are large, resource-rich, middle-income BRICS members with democratic backsliding trajectories. Brazil participated in Super Garuda Shield 2025. Watch for: BRICS NDB co-borrowing dynamics, commodity market coordination (palm oil/soy competition), and parallel democratic erosion patterns that may create mutual legitimation dynamics.

**South Africa:** BRICS co-membership creates institutional connection. Limited bilateral depth but shared Global South positioning. Watch for: BRICS summit dynamics, NDB governance evolution, and whether the Indonesia-South Africa-Brazil axis develops coherence within BRICS as a democratic middle-power caucus or fragments under domestic authoritarian pressures.
