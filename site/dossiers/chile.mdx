# Order's long shadow over copper, lithium, and constitutional exhaustion

Chile stands at a sharp political inflection point. After four years under President Gabriel Boric — the youngest and most left-wing leader since Salvador Allende — voters elected the far-right José Antonio Kast in December 2025 with 58% of the runoff vote. Mr. Kast takes office on March 11, 2026, inheriting the world's largest copper producer, a critical node in the global lithium supply chain, and a mega-drought now in its fifteenth year. Two failed attempts to replace the Pinochet-era constitution have left the 1980 charter paradoxically strengthened by democratic rejection of alternatives. An intensifying Mapuche insurgency, the penetration of transnational criminal organisations led by Venezuela's Tren de Aragua, and a 25-25 Senate split will constrain the new president's ambitious security agenda. Chile's extreme geography — 4,300 km of Pacific coastline, Antarctic claims larger than its continental territory, and 40% of exports flowing to China — makes it a strategic actor far beyond its 20 million people. The tension between Portalian order and popular upheaval, visible from the 1833 constitution through the 1973 coup to the 2019 estallido social, remains the country's defining fault line.

## I. Political actors and institutions in the Kast transition

**Gabriel Boric** governed from March 2022 as a minority president whose coalition never controlled Congress. Elected at 36—the youngest president in Chilean history—he initially led through the Apruebo Dignidad bloc (Frente Amplio and the Communist Party), then broadened governance to include the center-left Socialismo Democrático alliance (Partido Socialista, PPD, Partido Radical), formalized as Unidad por Chile in November 2022. His approval ratings entered a structural trough early: from roughly 50% at inauguration to **22% by August 2023**, recovering only marginally to around 28% by late 2025. His key appointments reflected ideological breadth—Finance Minister **Mario Marcel**, the reassuringly orthodox former Central Bank president, coexisted with Communist Party spokesperson **Camila Vallejo** and Frente Amplio's **Giorgio Jackson** as Secretary General of the Presidency.

Boric's legislative record was constrained but not negligible. He secured a **reduction of the work week from 45 to 40 hours**, multiple minimum wage increases, a mining royalty law, and the National Lithium Strategy. His signature ambition—replacing the 1980 Constitution—failed twice. The left-leaning 2022 draft was rejected **61.9% to 38.1%** in September 2022 at the highest turnout in Chilean history (85.9%), and the right-leaning 2023 draft was rejected **55.7% to 44.3%** in December 2023. Pension reform, his top domestic priority, was finally passed as Law 21,735 in January 2025, creating a mixed system with rising employer contributions phased over nine years.

The party system has been dramatically reshaped. The traditional center-right coalition **Chile Vamos** (Renovación Nacional, UDI, Evópoli) was eclipsed by the surging **Partido Republicano**, founded in 2019 by José Antonio Kast after two decades in UDI. Kast's trajectory charts the right's radicalization: **7.9%** as an independent in 2017, **27.9%** winning the first-round presidential vote in 2021, dominance in the 2023 Constitutional Council, and **31 Chamber seats** in November 2025—making Republicanos the single largest party. Combined with National Libertarian Johannes Kaiser's 14% first-round share, the far right now anchors Chilean politics. Evópoli and the historic Radical Party (167 years old) face dissolution for failing minimum vote thresholds.

Congress remains deeply fragmented. The **Chamber of Deputies** (155 seats, elected from 28 multi-member districts by proportional representation) saw Unidad por Chile win about 61 seats, the Kast-aligned Cambio por Chile coalition take 42, Chile Vamos with allies capture 34, and Franco Parisi's populist Partido de la Gente secure 20. The **Senate** (50 seats, 8-year staggered terms) reached a **25-25 tie** between left and right blocs after the 2025 renewal. No coalition has held a clear majority in decades, requiring constant cross-aisle negotiation.

The **Constitutional Tribunal** (10 members with 9-year terms, appointed by the president, Supreme Court, Senate, and Chamber) exercises powerful ex ante and ex post constitutional review. Both failed constitutional drafts proposed modifying its role, reflecting persistent controversy over its capacity to block legislation. The **Supreme Court** comprises 21 members (16 career judges, 5 external lawyers) selected by the president from Supreme Court-proposed lists and confirmed by the Senate. In February 2025, the Boric government requested a Venice Commission opinion on creating a Judicial Appointments Council to reduce the Court's self-referential appointment power.

The **Central Bank of Chile**, under Governor **Rosanna Costa**, maintains strong constitutional independence. Its five-member board targets **3% inflation (±1%)**. After peaking at 11.25% in mid-2023, the monetary policy rate was cut progressively to **4.5% by December 2025**, with inflation declining from 12.8% in 2022 to around 4% by late 2025. The Bank estimates trend GDP growth at a modest **1.8%** for 2025–2034.

Chile's **Armed Forces** total about 170,000 personnel across the Army (46,350, operating 170+ Leopard 2A4 tanks), Navy (19,800 including Marines, 66 surface vessels, 4 submarines), and Air Force (11,050). Defense spending reached **\$5.1 billion in 2024**—among the highest ratios in Latin America. The 2005 constitutional reforms removed the military's role as "guarantors of institutional order" and gave the president power to dismiss commanders. The old Ley Reservada del Cobre, which channeled 10% of copper revenues directly to the military, was reformed in 2019.

The **Carabineros de Chile**, the national police force of about 37,000 officers, remain in institutional crisis. Director General **Ricardo Yáñez**, who oversaw anti-protest operations during the 2019 estallido social, faces formal criminal charges for human rights violations including the **427 eye injuries** inflicted by police. Organized crime corruption incidents rose 690% in 2024. The force's transfer from the Defense Ministry to the Interior Ministry in 2011 and the establishment of a Human Rights Directorate have not resolved structural problems.

Among **key economic actors**, the state copper company **CODELCO** (world's largest copper miner, **1,442 ktons in 2024**, targeting 1.7 million tonnes by 2030) operates under Chairman Máximo Pacheco with a net financial debt of \$22.3 billion. CODELCO's new lithium joint venture with SQM, "Nova Andino Litio," extends Salar de Atacama operations to 2060. The **Luksic family** (net worth \$25–29 billion, controlling Quiñenco, Antofagasta Minerals, Banco de Chile, and CCU) is Chile's wealthiest. The **Matte Group** controls forestry giant CMPC and energy company Colbún, while the **Angelini Group** controls fuel distributor Copec and forestry company Arauco—both operating extensively on lands claimed by Mapuche communities.

The **Mapuche conflict** represents Chile's most persistent security challenge. The **Coordinadora Arauco-Malleco (CAM)**, founded in 1998, pursues territorial autonomy through arson attacks on forestry equipment, land occupations, and sabotage. Its leader **Héctor Llaitul** was sentenced to **23 years in prison in May 2024**. The Chamber of Deputies declared CAM and allied groups (Resistencia Mapuche Malleco, Weichán Auka Mapu) illegal terrorist organizations in May 2022. Boric's Presidential Commission for Peace and Understanding delivered 21 recommendations in May 2025, but the incoming Kast administration has promised further militarization.

Externally, **China is Chile's largest trading partner** (bilateral trade of **\$61.7 billion in 2024**, with 40% of Chilean exports going to China). The **United States** maintains a duty-free FTA since 2004, though Trump-era 10% tariffs imposed in April 2025 created new friction. Chile is a founding member of the **Pacific Alliance** (with Mexico, Colombia, Peru), a member of **APEC** (cancelled its 2019 hosting due to protests), and signed an upgraded Advanced Framework Agreement with the EU entering force in February 2025.

## II. Formation traumas that structure Chilean politics

Chile's founding mythology begins not with revolution but with order. **Bernardo O'Higgins**—illegitimate son of an Irish-born Spanish governor, educated in London, and influenced by the independence conspiracies of the Lautaro Lodge—declared independence on **February 12, 1818**, after José de San Martín's Army of the Andes crossed the cordillera in a legendary 20-day march that killed 2,000 of its 5,000 men to cold and altitude. The decisive **Battle of Maipú** on April 5, 1818, saw about 2,000 royalists killed and 3,000 captured. O'Higgins governed as Supreme Director with authoritarian instincts until forced into Peruvian exile in 1823. His legacy is paradoxical: revered as the Father of the Nation yet toppled for authoritarianism, establishing an early template in which Chileans celebrate strong leaders but eventually reject their overreach.

The deeper founding principle is **Diego Portales's** authoritarian conservatism of the 1830s. Portales, a merchant who never held the presidency but wielded decisive power as Minister of War, Interior, and Foreign Relations, authored the concept of the "peso de la noche"—the idea that social order in Chile rested on the passivity of the masses and colonial inertia, not democratic legitimacy. His famous letter of July 16, 1832, stated that "the almost general tendency of the masses to repose is the guarantee of public tranquility." The **1833 Constitution** he inspired endured for 91 years, creating a strong executive with restricted suffrage and Catholicism as the state religion. Portales was assassinated in 1837, but his principle that order trumps liberty became the central tension of Chilean governance—a lineage running directly from Portales through Pinochet. Historian Alfredo Jocelyn-Holt's concept of the "peso de la noche" continues to frame debates about whether Chilean stability reflects genuine democratic achievement or inertial authoritarianism.

The **War of the Pacific (1879–1884)** forged Chile's military identity and its most potent national mythology. When Bolivia violated an 1874 treaty by taxing Chilean nitrate companies, Chile occupied Antofagasta on February 14, 1879, and declared war on both Bolivia and its secret ally Peru on April 5. The **Battle of Iquique on May 21, 1879**, produced Chile's paramount national hero: Captain **Arturo Prat**, who boarded the vastly superior Peruvian ironclad Huáscar in a suicidal charge and was killed by rifle fire. Scholar William Sater called him a "secular saint"—his action was a tactical catastrophe but an unparalleled propaganda victory. May 21 is a national holiday; Prat's face appears on the 10,000-peso note; "Arturo Prat" is the most common street name in Chile. After capturing the Huáscar at the Battle of Angamos, Chile took Lima in January 1881. The **Treaty of Ancón (1883)** gave Chile the nitrate-rich province of Tarapacá; the **1904 treaty** with Bolivia permanently ceded the Antofagasta littoral, stripping Bolivia of its entire 380-kilometer coastline. In 2018, the ICJ ruled **12–3** that Chile has no legal obligation to negotiate sovereign sea access for Bolivia—but Bolivia's claim remains a constitutional imperative.

The **1891 Civil War** created a lasting precedent for executive-legislative conflict. President José Manuel Balmaceda attempted to govern without congressional budget approval; Congress, backed by the Navy and Prussian-trained junior officers, defeated his Army forces at the battles of Concón and Placilla. Balmaceda committed suicide on September 19, 1891, the day after his term legally expired. The war ushered in a **Parliamentary Republic (1891–1925)** in which Congress dominated the executive—a system that ended only with the 1925 Constitution.

The **September 11, 1973 coup** against Salvador Allende remains the defining trauma. Allende, elected in 1970 as the first democratically elected Marxist president in Latin American history, pursued nationalization of copper mines, banking, and industry while inflation soared to 700%. The CIA spent **\$8 million on covert operations** to destabilize his government (Church Committee findings, 1976), funding opposition media, truckers' strikes, and maintaining contacts with military plotters. On September 11, Air Force jets bombed La Moneda palace; Allende died inside, confirmed by 2011 autopsy as suicide. General **Augusto Pinochet** set up a junta that suspended the constitution, dissolved Congress, and banned political parties. The **Rettig Commission** documented **3,197 killed or disappeared**; the **Valech Commission** identified about **40,018 victims** of political imprisonment and torture, with an estimated 200,000 forced into exile. The DINA secret police under Colonel Manuel Contreras operated torture centers including Villa Grimaldi (4,500 prisoners, 241 executed or disappeared) and participated in Operation Condor, assassinating opponents across borders—including Orlando Letelier in Washington, D.C. on September 21, 1976.

The **"two Chiles" narrative** endures. A May 2023 poll found **36% of Chileans believed the coup was "necessary"** and nearly 40% credited Pinochet with "modernizing" the country, while 60% showed no interest in the 50th anniversary commemoration. This divide—Allende as democratic martyr versus reckless Marxist, Pinochet as economic savior versus mass murderer—structures every political contest. The Museo de la Memoria y los Derechos Humanos, inaugurated in 2010 by Michelle Bachelet (herself detained and tortured under the dictatorship), houses testimonies recognized by UNESCO. Villa Grimaldi Peace Park, opened in 1997, was the first such memorial in South America. The 1978 amnesty decree shielding perpetrators from the worst years of repression remains unrepealed.

The **2019 estallido social** constituted a new formation trauma. Triggered by a 30-peso metro fare increase on October 18, it exposed three decades of accumulated grievance over inequality, inadequate pensions, market-driven healthcare, and privatized education—the slogan "No son 30 pesos, son 30 años" (It's not 30 pesos, it's 30 years) indicted the entire post-dictatorship model. An estimated **1.2 million people** marched in Santiago on October 25, 2019—the largest demonstration in Chilean history. Carabineros responded with extreme force: **36 deaths, 2,500+ injuries, and 427 eye injuries** from rubber bullets. President Piñera's declaration "We are at war" further inflamed the crisis. The November 15 Agreement for Social Peace launched the constitutional process, and a **78.3% majority** voted to rewrite the constitution in October 2020. But both resulting texts were rejected—the left-wing version in 2022, the right-wing version in 2023—leaving the **1980 Pinochet-era Constitution (as amended) still in force**. Constitutional fatigue has set in. The double rejection has been interpreted as evidence that Chile's electorate is more moderate than either activist camp assumed, with "withdrawn citizens" mobilized by compulsory voting rejecting what they perceived as extremism from both directions.

## III. Geography as strategic destiny

Chile's extreme geography functions as both asset and constraint. The country stretches **4,270 km** north to south but averages only **177 km** wide—at its narrowest, near Puerto Natales, just 15 km. This creates what the Chilean Navy itself describes as an "island": bounded by the Atacama Desert (north), the Andes (east), the Pacific (west), and Antarctic ice (south). The country spans 18 Köppen climate zones from tropical desert to subantarctic tundra. Its **6,435 km coastline** and Exclusive Economic Zone of about **3.6–5 million km²** (including island territories) exceed its continental area six times over.

The **Atacama Desert**, the world's driest non-polar desert extending roughly 1,000 km through the Norte Grande, contains Chile's most strategically valuable resources. Chile holds the world's **largest copper reserves (180 million tonnes)** and is the largest producer. The **Salar de Atacama**, the world's largest actively mined salt flat, holds the planet's highest-concentration lithium brines—Chile possesses about **36–52% of global lithium reserves** (estimates vary). Mining operations pump roughly 2,000 liters per second of groundwater from the salar, causing measurable subsidence. Water scarcity is existential for northern mining: desalinated and reused water now meets **40% of northern mining demand**, projected to exceed 65% by 2032. Antofagasta, a city of 425,000, became the first major Chilean city fully supplied by desalinated water in 2025.

The **Andes** form the world's longest mountain barrier along Chile's 6,691 km border with Argentina—the world's third-longest international boundary. Ojos del Salado (6,893 m), the highest volcano on Earth, sits on this border. Despite over 40 border crossings, most are seasonal and unpaved. The critical **Paso Los Libertadores** (Cristo Redentor tunnel at 3,175 m) carries the bulk of Chile-Argentina commercial traffic but closes frequently in winter from snow, creating a strategic chokepoint vulnerability. The proposed Agua Negra tunnel (\$1.5–1.6 billion, two parallel 13.9 km bores) would create an all-season Atlantic-Pacific corridor but remains significantly delayed.

Chile claims **1,250,257 km²** of Antarctic territory—larger than its continental area—overlapping with Argentine and British claims (all frozen under the Antarctic Treaty). Chile maintains **13 Antarctic bases** (4 permanent, 5 seasonal, 4 shelters), the most prominent being Base Presidente Eduardo Frei Montalva on King George Island, adjacent to Villa Las Estrellas, the only civilian Antarctic settlement. Punta Arenas serves as the departure point for 22 countries' Antarctic operations, and Chile's new icebreaker Almirante Viel completed its maiden Antarctic voyage in 2024–2025. Chile presents itself as a "tricontinental nation" (South America, Antarctica, Oceania via Easter Island).

**Easter Island (Rapa Nui)**, located 3,512 km from the mainland—the most remote inhabited island on Earth—massively extends Chile's Pacific presence. Its airport runway, extended by NASA in 1987, and its EEZ projection give Chile strategic Pacific depth. The Rapa Nui Parliament has pursued decolonization, sending a formal self-governance request under a free association model to President Boric in August 2024.

Chile sits squarely on the Ring of Fire. The **1960 Valdivia earthquake** (magnitude 9.5) remains the strongest ever recorded globally; the **2010 Maule earthquake** (8.8) caused \$30 billion in losses and exposed tsunami warning gaps. Chile enforces strict seismic building codes (NCh433 standard), and its iterative resilience approach is considered a global model.

The **Strait of Magellan** (600 km, entirely under Chilean sovereignty) and **Beagle Channel** (240 km, shared with Argentina in its eastern portion) are, together with the Drake Passage, the only navigable routes between the Atlantic and Pacific around South America. The 1978 Beagle Channel crisis nearly produced an Argentine invasion; papal mediation by John Paul II averted war, leading to the 1984 Treaty of Peace and Friendship ratified by Argentine referendum at 82.6%.

Central Chile's **mega-drought**, now in its fifteenth year, is the longest in 700–1,000 years. National surface water availability dropped roughly 20% between 2009 and 2022. Santiago prepared four-tier water rationing plans in 2021–2022. The emblematic case of Petorca, where avocado monoculture dried entire rivers, produced the slogan "No es sequía, es saqueo" (It's not drought, it's plunder). The 1981 Water Code, which uniquely privatized water rights as tradeable property (90% held by private companies), was reformed in 2022 to cap new rights at 30 years and recognize the human right to water.

## IV. How empires built the Chilean state

Chile's institutional architecture reflects distinctive imperial layering. The **Captaincy General of Chile**, founded in 1541 by Pedro de Valdivia and formally subordinate to the Viceroyalty of Peru, created a centralized administrative state with a social hierarchy of peninsulares, criollos, mestizos, and indigenous peoples. By the late colonial period, **67% of regular army officers and 77% of militia officers** were Chilean-born criollos, foreshadowing the independence movement. Basque immigrants mixed with the Castilian aristocracy to form a political-economic elite that persists today.

The **Arauco War (1536–1883)**, one of the longest conflicts in world history at roughly 347 years, distinguished Chile from every other Latin American colonial experience. The Mapuche maintained political independence from both the Inca Empire and Spain, defending a frontier along the Biobío River formalized in the remarkable **Parliament of Quilín (1641)**—the only instance in which the Spanish Crown formally recognized indigenous sovereignty in the Western Hemisphere. This frontier required a permanent standing army financed by Peru, giving Chile a uniquely militarized colonial culture. Spain called it "Indian Flanders." The 30-plus parlamentos (formal diplomatic treaties) between Mapuche leaders and Spanish officials resembled European treaty-making, and Ercilla's 1569 epic poem *La Araucana* extolled Mapuche martial virtues so powerfully that Cervantes praised it. Chile's military self-image was forged in this crucible.

**Andrés Bello's Civil Code of 1855**, blending Spanish colonial law with the Napoleonic Code, became the most influential codification in Latin American legal history—adopted wholesale by six countries and serving as the primary source for several others. Its rationalist, code-based structure, emphasizing systematic statutory authority over judicial precedent, continues to define Chilean governance.

The **hacienda system** concentrated land ownership so severely that by 1965, **730 estates controlled half of Chile's agricultural land** while 66% of the agricultural workforce depended on the latifundium system. The inquilino (tenant farmer) system approximated serfdom. Eduardo Frei's 1967 agrarian reform and Allende's acceleration expropriated roughly 10 million hectares across 5,809 estates. Pinochet's counter-reform returned about 30% to former owners, auctioned 30% to private buyers, and parceled the remainder into individual plots—which capital-poor peasants often sold. By 1997, land concentration was **more extreme than in 1965**.

**British economic imperialism** dominated the 19th century. After the War of the Pacific, John Thomas North—"the Nitrate King"—and firms like Antony Gibbs & Sons controlled the nitrate industry that constituted over **70% of Chile's exports by 1913**. Valparaíso became virtually a British colony (32,000 English residents), with British capital funding railways (7,658 km of track at peak), banks, and commerce. President Balmaceda's confrontation with British nitrate interests contributed to the 1891 Civil War. British influence collapsed after World War I and the Haber-Bosch synthetic nitrate process destroyed the industry.

**German influence** operated on two tracks. The 1845 Law of Selective Immigration brought about 6,000–11,000 German settlers to the Lake District region (Valdivia, Osorno, Puerto Montt), creating a prosperous European settler zone with an estimated **800,000 descendants** today. Simultaneously, Prussian Captain **Emil Körner**, contracted in 1885, Prussianized the Chilean Army—reforming armaments (Krupp artillery), instituting Latin America's first conscription system, establishing the War Academy on the German Kriegsakademie model, and importing **36 Prussian officers** by the mid-1890s. Chilean military parades still feature the goose-step, Stahlhelm helmets, and Pickelhaube at the Military School.

**American hegemony** defined the 20th century. By the late 1950s, Anaconda Copper and Kennecott controlled over **80% of Chile's copper production**, with Anaconda's Chilean operations generating \$4.05 of its total \$4.31 earnings per share. When Allende nationalized copper in **July 1971** by unanimous congressional vote, Nixon told CIA Director Helms to "save Chile" with "\$10 million available, full-time job" and to "make the economy scream." The Church Committee documented \$8 million in covert spending, CIA funding of El Mercurio, and the CIA-linked assassination attempt on constitutionalist General René Schneider.

The **Chicago Boys**—about 100 Chilean economists trained at the University of Chicago under Milton Friedman and Arnold Harberger through a 1955 State Department program—implemented what Friedman called the "Miracle of Chile." Led by **Sergio de Castro** and **José Piñera**, they introduced shock therapy (GDP contracted 12.9% in 1975), privatized the pension system (AFPs, 1981), deregulated financial markets, reduced tariffs to a uniform 6%, and privatized state enterprises—many sold to regime-connected buyers at below-market prices. Corrupt privatization transferred wealth estimated at **40% of GDP** to connected groups. Income inequality spiked, though sustained growth (averaging 7% from 1985–1997) eventually made Chile Latin America's richest country per capita.

The **1980 Constitution**, drafted primarily by 27-year-old jurist **Jaime Guzmán** at Pinochet's direction, was designed explicitly to constrain future governments. Guzmán stated: "Even if our opponents manage to gain access to power, they should be constitutionally constrained to follow not so different a path than the one we ourselves would like to follow." Key mechanisms included the **binominal electoral system** (allowing the right to capture 50% of seats with 30% of votes), designated senators, senators-for-life, two-thirds quorums for constitutional amendments, and an economic constitution protecting market prerogatives. The 2005 reforms under President Lagos eliminated designated senators and gave the president power to remove military commanders, but the structural economic framework persisted. After both replacement attempts failed (2022, 2023), the 1980 charter—amended over 60 times but structurally intact—remains the fundamental law.

## V. The cleavage structure beneath the surface

Chile's ethnic composition is relatively homogeneous by Latin American standards. Genetic admixture studies indicate about **51.6% European, 42.1% Amerindian, and 6.3% African** ancestry in the general population. But this average conceals a critical pattern: upper-class Chileans register significantly higher European ancestry while lower socioeconomic strata show stronger Amerindian genetic contribution, meaning class and ethnicity are structurally intertwined.

The **Mapuche** constitute Chile's primary ethnic cleavage. The 2024 Census recorded **1,623,073 self-identified Mapuche** (8.8% of the population, down from 12.8% in 2017). Despite popular association with the rural south, the largest Mapuche population (491,213) now lives in metropolitan Santiago. The La Araucanía region has the highest proportion at 34.5% and is consistently Chile's poorest region. Mapuche poverty rates run roughly double the national average (**30.8% versus 19.9%**). Mapudungun, the Mapuche language, is classified as endangered by UNESCO, with only about 10% of self-identified Mapuche actively speaking it and intergenerational transmission critically low (2.4% of urban speakers use it with children).

The **Araucanía conflict** represents the territorial dimension of this cleavage. After Chile's 1861–1883 military occupation ended 350 years of Mapuche independence, land was reduced to roughly 3,000 small reservations. Pinochet's 1979 Decree-Law 2568 forced division of communal properties, while Decree 701 subsidized massive pine and eucalyptus plantations—operated by **Forestal Arauco (Angelini Group)** and **CMPC/Mininco (Matte Group)**—on claimed Mapuche lands. These monoculture plantations absorb far more groundwater than native forests, devastating subsistence farming. The conflict has produced an escalating cycle of arson (forestry machinery, trucks, buildings), land occupations, and militarized state response. The arrest of CAM founder Héctor Llaitul in August 2022 and his sentencing to 23 years in May 2024 has not diminished violence; newer, more decentralized groups like Weichán Auka Mapu are less disciplined and better armed than CAM.

Other indigenous groups include the **Aymara** (178,637 in 2024, concentrated in the far north), the rapidly re-identifying **Diaguita** (153,231, up sharply from 88,000 in 2017), and smaller populations of Atacameño, Quechua, Rapa Nui, Kawésqar (near extinction), and Yagán. Chile remains the **only country in Latin America without constitutional recognition** of indigenous peoples.

**Immigration** has emerged as a transformative new cleavage since about 2015. Chile's foreign-born population surged from roughly 2% to an estimated **10% by 2024–2025**, driven by Venezuelan (532,000+), Haitian (\~180,000), and Colombian (147,000+) immigration. Haitian immigration carries an acute racial dimension—as visibly Black immigrants in a predominantly mestizo society, Haitians face discrimination compounded by language barriers. The September 2021 burning of a makeshift immigrant camp in Iquique marked a nadir of anti-immigrant hostility. Immigration dominated the 2025 election: Kast promised mass deportations, a "Border Shield" of walls and ditches, and a Bukele-style mega-prison, while even the Communist candidate Jeannette Jara adopted tough law-and-order messaging. Polling showed **92% of Chileans supporting stricter immigration controls**.

**Class remains the dominant political cleavage**, more than ethnicity or religion. Chile's **Gini coefficient of 43.0** (2022) has declined from 56.4 in 1995 but remains among the highest in the OECD. The top 1% earns about **25% of pre-tax income and owns nearly 50% of total wealth**. Geographic segregation in Santiago is extreme: the northeastern "barrio alto" (Las Condes, Vitacura, Lo Barnechea) concentrates the wealthy elite, while southern peripheries (La Pintana, Puente Alto) are predominantly low-income. Pinochet's regime forcibly relocated about 180,000 residents from middle-class comunas to the southern outskirts. The three-tier educational system (non-subsidized private schools for the elite 8%, voucher schools for the middle 56%, municipal schools for the poor 36%) reproduces inequality generationally: the OECD found socioeconomic background explained educational performance variance in Chile "stronger than anywhere in the OECD." Health stratification mirrors this pattern: public FONASA covers 78–80% of the population while private ISAPRE insurance, receiving 54% of social insurance revenues, serves the wealthiest 16–19%.

**Santiago centralism** dominates national life. Greater Santiago's 7.1 million residents constitute roughly 40% of the population and captured **69% of GDP growth** between 2000–2013. Chile is "one of the most centralized countries of the OECD" and the sole OECD nation where local borrowing is illegal. The first direct election of regional governors occurred only in 2021.

The **religious landscape** is shifting rapidly. Catholic identification plunged from over 90% through most of the 20th century to **53.7% in the 2024 Census** and possibly as low as 43.8% by some survey measures. Evangelical/Pentecostal Christianity has grown to roughly 16–19%, concentrated among lower socioeconomic groups and indigenous populations (38% of indigenous people are Evangelical). Meanwhile, **25.7% reported no religious affiliation** in the 2024 Census, making Chile the second least religious country in Latin America after Uruguay. This secularization carries political weight: observant Christians (especially Evangelicals) align increasingly with the right, while the growing irreligious population skews left. Kast's base unites traditional Catholics and Evangelicals on socially conservative values.

## VI. An economy built on copper and constrained by concentration

Chile's economy is a paradox of openness and dependency. With **35 trade agreements covering 64 economies representing 86% of world GDP**, it is Latin America's most open economy. Yet its export structure remains dominated by a single commodity: copper accounts for roughly **50% of total exports and 20% of government revenue**. Chile produced **5.5 million metric tonnes of copper in 2024** (about 24% of global output), holding the world's largest reserves at 180 million tonnes. The tension between state-owned CODELCO (1,442 ktons in 2024, world's largest producer) and private miners (BHP's Escondida alone produces over 1 million tonnes) defines mining politics. CODELCO's \$22.3 billion net debt and declining ore grades (averaging 0.7% versus a historical 1.2%) represent structural challenges; BHP is investing \$10.8 billion to modernize its Chilean operations.

**Lithium** is the emerging strategic asset. Chile holds the world's largest reserves and is the second-largest producer (**49,000 tonnes in 2024**). Only two companies—SQM and Albemarle—extract from the Salar de Atacama. Boric's April 2023 National Lithium Strategy set up state majority participation through a CODELCO-SQM joint venture extending operations to 2060, with the state receiving **70–85% of operating margin** from new production. A parallel deal with Rio Tinto targets the Maricunga salar for \$900 million. But lithium carbonate prices collapsed from 2022 peaks to roughly \$14,000/tonne in 2024, dampening revenue projections. Tianqi Lithium of China holds a **22.16% stake in SQM**, adding geopolitical complexity.

Chile's dependency on China as a trade partner is extreme. China has been Chile's largest trading partner for over 12 consecutive years, with **bilateral trade of \$61.7 billion in 2024** and 40% of total Chilean exports (\$37.6 billion) flowing to China. About half of Chilean copper and **71% of lithium exports** go to China. The relationship is deepened by a 2006 FTA (expanded 2017), a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership since 2016, and BRI participation since 2018. This concentration creates vulnerability to Chinese economic slowdowns, policy shifts, or geopolitical disruptions.

Macroeconomic indicators paint a picture of moderate prosperity with persistent inequality. **GDP reached about \$330–335 billion (nominal) in 2024**, with per capita PPP of **\$34,789**—the highest in Latin America. Growth recovered to 2.6% in 2024 after near-stagnation (0.2%) in 2023, with 2025 forecasts around 2.0–2.5%. Inflation spiked to **12.8% in 2022** before declining to around 4% by late 2025. Unemployment hovers around **8.5%**. The poverty rate stands at 5% by international metrics but **17.3% using the more demanding CASEN methodology**, with multidimensional poverty at 20.9%.

The **AFP pension system**, set up in 1981 as the world's first fully privatized national pension scheme, has become a political flashpoint. Average replacement rates have fallen to roughly **44%** against an original 75% target. The gender gap is severe: women with elementary education face projected 11% replacement rates versus 47% for comparable men. The January 2025 reform (Law 21,735) creates a mixed system, raising **employer contributions from 1.5% to 8.5%** phased over nine years, creating a new public Autonomous Pension Protection Fund, and introducing gender gap compensation benefiting about one million women.

Chile's fiscal framework rests on a **structural balance rule** adopted in 2001 and enhanced in 2024 with a debt ceiling. Central government debt reached about **41–42% of GDP in 2024**—below the Latin American average of 54.6% but up sharply from 3.3% in 2008. Two sovereign wealth funds—the **Economic and Social Stabilization Fund (ESSF, \$7.5 billion)** and the **Pension Reserve Fund (PRF, \$6.5 billion)**—provide fiscal buffers ranked among the world's most transparent. Boric's comprehensive tax reform, aiming to raise revenue equal to 3.6% of GDP, was rejected in March 2023. Only the mining royalty component passed, generating an estimated **\$1.5 billion annually**—far below original targets.

Chile's financial sector is Latin America's most stable. The top banks—BCI, Santander Chile, Banco de Chile (Luksic), and state-owned BancoEstado—operate in a market valued at about \$256 billion, supervised by the unified financial regulator CMF. The Santiago Stock Exchange is Latin America's fourth-largest with **240+ listed companies** and roughly \$250 billion market capitalization. Informality is low by regional standards at **27–30%** of employment, compared to 50–60%+ in most Latin American peers.

## VII. Infrastructure pathways and their strategic implications

Chile's infrastructure reflects both the challenges and opportunities of its extreme geography. The **Pan-American Highway (Ruta 5)** runs 3,364 km as the nation's transportation spine, privatized through toll concessions with operators including Spain's Abertis/VíasChile and, notably, **China Railway Construction Corporation**, which acquired 364 km of concessions (Chillán-Talca and Collipulli-Temuco) for \$1.45 billion—the first PPP awarded to a Chinese company in Chile. The **Santiago Metro** operates 7 lines, 143 stations, and 149 km of track, carrying **2.4 million passengers daily**. The \$2.53 billion Line 7 (24.8 km, 21 stations) is 30% complete with 2028 projected operations. By 2033, the system plans 199 stations and 231.5 km—55% growth that would make it Latin America's longest metro.

Port infrastructure is undergoing transformation driven by competition with Peru's Chinese-financed Chancay port. **San Antonio**, Chile's busiest port (1.8 million TEUs in 2024, \$42.8 billion in trade), is building a **\$4–4.45 billion Outer Port** with 6 million TEU capacity at full build-out, targeting first vessel operations by 2036. Santiago's **Arturo Merino Benítez Airport** opened a new \$990 million international terminal doubling capacity to 30+ million passengers, with a \$4 billion master plan to reach 84 million by 2050.

Chile's energy transition is among the developing world's most aggressive. **Renewables reached 70% of electricity generation in 2024**, with wind and solar hitting a record 33% (surpassing 40% in December 2024). The Atacama Desert has the world's highest solar irradiance, with capacity factors exceeding 30%—double the global average. Chile is phasing out coal faster than any other developing economy, targeting full elimination by 2040, with roughly 700 MW withdrawn from the northern grid in early 2026. But the transition creates an acute **curtailment crisis**: **5,642 GWh of solar and wind were curtailed in 2024**—19% of all wind and solar output, representing \$562 million in losses since January 2022. The fundamental cause is insufficient north-to-south transmission.

The **\$1.48 billion Kimal-Lo Aguirre HVDC line**—Chile's first direct current transmission, spanning 1,346 km from Antofagasta to Santiago at 3,000 MW capacity—is the critical infrastructure response, with **China Southern Power Grid** as a consortium partner alongside Transelec and ISA InterChile. Environmental approval came in November 2025, with operations expected in 2029. Two LNG import terminals—**GNL Quintero** (Valparaíso region, operational 2009) and **GNL Mejillones** (Antofagasta region, operational 2010)—provide energy security given Chile's lack of domestic gas production.

Chile's **green hydrogen ambitions** matter globally. The National Strategy targets 1 million tonnes annually by 2030 at less than \$1.50/kg. Seventy-three projects have been announced, led by HIF Global's **Haru Oni** demonstration plant in Magallanes (producing e-gasoline for Porsche) and TotalEnergies' **H2 Magallanes** mega-project valued at \$16.3 billion. Magallanes' extraordinary wind capacity factors (up to 70%) approach baseload equivalent, making the region potentially the world's cheapest green hydrogen source.

Digital infrastructure positions Chile as Latin America's connectivity hub. Multiple submarine cables serve the country, including Google's **Curie** (2020, connecting to the US), the **Mistral/SPSC** (7,300 km Pacific coast cable, 132 Tbps capacity), and the transformative **Humboldt** cable (14,800 km trans-Pacific connecting Valparaíso to Sydney via French Polynesia, expected 2026–2027)—the first submarine cable in the South Pacific. Google operates Latin America's largest data center in Quilicura (Santiago), and Microsoft opened its first Chilean data center region in June 2025.

The **water infrastructure crisis** is perhaps the most consequential. Chile's 24 operational desalination plants (combined capacity 10,583 liters/second) serve primarily the mining sector, with Antofagasta becoming the first major Chilean city fully supplied by desalinated water. Mining's seawater use is projected to increase 167% by 2032. The 2022 Water Code reform capped new water rights at 30 years and recognized the human right to water, but structural shortages in central Chile remain unresolved, with agriculture consuming 72% of freshwater.

China's infrastructure footprint is growing across multiple sectors: CRCC highway concessions (\$1.45 billion), China Southern Power Grid in the Kimal-Lo Aguirre HVDC line, Tianqi Lithium's SQM stake, and Huawei's 5G equipment. Traditional Western investment still dominates aviation (Groupe ADP/VINCI), metro rolling stock (Alstom), and mining operations (BHP, Anglo American, Albemarle), but the strategic trend is toward deepening Chinese involvement in critical infrastructure—highways, power transmission, and critical minerals.

<strong>Structural tensions that will define the Kast era</strong>

Chile enters the Kast presidency on March 11, 2026, with a distinctive set of structural tensions that no single administration can resolve. The **commodity paradox**—world-leading copper and lithium reserves that generate wealth but deepen dependency on China and global price cycles—will only intensify as the energy transition accelerates demand for both metals. The **constitutional impasse**, with the Pinochet-era charter now carrying paradoxical democratic legitimacy through double rejection of alternatives, means institutional reform will proceed incrementally rather than through foundational rewriting. The **Mapuche conflict**, now featuring more decentralized and better-armed groups than the original CAM, faces likely escalation under Kast's promised militarization, with the underlying land dispossession and forestry industry dynamics unchanged. The **mega-drought**, driven by climate change and a water rights system only partially reformed, threatens both Santiago's water security and the agricultural heartland. Immigration, the issue that most propelled Kast's victory, will test whether mass deportations and border fortification can be operationalized without disrupting the labor force that now contributes 10% of GDP. And the broader Latin American rightward trend that Kast represents—fusing Pinochetist economic orthodoxy with populist anti-immigration and anti-crime messaging—will be tested against the deep class inequality that the 2019 estallido revealed as Chile's most volatile fault line. The "weight of the night" that Portales observed in 1832 has grown lighter; what replaces it remains Chile's open question.

## VIII. Environmental and climatic structure

<strong>The mega-drought rewrites Chile's hydrological baseline</strong>

Chile's mega-drought, which began in **2010**, is the longest-lasting drought in the country in 700–1,000 years, according to University of Chile climatologist René D. Garreaud. As of 2026, it has persisted for about 16 years. National surface water availability dropped **\~20%** between 2009 and 2022 (OECD). Santiago recorded just 82 mm of precipitation in 2019 — a 75% deficit — and 113 mm in 2021. Streamflow deficits of **47–76%** have been measured in the Coquimbo and Valparaíso basins, and river flows in some central basins have declined by up to 90%. Laguna de Aculeo, 50 km south of Santiago, dried up entirely in 2018. By 2020, nearly **500,000 Chileans** depended on emergency water tanker trucks. Chile now ranks 16th of 164 countries globally for water stress.

In April 2022, Chile announced an unprecedented water rationing plan for Santiago's about 6 million residents, operated by **Aguas Andinas** (a subsidiary of Veolia serving \~40% of the Chilean population). The plan employs a four-tier alert system (Green, Preventive, Yellow, Red) keyed to Maipo and Mapocho river levels. Under a Red Alert, rotating water cuts of up to 24 hours would affect \~1.7 million residents. Aguas Andinas is investing in new reservoirs, wells, and the "Pirque Mega Ponds" project to add 6 m³/s capacity and extend potable water autonomy to 48 hours.

In the mining regions, groundwater depletion is severe. In the **Salar de Atacama**, groundwater levels have fallen more than 10 meters in 15 years, and the salar is sinking 1–2 cm annually from lithium brine extraction. Antofagasta became the first major Chilean city to rely entirely on desalinated seawater in 2025, with over 1,400 l/s capacity.

<strong>Transboundary water disputes and the Silala ICJ ruling</strong>

The most important transboundary water case — *Dispute over the Status and Use of the Waters of the Silala* (Chile v. Bolivia) — concluded at the International Court of Justice on **December 1, 2022**. Chile filed the case in 2016. The Court found that both parties agreed the Silala constitutes an "international watercourse" governed by customary international law, affirming equitable and reasonable utilization rights — Chile's primary objective. The Court rejected Chile's claim that Bolivia breached notification obligations and rejected Bolivia's counterclaims about enhanced flow. Judge Peter Tomka wrote that the judgment "most likely comes as a surprise to the Parties... it decides almost nothing." Neither country is party to the 1997 Convention on the Law of Non-Navigational Uses of International Watercourses. The broader Chile-Bolivia relationship remains shaped by the War of the Pacific (1879–1884): Bolivia lost its coastline, and the ICJ ruled in October 2018 (12 to 3) that Chile had no legal obligation to negotiate sovereign sea access.

The **Lauca River** (originating in Chile, flowing into Bolivia's Coipasa Lake) remains a longstanding but lower-intensity dispute. Chile-Argentina transboundary water relations are managed through bilateral commissions governing shared Andean watersheds, without major active disputes.

<strong>Glaciers are retreating and climate projections point to continued drying</strong>

Chile holds about **23,708 km²** of glacierized area — roughly 80% of the Southern Andes' total ice — catalogued in the DGA's Public Glacier Inventory (2014, updated 2022). Glacier retreat is accelerating. Cerro El Plomo glaciers near Santiago lost **17% ± 6%** of their area between 2000 and 2025. The Olivares Alpha glacier shrank by 28% between 2004 and 2014, with mining-related air pollutants responsible for an estimated 82% of its retreat. In Patagonia, the Greve glacier lost 10 km² and the Marinelli glacier retreated 15 km between 1913 and 2011. The Echaurren Norte glacier, the DGA's reference glacier monitored since 1975, has shown sustained mass-balance losses since 2010.

Climate projections are stark. Under a high-emissions scenario (Above 3°C), Chile faces an overall **16% decline** in precipitation, with five regions exceeding 30% decline. Central Chile is projected for continued drying through the 21st century under CMIP6 models. The IEA projects hydropower capacity factor losses of 14–25%. The snow-rain transition line is projected to shift upward **400–600 meters** under RCP8.5, increasing landslide and flood risk. Chile shattered its national heat record on January 26, 2017, when Cauquenes reached **45.0°C**.

<strong>The 2024 Valparaíso megafire was among the world's deadliest</strong>

Fires that erupted on **February 2, 2024** in hills south of Viña del Mar and Quilpué became one of the five deadliest wildfires globally since 1900. The final death toll reached **136** (SENAPRED, February 29, 2024), with only 35 identified. Some **14,823 residences** were affected — 12,122 in Viña del Mar and 2,701 in Quilpué — alongside about 1,250 injuries. The main fire at Las Tablas–Peñuelas consumed **8,500 hectares**; nationally, about 43,000 hectares burned. President Boric declared a State of Catastrophe, imposed curfew, and deployed 31 firefighting aircraft, 1,400 firefighters, and 1,300 military personnel. He and Governor Rodrigo Mundaca publicly suggested the fires may have been intentionally started.

This catastrophe followed the familiar pattern of the **2017 "Tormenta de Fuego,"** when 547,190 hectares burned — a 5,000% increase from the prior season — killing at least 11 people, destroying the town of Santa Olga, and causing an estimated 76 premature deaths from smoke exposure. In both events, **Pinus and Eucalyptus plantations** burned disproportionately to their landscape share. The two dominant forestry companies, **Arauco** (Angelini Group) and **CMPC** (Matte Group), have replaced vast tracts of native vegetation with exotic monocultures across central Chile, where less than 20% of original native vegetation remains.

Criminal proceedings from the 2024 fires moved forward in July 2025, when Valparaíso's Guarantee Court presented accusations against **9 individuals** — including CONAF and SENAPRED workers — charged with forest fires resulting in death. The prosecution sought qualified life imprisonment. **Trial began March 9, 2026.**

<strong>Climate policy earns an "Almost Sufficient" rating but tensions persist</strong>

Chile's **Framework Law on Climate Change (Law 21.455)**, passed June 2022, legally binds the country to **net-zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050** — one of few developing nations with a legislated target. The updated 2020 NDC caps 2030 emissions at **95 MtCO₂e** with a carbon budget of 1,100 MtCO₂e for 2020–2030. Chile committed to peaking emissions by 2025; the Climate Action Tracker estimates they actually peaked in 2021. Chile's 2035 NDC includes mining-specific targets: 57% reduction in open-pit copper mining emissions and 74% in underground operations.

The **coal phase-out**, announced June 2019 under Piñera, targets closure of all 28 coal-fired plants by 2040. As of July 2025, **11 plants (\~1.7 GW)** have been retired, achieving the 2024 milestone. Boric signaled intent to advance the timeline to 2035. Renewable electricity has surged from **46% to 70%** in five years. Chile targets 80% by 2030 and 100% by 2050. Santiago operates the **largest electric bus fleet outside China** (over 38% of buses electric by early 2025), and **Cerro Dominador**, Latin America's first concentrated solar power plant, was inaugurated in June 2021. Chile introduced Latin America's first carbon tax in 2014 (\$5/tCO₂), with a decarbonization plan raising this to \$35 by 2030 and \$80 by 2040. The Climate Action Tracker rates Chile **"Almost Sufficient"** — one of very few countries at this level.

Yet structural tensions are real. Copper mining consumed **12,089 liters per second** in 2020, with projected water demand rising 17.6% by 2034. In the Salar de Atacama, **SQM** (authorized 1,600 l/s brine extraction and 240 l/s freshwater) and **Albemarle** together supply roughly 30% of global lithium demand. Producing one tonne of lithium carbonate evaporates about **500,000 liters of brine**, with up to 95% lost irrecoverably. Lithium and copper extraction reportedly consume over 65% of local water supply in the Salar de Atacama area. The "Salar Futuro" SQM-Codelco partnership is piloting direct lithium extraction technology to reduce water impact.

<strong>The 2022 Water Code reform changed much but preserved the market core</strong>

**Law 21.435**, published April 6, 2022 after 11 years in Congress, enacted **137 modifications** to Chile's Water Code. For the first time, it enshrined the **human right to water** as "essential and inalienable" and set priority for human consumption over productive uses. All new water rights (derechos de aprovechamiento de aguas) are now limited to **30 years** (previously perpetual), with unused consumptive rights expiring after 5 years. The DGA received strengthened powers to impose water redistribution during extreme drought.

Critically, however, **pre-existing water rights remain indefinite property rights**. The market-based trading system created by the 1981 Code continues. Brine for lithium mining is still classified as a mining resource outside the Water Code — a wide regulatory gap. The Constitutional Tribunal struck down a provision on priority ranking of existing rights. Enforcement capacity remains uncertain: the DGA faces about 17 new procedures, 5 new regulations, and 6 new resolutions to implement, with limited institutional capacity.

**Desertification** is advancing through Norte Chico. CONAF identifies 23% of national territory at high desertification risk, with the Coquimbo Region's 2.2 million hectares most severely affected. La Serena's 30-year average precipitation has collapsed from 170 mm to below 80 mm. Chile hosted the **COP25 presidency** in 2019 (relocated from Santiago to Madrid due to the estallido social), establishing the Santiago Network on Loss and Damage.

## IX. Illicit networks and shadow governance

<strong>Chile's security transformation from regional outlier to criminal battleground</strong>

Chile has undergone a rapid and dramatic security deterioration. More than **136,000 crimes linked to organized crime** were reported during 2022–2023, a rate of 775 incidents per 100,000 inhabitants. Homicide rates rose **40% from 2016 to 2021**. Kidnappings hit **868 in 2024** — the highest in a decade — with nearly 40% linked to organized crime. Corruption cases surged **690%** in 2024 versus 2023, alongside a 92.5% increase in cybercrime and 79.7% in arms offenses. Chile now hosts more than a dozen foreign criminal groups, including Venezuela's **Tren de Aragua**, Colombia's Los Sparks, and Peru's Los Pulpos.

<strong>Tren de Aragua has built a deep operational presence</strong>

The Tren de Aragua (TdA), born circa 2004 in Tocorón prison (Aragua state, Venezuela) under leader **Héctor "Niño Guerrero" Rusthenford Guerrero Flores**, has become Chile's gravest transnational criminal threat. Its expansion followed Venezuelan migration routes. TdA operates across extortion, human trafficking for sexual exploitation, drug trafficking, contract killing, kidnapping, and migrant smuggling. Between 2022 and July 2025, **17 torture houses** linked to TdA-associated gangs were discovered in Santiago. The organization maintains territorial presence from Arica in the north to Puerto Montt, 3,000 km south.

The **assassination of Venezuelan dissident Lieutenant Ronald Ojeda Moreno** crystallized the threat. Ojeda was abducted from his Santiago apartment on February 21, 2024, by individuals dressed as police; his body was found in a suitcase buried under 4.5 feet of concrete. By mid-2025, 12 individuals had been arrested. Chilean prosecutors alleged Venezuelan Interior Minister **Diosdado Cabello** ordered the killing through Niño Guerrero, executed by the "Los Piratas" cell (dismantled January 2025). President Boric publicly linked the Maduro regime to the assassination in an October 2025 speech in Rome.

Key arrests include **Larry Álvarez Nuñez ("Larry Changa")**, TdA co-founder, captured in Colombia on July 1, 2024 — he had lived in Chile from 2018–2022, selling arepas near the presidential palace while directing operations. **Rafael Gámez**, leader of Chile's TdA branch, was arrested in Texas in December 2024 for human trafficking; Chile initiated extradition. In November 2024, 34 of 38 charged in the Arica "Los Gallegos" mega-trial were convicted. The U.S. Treasury designated TdA a transnational criminal organization on **July 11, 2024**, with a **\$12 million reward** for leadership information. President Trump designated TdA a Foreign Terrorist Organization on January 20, 2025.

<strong>Drug and precursor trafficking exploit Chile's port infrastructure</strong>

Chile functions as a transit country for cocaine from Bolivia and Peru, with its efficient port system — built for copper and agricultural exports — now serving as a narco-corridor. A CEP study by Valeska Troncoso found Chile's ports have become "preferred routes" for organized crime. The most explosive seizure came between October 2024 and April 2025, when the Fiscalía Regional de Arica seized about **500 tons of precursor chemicals** in the ports of Arica and Iquique — enough to produce an estimated 400 tons of cocaine. Fiscal Mario Carrera described it as "the largest seizure in history, not just of Chile, but anywhere." Precursors were being imported from **China** through Chilean ports and destined for Bolivia. Overall drug seizures reached a record **20 tons in 2024**.

<strong>Timber theft and the arson economy in Araucanía</strong>

The forestry-related criminal economy is substantial. A 2022 Congressional Commission on Timber Theft valued the illegal timber economy at **US\$92.4 million**, equivalent to about 50,000 truckloads. Another estimate placed it at US\$100 million annually, involving roughly 650 people illegally felling \~5,000 hectares. The economy escalated rapidly: from US\$20 million (2018) to US\$67.8 million (2020). Corma (Corporación Chilena de la Madera) reported \$167 billion Chilean pesos in industry losses over three years.

Named criminal actors include **Walter Fernando Araneda Parra**, head of a major timber theft organization operating through front companies (Los Coigües SpA) that sold stolen wood to legitimate buyers including Arauco and CMPC — convicted with a 5-year sentence upheld by the Supreme Court in 2024. **Waldo Nuñez Cáceres** paid Mapuche groups 600,000 Chilean pesos (\~\$635) per truck of stolen wood. Two Carabineros officers (Darwin Carrillo Rodríguez, Luis Berna Toro) were arrested for providing intelligence to timber mafia leader Celso Fonseca. This criminal economy operates alongside — and is often conflated with — the Mapuche armed conflict involving groups like CAM, Weichan Auka Mapu, and Resistencia Mapuche Lavkenche, though parliamentary analysis suggests organized crime groups operate under the cover of legal companies rather than being primarily indigenous-led.

<strong>Pacogate revealed systemic institutional corruption within Carabineros</strong>

**Pacogate** remains the largest corruption case since Chile's 2000 criminal procedure reform. Between 2006 and 2017, over **35 billion Chilean pesos (\~US\$36 million)** was embezzled through salary funds, allowance diversions, and misappropriation of secret funds (gastos reservados). Some 180 individuals were investigated, 136 formally charged, and over 96 convicted. The mechanisms were systematic: excess deposits routed into subordinates' bank accounts, who were instructed to "return" funds to accounts controlled by the criminal network.

Former General Director **Eduardo Gordon** was convicted on October 30, 2024. Prosecution sought **24 years** for former General Directors **Bruno Villalobos** and **Gustavo González Jure**, the latter having handled 2.147 billion pesos and \$360,000 USD in secret funds. Carabineros' public approval collapsed from **77% (February 2017) to 52% (July 2017)** after the scandal broke. Five of the last seven directors-general have been formally charged in criminal proceedings.

<strong>Money laundering through ZOFRI and real estate</strong>

The **Zona Franca de Iquique (ZOFRI)** case of January 9, 2026 showed the scale of financial crime infrastructure. A joint PDI-FBI operation dismantled a network that laundered **US\$200+ million** through the Chilean financial system. Some 44–45 individuals (mostly Chinese nationals) were detained across five regions. The network created **119 shell companies** — including 16 "kamikaze" companies that existed only hours — and at least US\$67 million came from "pig butchering" scams targeting 400+ elderly U.S. victims. An ex-executive of **Banco Santander** allegedly facilitated cash withdrawals bypassing controls. Fiscal Nacional **Ángel Valencia** personally led the operation.

In real estate, one-third of all money laundering asset seizures since 2018 correspond to property — **\$32 billion pesos**. GAFILAT's September 2021 evaluation identified vulnerabilities in free trade zones and real estate. Chile's three FTZs (ZOFRI, Punta Arenas, Arica) operate with no duty, no VAT, and no corporate tax.

<strong>The assessment is capacity gap with emerging corruption vectors</strong>

Chile's situation is **predominantly an enforcement and institutional capacity gap** rather than systemic state-criminal symbiosis of the Mexican or Venezuelan type. Northern border regions have minimal state presence; the Colchane crossing point is dramatically understaffed. But specific corruption vectors are emerging: two PDI officers arrested for assisting TdA's sexual exploitation operations, Carabineros officers providing intelligence to timber mafias, and the ZOFRI case revealing banking sector complicity. The **Pandora Papers** (October 2021) exposed elite offshore structures — most notably former President **Sebastián Piñera's** December 2010 sale of Minera Dominga to close friend Carlos Alberto Délano for US\$152 million, with US\$138 million routed through the British Virgin Islands and a final installment contingent on the absence of environmental protections within the president's own government's authority. Other families named included Luksic, Cortés Solari, Cueto Plaza, Leonardo Farkas, and Holger Paulmann.

Anti-corruption institutions show mixed effectiveness. The **Contraloría General** retains **85% public trust** but its former Comptroller Ramiro Mendoza admitted knowing of Carabineros irregularities since 2010 without acting. The **Fiscalía Nacional** maintains 92 specialized anti-corruption prosecutors and its UNAC unit, and it led the ZOFRI megaoperation. Chile's Transparency International score stands at **63 points (rank 32 of 180)** — best in Latin America after Uruguay — but declining since its 2014 peak. Some **85% of Chileans** believe public organisms are "very or fairly corrupt" (2024 Criteria survey).

<strong>Kast's security agenda takes shape as he enters office</strong>

**José Antonio Kast** won the December 14, 2025 runoff with **58.16%** against Communist Party's Jeannette Jara (41.84%), the highest vote share since the return to democracy, carrying all regions. He takes office March 11, 2026. His **"Plan Implacable"** includes maximum-security prisons with total isolation for drug trafficking leaders, deportation of 330,000+ irregular migrants (primarily Venezuelan), border infrastructure along the Bolivia and Peru borders, creation of an ICE-style police force, and prohibition of "narcofunerales." Kast has repeatedly praised **Nayib Bukele's** crackdown, arguing Chile needs "more Bukele." Before and after his election, Venezuelan migrants began mass displacement to Peru, prompting Peru's President José Jerí to declare a state of emergency along the southern border. His Senate is evenly divided; governance will require coalitions with the populist People's Party.

## X. Information ecosystem and media structure

<strong>A print duopoly in financial collapse still shapes Chile's information space</strong>

Chile possesses what Bertelsmann SGI describes as "the most concentrated media ownership in Latin America." The **El Mercurio** group (Edwards family) and **COPESA** (Álvaro Saieh) together control about **90% of print media**. Both are now in severe financial distress, but their structural influence persists.

**El Mercurio S.A.P.** publishes the flagship El Mercurio, La Segunda, Las Últimas Noticias, and **21 regional dailies** spanning from Arica to Patagonia. After Agustín Edwards Eastman died on April 24, 2017, control passed to his six children, with **Cristián Edwards del Río** (MBA Wharton, former New York Times executive) becoming the effective head. The Edwards family's history is inseparable from Chilean political power: Agustín Edwards met Henry Kissinger and CIA Director Richard Helms on September 5, 1970, pushing for a preemptive coup against Allende; the CIA provided covert financial support to El Mercurio. By 2024–2025, El Mercurio S.A.P. accumulated about **\$362 million pesos** in unpaid pension contributions and labor debts, with multiple AFPs initiating legal actions.

**COPESA**, 100% owned by Álvaro Saieh Bendeck (Forbes: \~\$1.5 billion net worth, September 2024), publishes La Tercera, La Cuarta (digital-only since \~2021), and Pulso. COPESA posted losses of **\$5 billion CLP in 2024** and owes \~\$6 billion CLP to providers. In February 2025, journalists staged a "brazos caídos" work stoppage over partial, late salary payments. Saieh's broader **CorpGroup** filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy under U.S. law. COPESA has been described alongside El Mercurio as "muertos caminando" (walking dead) by media analysts.

<strong>Television ownership maps onto Chile's business elite</strong>

Television concentrates in four major channels, all connected to economic conglomerates:

- **Mega** (Bethia Group, Heller Solari family, headed by Carlos Heller): the most-watched free-to-air channel, averaging **7.5 rating points** (Kantar Ibope 2023) and the only major TV player turning a profit. Bethia repurchased Discovery's 27.5% stake in October 2023, regaining 100% ownership.
- **Chilevisión**: passed from Sebastián Piñera (2006) to Turner/WarnerMedia (2010) to Paramount (2021) to **Vytal Group** (sale completed January 13, 2026).
- **Canal 13**: 100% owned by **Grupo Luksic** since Andrónico Luksic Craig acquired control in 2010 (US\$55M for 67%), purchasing the remaining 33% from the Pontificia Universidad Católica in 2017. The channel has posted losses exceeding US\$50M since 2014.
- **TVN** (state-owned but self-funded through advertising): averaged just **3.9 rating points** in 2023, the lowest of the four.
- **CNN Chile** was sold by Warner Bros. Discovery to **Jorge Carey Carvallo** in 2025.

<strong>Digital natives and investigative outlets provide countervailing voices</strong>

The most consequential media actor in Chile may now be **CIPER Chile** (Centro de Investigación Periodística), a nonprofit investigative center founded in 2008 that has become the essential agenda-setter. CIPER broke the **"Caso Audios"** in 2024 — secretly recorded conversations revealing bribery planning that led to high-profile arrests and uncovered a web of judicial-political corruption. Academic studies confirm CIPER ranks higher than traditional papers in social media impact.

**El Mostrador**, founded in 2000 as the first exclusively digital newspaper in the Spanish-speaking world, remains a major agenda-setter. **Interferencia**, edited by Víctor Herrero (author of the Edwards family biography), pursues investigative work but faces legal pressure — Herrero was convicted in May 2023 on criminal charges related to an article about a police officer. **The Clinic**, once satirically progressive, was acquired by businessman Jorge Ergas in 2020 and has shifted toward a more general editorial style.

In radio, **Radio Bío-Bío** operates 40 frequencies covering 98% of Chile's population and ranks among the most trusted brands (Reuters Institute 2025). **Radio Cooperativa**, historic for opposing the Pinochet dictatorship, maintains influence. Spain's **PRISA Group** controls a large radio portfolio through Radio ADN and received the most radio concessions. Copesa, Radio Cooperativa, and El Mostrador all sued Google in 2024–2025 over alleged advertising monopoly practices — Copesa was the first Latin American media company to do so.

<strong>Social media penetration is among Latin America's highest</strong>

Chile has **95% internet penetration** and the highest social media penetration among Latin America's largest markets. **WhatsApp** reaches 93.8% of users and is used by 26% for news. **Instagram** reaches 87.2%, **Facebook** 85.6%, and **TikTok** about 66% (16.2 million users aged 18+ in late 2025, growing 16.4% year-over-year). Only **10% of Chileans** pay for online news, constraining digital media business models.

Disinformation has been a potent factor in recent political processes. During the 2019 estallido social, disinformation spread through messaging services was identified as "one of the most relevant consequences of the political crisis." Government officials allegedly coordinated with TV executives to emphasize violence and minimize police brutality. During the 2022 constitutional plebiscite, **58% of Chileans** reported exposure to misinformation, including false claims that the new constitution would change Chile's flag and national anthem. CIPER found **202 disinformation complaints** to SERVEL went "uninvestigated and unsanctioned" due to legal gaps.

Chile's media system exhibits features of Hallin and Mancini's **Polarized Pluralist Model** (Southern European type): high political parallelism, instrumentalization by private interests, weak professional autonomy. But the extreme ownership concentration and commercial/neoliberal structure distinguish it from European state-subsidized models. Academic work by Mellado and Lagos (2013) argues the original framework is "insufficient for contextualizing" media systems in new democracies like Chile.

In coverage of the Mapuche conflict, academic analysis by Del Valle Rojas and Osorio Solano documented that negative representations in El Mercurio increased from 15.8% (1950–1973) to **40.47%** (2013–2014). Three stereotypes persist across eras: "savages," "layabouts," and since the 1990s, **"terrorists."** El Mercurio quotes Mapuche sources in only 4.42% of articles, versus 19.48% in regional papers.

Chile's press freedom has fluctuated: RSF ranked Chile **52nd** in 2024 (up from 83rd in 2023) but dropped to **69th** in 2025. RSF notes that "legal proceedings against media outlets and journalists have become a recurring strategy to silence them."

## XI. International institutional commitments and legal frameworks

<strong>A 35-agreement FTA network covering 86% of world GDP anchors Chile externally</strong>

Chile maintains about **35 trade agreements covering 64 economies** representing \~86% of world GDP. Nearly 98% of Chilean trade occurs with FTA partners. This network is structurally the most important single constraint on Chile's foreign policy flexibility.

The **US-Chile FTA** (signed June 6, 2003; entered into force January 1, 2004) was the first US FTA with a South American nation. It became **fully tariff-free on January 1, 2015**. Bilateral trade in goods and services totaled nearly **\$47 billion** in 2024. The **EU-Chile Advanced Framework Agreement**, signed December 13, 2023, is the most comprehensive recent addition. Its Interim Trade Agreement entered into force **February 1, 2025**, with provisional application of the political and cooperation pillars beginning June 1, 2025. It is the first EU agreement with a dedicated **hydrogen chapter** and a trade-and-gender chapter, and it commits Chile to Paris Agreement, CBD, and CITES implementation. Full entry into force awaits ratification by all 27 EU member states.

The **China-Chile FTA** (original 2006, upgraded 2017, entered force March 1, 2019) underpins Chile's most important bilateral trade relationship: in 2024, China absorbed **\$37.6 billion** in Chilean exports — about 38% of the total. Chile was the first Latin American country to sign an FTA with China. Chile's total 2024 exports reached **\$99 billion**.

Chile hosted the **CPTPP signing ceremony** on March 8, 2018 in Santiago and was one of the founding P-4 members of its predecessor agreement. Despite President Boric's personal opposition, his administration did not block ratification; CPTPP entered into force for Chile on **February 21, 2023**. Chile negotiated side letters on ISDS with New Zealand, Malaysia, and Mexico. The **Pacific Alliance** (Chile, Colombia, Mexico, Peru), with its Commercial Protocol in force since May 1, 2016, has eliminated 92%+ of intra-bloc tariffs, set up integrated stock markets (MILA) and 8 shared embassies. Costa Rica was approved as an accession candidate in December 2024. Chile held the Pro Tempore Presidency in 2024.

<strong>UN system engagement is substantial, with peacekeeping as a signature contribution</strong>

Chile ratified the **ICC Rome Statute** (instrument deposited June 29, 2009) and enacted implementing legislation (Ley 20.357, July 18, 2009) covering genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity. Chile ratified the **crime of aggression amendments** on September 23, 2016, and did not sign a Non-Surrender Agreement with the United States. In early 2024, Chile and Mexico jointly referred the Israel-Hamas war to the ICC.

Chile's signature peacekeeping contribution was **MINUSTAH** (Haiti, 2004–2017). Over the full mission, more than **5,900 Chilean service members** served, with a regular deployment of \~400–500 uniformed personnel. Chile twice provided the mission's Special Representative: **Juan Gabriel Valdés** (2004–2006) and **Mario Fernández Amunátegui** (2011–2013). Chile was the first Latin American country to join the force (President Ricardo Lagos announced participation March 1, 2004). Legislative framework Law 20,297 (2008) requires presidential request and Senate approval for overseas troop deployments.

<strong>Regional architecture is fragmented and Chile navigates cautiously</strong>

Chile **co-founded PROSUR** under Piñera (March 22, 2019, Santiago) as a center-right alternative to UNASUR but **exited in 2022** under Boric; the forum is now effectively moribund. Chile withdrew definitively from **UNASUR** on April 21, 2019 under Piñera and has **not rejoined**, unlike Argentina and Brazil which returned in 2023. The Boric government called for building regional organizations not based on "ideological inclinations of incumbent leaders." Chile actively participates in **CELAC** and the **OAS**, where it aligned with the Lima Group in condemning Maduro's government.

Chile joined **APEC** in November 1994, hosted it in 2004, was forced to cancel the 2019 summit due to the estallido social, and is scheduled to host **APEC 2026**. **OECD membership** (January 7, 2010 — first South American member) provides institutional credibility but brings scrutiny: the 2024 Environmental Performance Review found Chile's carbon tax (\$5/tonne) "well below OECD average," GHG emissions not on track for net-zero 2050, and the Environmental Superintendence under-resourced.

<strong>Antarctic claims, BRICS interest, and sanctions navigation</strong>

Chile is one of **12 original signatories** of the Antarctic Treaty (signed December 1, 1959). Its claimed **Chilean Antarctic Territory** — south of 60°S between 53°W and 90°W — covers about 1,250,258 km², overlapping with Argentine and British claims. Chile maintains **12–13 active Antarctic bases** (4 permanent), including **Base Presidente Eduardo Frei Montalva** (the largest) and the civilian settlement **Villa Las Estrellas** (inaugurated 1984). Punta Arenas serves as the Antarctic gateway for 22 countries. The **Chilean Antarctic Institute (INACH)** coordinates over 100 scientific projects involving 350+ researchers.

Chile has **not formally joined BRICS** but has expressed growing interest. Under-Secretary for Foreign Relations **Gloria de la Fuente** confirmed Chile is "evaluating the potential benefits," describing a 2025 BRICS Summit invitation as "acknowledgment of Chile's institutional stability." About 45% of Chilean exports go to BRICS countries (\$46.1 billion in 2024). Chile was not among countries invited as BRICS "partner countries" at the October 2024 Kazan Summit. The question is politically polarized.

On **Russia sanctions**, Chile does not maintain an autonomous sanctions list and does not independently implement US/EU unilateral sanctions. Chile implements mandatory UN Security Council sanctions, condemned the invasion, supported UNGA resolutions, and called for troop withdrawal — but maintained economic pragmatism. This reflects the broader Latin American pattern of political condemnation without economic enforcement.

**Defection costs** from Western frameworks would be enormous. Chile's FTA network locks in preferential access to markets representing 86% of world GDP. OECD membership signals governance quality to institutional investors. The EU AFA explicitly conditions benefits on "human rights, democratic values, and rules-based international order." US defense cooperation would be jeopardized. Yet China absorbs 38% of exports, making full Western alignment equally constraining. Chile's strategic position requires sustained balance — a shift to BRICS at the expense of Western frameworks would sacrifice irreplaceable institutional benefits without guaranteed alternatives.

Chile has been involved in notable **WTO disputes**: *DS87/DS110* (EC/US challenged Chile's pisco tax — Chile lost), *DS207* (Argentina challenged Chile's agricultural price band — Chile lost), and *DS193* (the swordfish case with the EC, which also went to ITLOS before mutual resolution). In **investment arbitration**, the *MTD Equity v. Chile* ICSID case became a major precedent in international investment law, while the *Víctor Pey Casado* case — related to Pinochet-era expropriation of El Clarín newspaper — became one of the longest ICSID proceedings.

## XII. Military and security sector DNA

<strong>Force structure reflects a mid-sized military with Prussian institutional DNA</strong>

Chile's armed forces total about **80,000 active personnel**: the Army (\~46,350 including \~9,200 conscripts) under **General Javier Iturriaga del Campo** (appointed March 2022), the Navy (\~20,000–25,000 including 3,600 Marines) under **Admiral Juan Andrés De La Maza Larraín**, and the Air Force (\~10,000–11,000) under **General Hugo Rodríguez González**. Carabineros add about 50,000 personnel. Chile ranks 49th of 145 countries on the Global Firepower index.

Defense spending reached **\$5.105 billion** in 2024 (SIPRI), about **1.6–1.9% of GDP** — among the highest in Latin America. The **Ley Reservada del Cobre** (Law 13.196, 1958), which for decades secretly guaranteed the military 10% of CODELCO's copper export revenues for off-budget arms procurement, was reformed by **Law 21.174 in September 2019**. This created the Multi-year Fund for Strategic Defence Capacities subject to Congressional approval, effectively ending off-budget military financing. SIPRI highlighted Chile as a unique example of successfully transitioning to standard budgeting.

The **Prussian heritage** remains remarkably visible. After the War of the Pacific (1879–1883), Prussian Captain **Emil Körner** arrived in the 1880s and eventually rose to commanding general. Chile was the first non-European country to adopt the goose step (paso de ganso), debuted in 1898 and standardized by 1901. Today's annual Gran Parada Militar (September 19, O'Higgins Park) still features the **Stechschritt**, Stahlhelm-style helmets on NCO School soldiers, Pickelhaube helmets on artillery units, Preußischer Präsentiermarsch as march-past music, dark gray uniforms with ceremonial rapiers, and the **Academia de Guerra** modeled on the Prussian Kriegsakademie. The Navy, by contrast, follows British traditions (Lord Cochrane's founding legacy).

<strong>Civil-military relations have been transformed but not fully consolidated</strong>

The 2005 constitutional reforms under **President Ricardo Lagos** were watershed:

- Eliminated appointed/designated senators (including military representatives) and senators-for-life
- Restored presidential power to remove commanders-in-chief without National Security Council approval
- Converted the National Security Council to a merely advisory body
- **Removed the military's constitutional mandate as "guarantors of institutional order"** (garantes de la institucionalidad)

Lagos declared: "Chile now has a Constitution that does not divide us." The 2010 Organic Law of the Ministry of Defense (Law 20,424) further consolidated civilian control. Scholars describe the transition as "difficult, gradual, and unaccomplished" — the armed forces retain a degree of institutional independence and economic autonomy through entities like **FAMAE** (Army weapons factory, now partnering with Turkish Aselsan), **ENAER** (Air Force aeronautics company), **ASMAR** (Navy shipbuilding), and separate pension systems. **CAPREDENA** (military pensions) provides average male pensions of \$1,264,682 CLP — general-grade pensions can reach **12× higher** than civilian AFP system pensions.

<strong>The Carabineros crisis is structural, not episodic</strong>

The Carabineros are Chile's deepest institutional problem in the security sector. **Ricardo Yáñez Reveco** resigned as General Director in late September 2024 and was formally arraigned on **October 1, 2024** at Santiago's 7th Juzgado de Garantía, charged with "omisión de apremios ilegítimos con resultado de lesiones graves y homicidio" — failing to prevent subordinates' human rights violations during the 2019 estallido. Former directors **Mario Rozas** and sub-director **Diego Olate** were also charged. Prosecutor **Ximena Chong** compiled **1,386 documented cases** of human rights violations. During the estallido, 30–38+ people died, thousands were injured, and over **400 cases of ocular trauma** from rubber bullets and tear gas canisters were documented. Senator **Fabiola Campillai**, blinded by a tear gas canister, became a symbol. Five of the last seven Carabineros directors-general have been formally charged in criminal proceedings. New director **Marcelo Araya Zapata** was appointed by President Boric.

The **PDI (Policía de Investigaciones)** functions as Chile's civilian detective force, equivalent to an FBI. It handles criminal investigation and forensics, reports to the Ministry of Interior, and maintains an intelligence arm (**JIPOL**). The PDI was also implicated in the Guacamaya hack, and two PDI officers were arrested in April 2024 for assisting TdA's sexual exploitation operations.

<strong>Intelligence capabilities are critically weak</strong>

The **Agencia Nacional de Inteligencia (ANI)**, created in 2004 by Law 19,974, operates with a budget of about **US\$4 million** and roughly **125 personnel** — by comparison, Argentina's equivalent is 15× larger and Spain's 50× larger. ANI has a non-operational mandate (intelligence collection and analysis only), leads the State Intelligence System (SIE) comprising military intelligence, Carabineros intelligence (DIPOLCAR), and PDI intelligence (JIPOL), and maintains a cyber-intelligence department within its counterintelligence division.

ANI is widely criticized as dysfunctional. It **failed to anticipate the 2019 estallido social** (the prior director was fired), failed to prevent the Ronald Ojeda kidnapping in 2024, and has never become a credible intelligence agency. A modernization bill approved unanimously by the Senate would add Gendarmería, Customs, the Financial Analysis Unit, and the tax service to the SIE, double personnel, and create an Intelligence Advisory Council.

<strong>Defense procurement centers on German armor, American fighters, and French submarines</strong>

Chile's major equipment holdings and recent procurement actions include:

- **Leopard 2A4 tanks**: 140–186 operational (acquired from Germany, 2007–2008), designated Leopard 2A4CHL after modification. FAMAE and Turkish **Aselsan** signed a modernization contract in July 2023 ("Proaco" project) for new electronic fire control systems. Turbochargers were modified for Atacama Desert high-altitude operations above 4,300 meters.
- **F-16 fleet**: \~46 operational aircraft — 10 new-build F-16C/D Block 50+ (delivered from 2006, \$500M deal) and 36 ex-Netherlands F-16AM/BM MLU. A **\$177 million upgrade contract** was awarded to Lockheed Martin in October 2023 for M6.6 standard modernization of the 36 MLU jets, adding IFF and Link 16 capability, with completion by November 2032.
- **Submarines**: 4 boats — 2 Scorpène-class (O'Higgins, commissioned 2005; Carrera, commissioned 2006) and 2 Thomson-class Type 209/1400-L (commissioned 1984, upgraded 2009/2012 with SUBTICS combat systems and Black Shark torpedoes). In **July 2025, Chile initiated a formal process to acquire 4 new submarines** as part of a 20-year, \~\$25 billion modernization plan. Contenders include Type 218, Scorpène, and Blekinge-class designs. ASMAR aims to build next-generation submarines domestically.
- Recent orders include **205 Mercedes-Benz military trucks** (delivery 2H 2026) and Galvion helmets via US FMS (\$9.15M).

<strong>Joint exercises with the US are extensive and deepening</strong>

Chile maintains its closest defense relationship with the United States through the **Texas National Guard State Partnership Program** (since 2009). The 19th round of Annual Bilateral Staff Talks (August 2024) produced a **106-point action plan** through 2027. Key 2024 exercises included **UNITAS LXV** (hosted by Chile; 17 vessels, 4,300+ personnel from 24 nations), **RIMPAC 2024** (where Commodore Alberto Guerrero became the first officer from a non-founding, non-Anglophone nation to serve as deputy CTF commander), **Southern Fenix 2024** (inaugural exercise featuring the first HIMARS deployment to the SOUTHCOM area), and **Southern Seas 2024** with the USS George Washington carrier strike group. Chile also participates in the **DESI** (Diesel-Electric Submarine Initiative) program at San Diego. CJCS General Charles Q. Brown visited Santiago in 2024 to discuss cyber and space cooperation. Other defense partners include the UK (Type 23 frigates), France (Scorpène submarines), Israel (Python IV and Derby missiles), and Turkey (Aselsan electronics).

<strong>The 2022 Guacamaya hack exposed military surveillance of civil society</strong>

On **September 19, 2022** — deliberately timed for Chile's "Glories of the Army" military parade — the Guacamaya hacktivist group breached the Estado Mayor Conjunto (EMCO) by exploiting **ProxyShell vulnerabilities** in Microsoft Exchange servers that had been known since March 2021 but never patched. The leak comprised **400,000+ emails** from 162 EMCO accounts (\~350 GB), spanning 2012–2022. Key revelations included military surveillance of civil society organizations (Communist Youth, environmental activists, elected politicians), **CLP\$692 million** spent in 6 months to militarize Biobío during the state of emergency, cybersecurity strategies, and classified documents. EMCO chief **General Guillermo Paiva** resigned four days later. Amnesty International condemned the "undue monitoring of civil society organizations."

<strong>Conscription is technically compulsory but effectively voluntary</strong>

Chile's Servicio Militar Obligatorio (founded 1900, reformed by Law 20,045 in 2005) operates as a compulsory-selective system. A lottery (sorteo general) is held, but since 2007 **nearly all recruits have been volunteers** — in 2008, 70,461 were selected by lottery but none were called against their will. Annual intake varies between 11,000 and 16,000. Duration is 12 months (Army) or 22 months (Navy/Air Force).

General **Javier Iturriaga's** behavior during the 2019 estallido — his declaration **"I'm not at war with anyone"** — was the pivotal civil-military moment of the crisis. It represented a deliberate refusal to escalate despite President Piñera's confrontational rhetoric ("we are at war against a powerful enemy"), signaling the military would not repeat 1973. Iturriaga subsequently became Commander-in-Chief of the Army in March 2022. The historical pattern — 1891 Civil War, 1924–25 juntas, 1973 coup — makes this restraint notable as evidence of genuine institutional transformation, even if it remains a work in progress.

<strong>Converging pressures on an institutional framework not built to handle them simultaneously</strong>

Chile's structural position as of March 2026 is defined by converging pressures that its institutional framework was not designed to handle simultaneously. The **mega-drought** represents an existential threat to the central valley economic model, and the 2022 Water Code reform — while symbolically important — preserved the market core that enables over-extraction. The **criminal penetration** by Tren de Aragua and others exploits a capacity gap rather than state complicity, but the gap is widening faster than institutional reforms can close it. The **media duopoly** is financially collapsing even as its structural influence persists, while digital alternatives like CIPER are reshaping accountability journalism with minimal resources. Chile's **35-agreement FTA network** is both its greatest asset and its tightest constraint — it locks the country into Western institutional frameworks with enormous defection costs while China absorbs 38% of exports. The **military sector**, despite genuine democratic transformation since 1973, retains institutional autonomy through separate economic enterprises and pension systems, while ANI's 125 personnel and \$4 million budget represent a critical intelligence deficit for a country facing transnational threats. Kast's inaugururation tomorrow marks the beginning of Chile's most security-oriented governance since the return to democracy — whether his "Plan Implacable" strengthens or destabilizes the institutional fabric will determine the country's trajectory.

## XIII. History of dissent and civil society infrastructure

Chile possesses one of Latin America's deepest and most institutionally layered civil society architectures, built across cycles of mobilization and repression stretching back over a century. **The trajectory from the 2019 estallido social to Kast's December 2025 election marks a dramatic reversal**: the progressive social movements that forced a constitutional opening have been organizationally hollowed, while conservative civil society infrastructure — think tanks, evangelical churches, business associations — enters a period of maximal political influence. The incoming Kast administration confronts a civil society landscape that is demobilized on the left but structurally intact, meaning latent reactivation capacity remains high.

<strong>The labor movement: structurally fragmented but with strategic chokepoints</strong>

Chile is one of only three OECD countries where union density increased since 1985, rising from **13% in 2007 to a peak of 22.2% in 2020**, before declining to about **19% by 2023**. This headline figure obscures deep structural fragmentation: **51.3% of Chile's nearly 9,000 active unions have 50 or fewer members**, a legacy of Pinochet's 1979 Labour Plan that deliberately atomized collective bargaining into firm-level units. Public sector unionization (\~41.3%) vastly exceeds private sector rates (\~10.1%).

The Central Unitaria de Trabajadores (CUT), Chile's dominant confederation, remains historically aligned with the Communist and Socialist parties. Under Boric, CUT enjoyed its closest government relationship since the Concertación era — Labor Minister Jeannette Jara was herself a former union leader, and the passage of the **40-hour workweek law in 2023** (reducing from 45 hours, phased implementation) marked CUT's signature legislative achievement. Copper miners' unions retain outsized leverage through their position at the chokepoint of Chile's export economy; rising strike activity since 2007 has been identified as a fundamental driver of membership growth. But employer strategies of subcontracting and temporary employment — particularly in mining, forestry, and fishing — continue to erode collective bargaining coverage, and the share of workers covered by agreements that do not confer the right to strike nearly tripled from 13% to 35% between 1990 and 2023.

Under Kast, whose Republican Party favors a pro-employer orientation and has pledged **\$6 billion in spending cuts**, the labor movement faces an adversarial state for the first time since Piñera's second term. No specific anti-union legislation has been announced, but the broader deregulatory agenda signals a less favorable environment for organized labor.

<strong>Student movements: from kingmakers to institutional absorption</strong>

The student movement's arc from the 2006 Revolución Pingüina through the 2011 mass mobilizations to the present day illustrates a classic cycle of radical movement success followed by institutionalization and energy dissipation. The 2006 high school protests mobilized nearly **790,000 students**; the 2011 university protests, led by CONFECH under figures like Camila Vallejo and Gabriel Boric, drew marches exceeding **600,000** in Santiago alone. These movements placed education and inequality at the center of national politics and ultimately produced a generation of politicians.

The institutionalization of the student movement reached its apex when **Boric was elected president in December 2021** — four former CONFECH leaders joined his cabinet, and dozens of former student activists received government appointments. CONFECH, which encompasses 60+ student federations, continues to advocate for public education and issued a 2024 platform ("Petitorio CONFECH 2024"), but its mobilization capacity is **substantially diminished** compared to 2011–2019. The constitutional process defeats and the rightward shift of Chilean politics have sidelined student demands. FECH (Universidad de Chile's student federation, founded 1906) maintains an active but smaller-scale presence. With Kast assuming office, the movement may face a hostile government that could paradoxically re-energize campus organizing.

<strong>The think tank landscape: ideological infrastructure mapped</strong>

Chile's think tank ecosystem is the densest in Latin America and serves as the primary intellectual infrastructure for both left and right governance projects. Political alignment is the organizing principle:

<strong>Right / Center-right:</strong>
- **CEP (Centro de Estudios Públicos)**: Founded 1980. Centrist-liberal, technocratic. Conducts Chile's most influential social surveys (since 1987). Co-authored an alternative constitutional text during the 2022 process alongside IES and Horizontal. Represents market-oriented establishment intellectualism.
- **Libertad y Desarrollo (LyD)**: Founded 1990, closely linked to UDI. Created explicitly to "safeguard the heritage of the dictatorship" per academic analysis. Free-market focus, ranked among top Latin American think tanks, active in legislative testimony.
- **Fundación Jaime Guzmán**: Named after the UDI founder and Pinochet-era constitutional architect (assassinated 1991). Preserves the intellectual framework of the 1980 institutional order.
- **Instituto Libertad**: Affiliated with Renovación Nacional (RN). Parliamentary advisory services.
- **Fundación para el Progreso (FPP)**: Founded 2012 by entrepreneur Nicolás Ibáñez, led by libertarian Axel Kaiser. Targets youth audiences through aggressive digital strategy. Linked to international libertarian movement.
- **IES (Instituto de Estudios de la Sociedad)**: Intellectual center-right developing "post-neoliberal" conservative thought. Key figure Daniel Mansuy.
- **Ideas Republicanas**: Directly linked to Kast's Partido Republicano. Described as "radical right," explicitly differentiating from Chile Vamos. Provides policy infrastructure for the incoming administration.

<strong>Left / Center-left:</strong>
- **Fundación Sol**: Left-wing, labor-focused, "outside the institutional axis." Closely connected to unions and social movements. Produces widely cited inequality and wage research.
- **Chile 21**: Founded 1992, center-left. Aligned with the Concertación/Nueva Mayoría tradition. Fundamental role in programmatic discussion under center-left governments.
- **FLACSO-Chile**: Part of the inter-American academic network. Progressive orientation, focused on democracy and social sciences.
- **Espacio Público**: Founded 2012, center/center-left technocratic. Three members served as Boric ministers.
- **Rumbo Colectivo**: Think tank of Revolución Democrática (Frente Amplio). Three former board members served in Boric government.
- **CIEPLAN**: Historic center-left economics institution, key to Concertación-era technocratic governance.
- **Nodo XXI**: Student movement-origin, education policy focused.

<strong>The 2019 estallido social: organizational architecture and its dissolution</strong>

The October 18, 2019 uprising, triggered by a 30-peso Metro fare increase, escalated into the largest protests in Chilean history — **1.2 million** in Santiago on October 25 alone, with 30 deaths and over 12,500 injuries nationwide. The movement's organizational architecture was distinctive for its **leaderless, horizontal** character:

**Cabildos abiertos** (open town halls) proliferated immediately — over **300 self-convened** by November 4, 2019, organized around three questions: causes of the crisis, priority demands, and citizen actions. **Territorial assemblies** emerged in neighborhoods nationwide, with some drawing 400+ participants. Plazas were symbolically renamed (Plaza Italia became "Plaza Dignidad"). **Unidad Social**, a coalition of about 100 organizations, served as a loose coordinating umbrella. The **Coordinadora Feminista 8M** was pivotal from day one, helping convene the October 21 general strike alongside CUT and participating in Unidad Social. Social media — Twitter, Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp — served as the primary coordination infrastructure in the absence of hierarchical leadership.

The organizational legacy proved fragile. Researchers note that many participants were "primo-manifestantes" (first-time protesters) without prior organizational ties, making sustained mobilization difficult. The cabildos transitioned into **ollas comunes** (communal kitchens) during COVID-19, repurposing solidarity networks for mutual aid. The constitutional process (78% voted for a new constitution in October 2020) absorbed institutional energy. But both constitutional drafts failed, and the progressive social base underwent what analysts describe as **"social demobilization"** and a "crisis of belonging." By 2025–2026, the organizational infrastructure that produced the estallido had largely dissolved, a key enabling condition for the far-right electoral victory.

<strong>Feminist movement: sustained mobilization capacity amid hostile turn</strong>

The feminist movement retains organizational capacity that distinguishes it from other demobilized progressive forces. **Las Tesis**, the Valparaíso-based collective that created "Un violador en tu camino" in November 2019 (subsequently performed in 300+ cities worldwide), continues as an active collective. The **Coordinadora Feminista 8M**, founded 2018, mobilized **800,000+ women on March 8, 2019** and nearly **2 million on March 8, 2020**. March 8, 2025 mobilizations spanned **40+ cities**, directly contradicting far-right claims that "feminism is dead."

The movement has expanded to intersectional concerns — migration, indigenous rights (incorporating Mapuche women's organizations like Ulcha Zomo), anti-extractivism, and LGBTQ+ rights. Nine femicides in early 2025 fueled continued mobilization. Under Kast, described as "notoriously opposed to feminist, environmentalist, indigenous, and LGBTQ movements," the feminist movement is expected to shift to a more oppositional posture and may serve as the primary vehicle for progressive street mobilization given the demobilization of other movement sectors.

<strong>Mapuche civil society versus armed groups: a critical distinction</strong>

The Mapuche political landscape spans a spectrum from institutional advocacy to armed insurgency, and conflation of these categories is a persistent analytical error:

**Political/civil organizations** include **Identidad Territorial Lafkenche** (founded 1996, coastal Mapuche, focused on legislative advocacy, has elected members to Congress), **Consejo de Todas las Tierras** (led by Aucán Huilcamán, institutional advocacy with occasional radical rhetoric), and **Ad Mapu** (historical organization from the dictatorship era). These operate within institutional channels.

**Armed/militant groups** include **CAM (Coordinadora Arauco-Malleco)**, founded 1998, whose leader Héctor Llaitul was sentenced to **23 years** in prison. CAM conducts land occupations, arson against forestry operations, and rejects all state dialogue while "militarization and Mapuche political prisoners" persist. **WAM (Weichan Auka Mapu)** is more radical — responsible for systematic arson campaigns (hundreds of logging trucks, 80+ churches torched), the April 2024 ambush killing of three gendarmes, and **11 coordinated arson operations in a single week** in November 2025 ahead of the December election. WAM's own December 2024 communiqué admitted some members engaged in drug trafficking. **Resistencia Mapuche Lavkenche** operates as another militant faction.

The state of emergency in Araucanía and Biobío provinces has been **continuously extended since May 2022** — 56 times by May 2025. Poverty in La Araucanía (17.4%) exceeds the national rate (10.8%). Boric's Presidential Commission for Peace and Understanding delivered 21 recommendations in May 2025, but Kast has pledged to abandon dialogue and treat Mapuche militants as "narco-terrorists." Argentina's Milei declared RAM a terrorist organization in February 2025, signaling regional coordination on this front.

<strong>Catholic Church: diminished but not irrelevant; evangelicals rising</strong>

The Karadima abuse scandal (Vatican conviction 2011, defrocking 2018) and its cascading revelations — 78+ complaints, 100+ priests investigated, all 34 bishops offering resignations in May 2018 — devastated Catholic institutional credibility. Catholic identification fell from **\~80% in 1987 to 53.7% in the 2024 census**. Chile is now the second least religious country in Latin America after Uruguay, with **25.7% declaring no religion**.

**Evangelical/Pentecostal churches** (16.2% of population, 2024 census) have become a growing conservative political force, particularly among lower socioeconomic groups and indigenous communities (**35% of Mapuche in Araucanía are evangelical**). Their mobilization on family values, opposition to abortion and same-sex marriage aligns naturally with Kast's moral conservatism. No "foreign agent" legislation targeting civil society has been enacted or proposed in Chile, contrasting with the global trend.

<strong>Human rights infrastructure faces existential threat</strong>

The **INDH (Instituto Nacional de Derechos Humanos)**, an autonomous public institution, documented **10,936 estallido-related cases**, over 3,400 civilian hospitalizations, and 347 eye injuries during the 2019 protests. As of late 2023, only 127 cases were formalized and 38 resulted in convictions — a **0.2% justice rate** for victims. Kast explicitly called for the INDH's closure during a 2021 debate. The Boric government's December 2024 protocol for protection of human rights defenders provides some legal framework, but its enforcement under Kast is uncertain. Veteran investigators predict Kast "will shut it down."

## XIV. Patron-client history and alliance genealogy

Chile's foreign policy architecture rests on a unique structural feature: **35 trade agreements covering 64 economies representing about 86% of global GDP** — the most extensive FTA network of any country on earth. This has enabled a hedging strategy between great powers that persists regardless of domestic political orientation. Kast's inauguration marks a tilt toward Washington in tone and security alignment, but structural economic dependence on Beijing (**40% of exports**) ensures the multi-vector approach endures. The January 2026 US military intervention in Venezuela and the March 2026 submarine cable dispute have made Chile's balancing act dramatically more difficult.

<strong>The US relationship: warm politically, strained structurally</strong>

Chile-US relations under Trump II have split along two axes. Diplomatically, the incoming Kast administration enjoys strong affinity — Secretary Rubio was the first to congratulate Kast on December 14, 2025, and Trump publicly claimed credit for the result. Chile remains the **only Latin American country in the US Visa Waiver Program**, and bilateral law enforcement cooperation deepened through the Ojeda case (three Tren de Aragua suspects extradited from the US to Chile in September 2025).

The trade picture is more complex. The **US-Chile FTA** (in force since 2004, fully duty-free since 2015) has been overlaid with Trump-era tariffs. A **10% IEEPA tariff** was imposed on Chilean imports from April 2025 (subsequently struck down by the Supreme Court in February 2026, replaced by 10% Section 122 tariffs). More consequentially, **50% Section 232 copper tariffs** took effect August 1, 2025 — but **exempted refined copper cathodes, minerals, concentrates, and anodes**, which constitute the vast bulk of Chilean copper exports to the US. Only **0.1% of Chilean copper exports** are actually subject to tariffs, significantly shielding Chile's revenue base. Commerce Secretary Lutnick has recommended phased tariffs on refined copper starting at 15% in 2027 and 30% in 2028, representing the major future risk.

A **seismic dispute erupted in early March 2026** over the **Chile China Express submarine cable project**: the US revoked visas for three Chilean government representatives, accusing them of compromising critical telecommunications infrastructure. Chile's Chamber of Deputies approved a request to seek withdrawal of US Ambassador Brandon Judd. This rift between the outgoing Boric government and Washington occurred days before the transition, creating an immediate foreign policy crisis for the incoming Kast team. Chile's total exports to the US reached **\$15.6 billion in 2024**, making it the second-largest destination after China.

Chilean public opinion has soured on the US: only a minority approves of Trump, and **majorities favor deepening trade ties with China**, viewing Beijing's influence more positively than Washington's.

<strong>China: structural dependency beyond any government's ability to sever</strong>

The Chile-China economic relationship has reached a scale that effectively constrains any Chilean government's strategic choices. **Bilateral trade hit \$61.66 billion in 2024** — 8.6 times the 2006 level and the highest per-capita trade value between China and any South American country. China has been Chile's largest trading partner for **12+ consecutive years**, absorbing **\$37.6 billion in exports** (40% of total) in 2024 alone. Major export categories include copper (\$23+ billion), lithium (\$6 billion to China in 2022), and cherries (\$3.3 billion in the 2024/25 season).

Chinese strategic penetration extends beyond trade. **Tianqi Lithium holds 23.77% of SQM** (acquired for \$4 billion in 2018), making it the second-largest shareholder. A May 2024 agreement gives SQM Salar de Atacama production rights through **2060**. China controls about **two-thirds of Chile's energy sector** through financial acquisitions. BYD and Tsingshan were selected in 2023 to build cathode/battery plants in Chile, though both projects paused by 2025 due to falling lithium prices and regulatory delays.

The digital infrastructure front is the sharpest battleground. Chile **"hard resisted"** US pressure to ban Huawei from 5G networks. Two competing submarine cable projects crystallize the rivalry: the **Humboldt Cable** (Valparaíso to Sydney via French Polynesia, partnership with Google, NEC-built) and the **Chile China Express** (backed by China Mobile), whose advancement triggered the March 2026 visa revocations. Kast's designated Foreign Minister Pérez Mackenna is expected to maintain "correct and functional" links with China while expressing a "strategic preference for the US" — but the 40% export dependency makes any meaningful decoupling operationally impossible.

<strong>EU: the advanced framework as strategic diversification</strong>

The **Chile-EU Advanced Framework Agreement** represents Chile's boldest effort to diversify beyond the US-China binary. The Interim Trade Agreement entered into force **February 1, 2025**, with the full AFA provisionally applied from **June 1, 2025**. The first EU-Chile Joint Council convened November 2025. The agreement makes **99.9% of EU exports tariff-free** and is projected to increase EU exports by **€4.5 billion**. Total EU-Chile goods trade surpassed **€21 billion in 2024**. Critically, the AFA is the first EU agreement with dedicated attention to **hydrogen**, ensuring non-discriminatory market access — a strategic play for Chile's green hydrogen ambitions.

<strong>Regional relationships: a rightward-tilting continent</strong>

The **Pacific Alliance** (Chile, Mexico, Colombia, Peru) has survived political turbulence — Mexico's AMLO refused to pass the presidency to Peru's Boluarte, and Colombia's Petro likewise refused recognition. Chile mediated. The Alliance-Singapore FTA entered force May 2025. Under Kast, ideological alignment within the Alliance improves (Colombia remains the outlier under Petro, with elections pending in 2026).

Kast visited **Milei the day after his election** (December 15, 2025), signaling the deepest ideological alignment between Buenos Aires and Santiago in decades. Both supported the January 2026 US military intervention in Venezuela. The **Ojeda assassination** (February 2024) — a Venezuelan ex-military dissident kidnapped and dismembered in Santiago by Tren de Aragua operatives allegedly on orders from Diosdado Cabello — severed Chilean-Venezuelan relations. Attorney General Valencia publicly named Cabello; Boric called Maduro a dictator. The January 2026 US capture of Maduro in "Operation Absolute Resolve" transformed the regional landscape entirely, with Kast calling it "great news for the region" while Boric condemned the strikes.

**Chile-Bolivia relations** experienced an unexpected thaw: Bolivia's new centrist president Rodrigo Paz (inaugurated November 2025) hosted Boric — the **first Chilean presidential visit to a Bolivian inauguration in 19 years**. FM meetings are planned, and visa elimination for diplomatic passports was agreed in April 2025. But Kast's border wall proposals may reverse this momentum. The fundamental constraint remains: Bolivia lost its Pacific coast in 1884 and has maintained only consular relations since 1978; the ICJ's 2018 ruling (12-3) that Chile has no obligation to negotiate sea access closed the legal avenue but not the emotional one.

<strong>Defense partnerships: deliberate diversification</strong>

Chile maintains defense relationships with multiple suppliers as a hedge against dependence on any single patron. **Three Type 23 frigates** from the UK (2006-2008, £134 million) are undergoing modernization with Lockheed Martin Canada combat systems, Hensoldt AESA radar, and MBDA Sea Ceptor missiles. **Two Scorpène-class submarines** from France (commissioned 2005-2006) are armed with WASS Black Shark torpedoes and SM-39 Exocet missiles. **Leopard 2A4 tanks** from Germany and **two Type 209 submarines** (with replacement planning underway) round out European procurement. Turkey's **Aselsan** provides electronic warfare systems; **Israel** supplies drones and communications. In August 2025, Boric approved a **National Shipbuilding Policy** targeting domestic construction of all surface vessels by 2030. Kast's Defense Minister Fernando Barros — who **defended Pinochet during the London extradition proceedings** — represents a qualitative shift in the civil-military relationship.

<strong>BRICS and multilateral positioning</strong>

Chile attended the 2025 BRICS Summit in Rio de Janeiro as a **guest** (not member or partner). Under-Secretary Gloria de la Fuente confirmed the Boric government was "evaluating potential benefits." Under Kast, BRICS membership is **very unlikely** — his pro-US alignment and Trump's hostility toward BRICS (threatening tariffs on members) effectively foreclose this option, though pragmatic advisors may prevent outright rejection given that \~45% of Chilean exports go to BRICS countries. Chile is neither a full member nor one of the 10 current BRICS partner countries.

## XV. Constitutional crises and regime transition patterns

Chile has undergone four major regime transitions since independence, each following a recognizable structural pattern: executive-legislative deadlock escalates under conditions of economic stress and institutional arbitration failure, eventually triggering military intervention or pacted resolution. **The two failed constitutional processes of 2022 and 2023 — one rejected from the left, one from the right — have paradoxically reinforced the 1980 Constitution's democratic legitimacy while leaving the structural grievances that produced the 2019 uprising entirely unaddressed.** Kast takes office with the strongest popular mandate since the return to democracy but faces the most constrained institutional environment of any incoming president.

<strong>The cyclical pattern: expansion and restoration</strong>

Chile's political history exhibits a striking pendulum regularity between periods of democratic expansion and conservative restoration:

**Cycle 1 (1920s–1930s)**: Alessandri's populist presidency and the 1925 Constitution expanded democratic participation and social rights, followed by military rule under Carlos Ibáñez del Campo (1927–1931) and conservative reassertion through the political system.

**Cycle 2 (1960s–1973)**: Frei Montalva's "Revolution in Liberty" and Allende's socialist experiment pushed redistribution and nationalization, terminated by the September 11, 1973 coup and Pinochet's 17-year dictatorship, which imposed a radical free-market restructuring via the Chicago Boys.

**Cycle 3 (2019–2026)**: The estallido social opened a constitutional reform window; Boric won as the most left-wing president since Allende; the Constitutional Convention attempted the world's most progressive constitution. The window closed with two failed plebiscites, Boric's approval collapsed to \~28%, and Kast's election with **58.2%** represents the most right-wing president since the dictatorship.

A structural regularity: **every Chilean presidential election since 2006 has produced alternation** between left and right (Bachelet → Piñera → Bachelet → Piñera → Boric → Kast). No incumbent coalition has won re-election.

<strong>Two constitutional failures and the "withdrawn citizen"</strong>

The first Constitutional Convention (2021–2022), elected under voluntary voting, was dominated by independents and the left. Its text — characterizing Chile as a "plurinational state," establishing autonomous indigenous territories, dismantling privatized water rights, enshrining abortion rights — was **rejected 62% to 38%** in the September 2022 exit plebiscite under newly restored compulsory voting. About **13 million voted**, versus 7.5 million in the Convention election — an influx of 6+ million new voters.

The second Constitutional Council (2023), where the Republican Party won 23 of 50 seats and veto power, produced a conservative text protecting prenatal life (threatening existing abortion laws) and reinforcing the neoliberal economic model. It was **rejected 55.76% to 44.24%** in December 2023.

The pivotal variable was the **"withdrawn citizen"** phenomenon: a stable segment of Chile's population characterized by low political engagement, institutional distrust, and no strong ideological identity. Under voluntary voting, these citizens abstained, producing an electorate skewed toward the politically engaged (and more progressive). Compulsory voting brought them in, and their default position was rejection of whatever the political class proposed. The entry and exit plebiscites were effectively votes by **different electorates**. Chile continues operating under the **amended 1980 Constitution**, which now possesses, as analyst Patricio Navia observed, new "democratic legitimacy" by virtue of voters rejecting both alternatives.

<strong>Kast's governing architecture: strong mandate, weak instruments</strong>

Kast's **58.2% runoff victory** (December 14, 2025) — winning all 16 regions with the highest vote total in Chilean democratic history — gives him an unusually strong popular mandate. But the congressional arithmetic is brutally constraining. The **Senate split 25-25** between left and right blocs; in the Chamber of Deputies, Kast's coalition (Cambio por Chile: Republicans, Christian Social Party, National Libertarians) holds **42 seats**, with the traditional right (Chile Grande y Unido: RN, UDI, Evópoli, Democrats) adding about **34**, for a combined **\~76 of 155 seats** — short of the 78-seat majority. The populist Partido de la Gente holds the swing vote. Organic constitutional laws requiring a **4/7 supermajority** are effectively beyond reach.

The cabinet announced January 20, 2026 reflects this reality: **only 8 of 24 ministers are party members**; 16 are independents drawn overwhelmingly from the private sector. Americas Quarterly analyst Robert Funk described it as resembling "a Board of Directors more than a cabinet." Key appointments include Finance Minister Jorge Quiroz (PhD Duke, Kast's principal economic advisor), Foreign Minister Francisco Pérez Mackenna (30-year CEO of the Luksic family's Quiñenco conglomerate), and — most controversially — Defense Minister Fernando Barros and Justice/Human Rights Minister Fernando Rabat, **both former Pinochet defense lawyers**. Johannes Kaiser's National Libertarian Party was excluded from the cabinet entirely.

<strong>Institutional veto architecture</strong>

Chile's post-1990 institutional framework creates a dense web of constraints on executive power that distinguishes it from most Latin American presidentialist systems:

The **Tribunal Constitucional** (10 members: 3 presidential appointees, 4 congressional, 3 Supreme Court) exercises both preventive and ex-post constitutional review and has been called the "Third Chamber" for its capacity to reshape legislation. The **Contraloría General** exercises the unique "toma de razón" power — pre-emptive legality review of executive decrees, with no equivalent in comparative law. The **Supreme Court** (21 justices, presidential appointment from Court-proposed lists, Senate confirmation) has increasingly asserted independence since democratization, including prosecution of hundreds of dictatorship-era human rights violators.

The 25-25 Senate split constrains all appointments requiring confirmation: Central Bank board members (Governor Rosanna Costa's term expires February 2027), Constitutional Tribunal members (requiring 2/3 congressional vote), and Supreme Court justices. GIS Reports' assessment: "Chile's presidency operates within a dense web of institutional veto points. Mr. Kast's mandate is therefore political rather than institutional."

<strong>Democratic erosion risk assessment</strong>

The appointment of two former Pinochet defense lawyers to Justice/Human Rights and Defense portfolios represents a qualitative departure from the post-1990 transitional justice consensus. Funding for the Museum of Memory (**500,000 visitors annually**), Villa Grimaldi, and the Plan Nacional de Búsqueda (searching for Pinochet-era disappeared) is secured through 2026 appropriations but vulnerable thereafter. Immigration enforcement expansion — an ICE-inspired agency, military border technology, criminalization of irregular entry (currently only administrative) — represents the area of greatest executive discretion, backed by **79% public support** for more restrictive migration policy. But the institutional architecture — divided Congress, autonomous Contraloría, Constitutional Tribunal, independent judiciary — creates substantial guardrails. The most probable outcome is pragmatic conservatism rather than institutional capture, but this assessment carries moderate confidence given the novelty of a far-right government within Chile's post-1990 democratic framework.

## XVI. Collective memory of humiliation and grievance

Chile's political landscape is structured by four overlapping memory formations — the 1973 coup, the War of the Pacific, the Mapuche dispossession, and the 2019 estallido — each actively narrativized by competing political factions and each constraining or enabling specific policy and alliance choices. **The most striking recent development is the erosion of the post-1990 consensus that the coup was unambiguously wrong**: polling shows those who say the coup was justified rose from 16% in 2013 to **36% in 2023**, while those saying it is never justified to carry out a coup fell to **41% — the lowest since 2003**.

<strong>September 11, 1973: a trauma whose political valence is shifting</strong>

The 50th anniversary in September 2023 crystallized the polarization. Boric organized extensive official commemorations at La Moneda, secured a joint declaration from all four living former presidents (Frei, Lagos, Bachelet, Piñera), and hosted heads of state from Mexico, Colombia, Bolivia, and Uruguay. The US sent Special Advisor Christopher Dodd and declassified Nixon-era daily briefs confirming US awareness of the coup plot.

The right's response was revealing: the opposition coalition "Let's Go Chile" issued a statement that **did not use the words "coup," "dictatorship," or "human rights violations"**, referring instead to a "breakdown of democracy" and refusing to participate in commemorations that honored Allende. Retired military brass protested that commemorations generated "division among compatriots."

CERC-MORI polling data tracks the tectonic shift: **47% of Chileans** now agree the Pinochet regime was "partly bad and partly good" (up from 27% a decade earlier). Nearly **40% believe Pinochet "modernized" the country**. Only **42%** believe the coup destroyed democracy — the lowest since 1995. **60%** expressed no interest in the 50th anniversary; **70%** believe commemorations "divide Chileans." As political scientist Claudia Heiss observed: "For young people, the dictatorship versus democracy axis is no longer a political faultline because they didn't live through it." Kast himself stated: "If Pinochet were alive, he would vote for me." On September 11, 2022, he posted: "On September 11th, 1973, Chile chose freedom."

<strong>The estallido social: competing traumas from a single event</strong>

The 2019 uprising has been narrativized into diametrically opposed memory formations. The left's framing — "It's Not 30 Pesos, It's 30 Years" — treats the estallido as a democratic awakening against three decades of inherited neoliberalism. The right, particularly Kast's Republicans, coined **"Octubrismo"** to conflate the uprising with its most destructive expressions. The right's narrative has won the quantitative battle: while **61.7% viewed the protests' impact positively in 2020**, that figure collapsed to **28.8% by October 2022**, with negative perceptions rising to 54.4%. By 2025, **50% of Chileans** view the estallido as "bad or very bad" for the country. Kast's victory is directly understood as the culmination of this backlash — the IRGAC assessed that "the new Chilean far-right has found in the Estallido a focal point for radicalization."

Human rights accountability for the estallido remains almost entirely unfulfilled: over 8,000 victims of state violence, 400+ severe eye injuries, but only a **0.2% justice rate**. The January 2024 indictment of three senior Carabineros commanders (including then-Director General Ricardo Yáñez) and August 2024 torture convictions of two officers represent milestones, but the five-year statute of limitations has expired for many cases.

<strong>War of the Pacific: the territorial myth that constrains diplomacy</strong>

The War of the Pacific (1879–1884) remains Chile's foundational national myth, with the **Battle of Iquique** (May 21) — celebrated as the Día de las Glorias Navales since 1905 — serving as the primary national commemorative event. Captain Arturo Prat's sacrificial last stand, though a military defeat, became the supreme rallying symbol. Prat is arguably Chile's greatest national hero; nearly every town has a street bearing his name.

The War's legacy creates enduring diplomatic constraints. Chile-Bolivia relations remain at consular-only level after **47+ years without ambassadors**, despite the ICJ's 2018 ruling definitively closing the legal avenue for Bolivian sovereign sea access. Bolivian children still sing songs about returning to the sea; Bolivia celebrates **"Día del Mar"** annually. The War's legacy also nearly derailed Bolivia's natural gas exports in 2002–2003, when domestic opposition blocked a Chilean pipeline route. Chile-Peru relations are more functional (Pacific Alliance partnership), but historical friction persists in border management. Chile's self-image as a "Pacific power" — flowing directly from the War's territorial acquisitions — drives its Indo-Pacific orientation and APEC engagement.

<strong>Mapuche memory: the "Pacificación" as ongoing colonial wound</strong>

The military occupation of Mapuche territory (1861–1883), euphemistically termed the "Pacificación de la Araucanía," reduced Mapuche territory to about **5% of its ancestral span** through the Títulos de Merced system (1884–1929), with an estimated **20,000–30,000 Mapuche** dead from violence, hunger, and disease. Three competing interpretations persist: the conservative view of national unification, the academic framing of ethnocide, and the Mapuche understanding of an invasion of a sovereign nation (Wallmapu).

CONADI has spent over **\$730 billion Chilean pesos (\~\$800M USD)** on land acquisition, returning nearly 300,000 hectares, but a 2022 study estimated **644,000 hectares** needed. CONADI itself projected **80–162 years** to clear the backlog. The Presidential Commission estimated total restitution costs at **\~\$4 billion USD**. Academic critique notes CONADI's reliance on settler-produced evidence (Títulos de Merced) while excluding claims for ancestral lands predating the reducciones. The November 2024 disappearance of **Julia Chuñil**, a 72-year-old Mapuche land defender whose community's land was returned to its non-indigenous owner by CONADI, became a national and international rallying point.

<strong>Memory sites under threat</strong>

Chile's commemorative infrastructure — the **Museo de la Memoria y los Derechos Humanos** (500,000 annual visitors), **Villa Grimaldi** Peace Park (former DINA torture center, 4,500 detainees, 240+ disappeared or killed), **Londres 38** (94 executed or disappeared), and dozens of other sites — faces its gravest threat since opening. Funding is secured through 2026 appropriations, but the Kast administration's "negacionismo" orientation (exemplified by the cabinet appointments) places these institutions at risk from 2027 onward. The Republican Party has proposed pardons for convicted Pinochet-era human rights violators (139 remain incarcerated) and systematically delegitimizes memory commemorations through social media strategies documented by the Transitional Justice Hub.

Kast's own family history adds a deeply personal dimension: his father **Michael Kast** was a confirmed **Nazi Party member** (joining NSDAP in September 1942 at age 18), fought in the German army, and emigrated to Chile in 1950. His brother **Miguel Kast** served as Pinochet's Labor Minister and Central Bank president. Kast himself campaigned for the "Yes" vote in the 1988 plebiscite.

## XVII. Cross-facet intersection analysis

<strong>Intersection 1: Compulsory voting × constitutional failure × Kast's mandate (Sections 15 × 13 × 16)</strong>

**Mechanism**: The reintroduction of compulsory voting in 2022 activated about **5–6 million "withdrawn citizens"** whose default political orientation is institutional distrust and status-quo bias. This single variable explains three otherwise puzzling outcomes: the rejection of the progressive constitution (62% against), the rejection of the conservative constitution (56% against), and Kast's 58.2% victory. The same population that rejected the right's constitutional project voted for the right's presidential candidate because the electoral question shifted from institutional design (where they rejected change in either direction) to immediate material concerns (crime, immigration). **This means Kast's mandate is substantively narrow despite being numerically overwhelming** — his support base chose him as a security and immigration executive, not as a transformative constitutional actor.

**Falsification**: If Kast interprets his mandate as broad and pushes maximalist cultural or constitutional reforms, the same withdrawn-citizen bloc that elected him would likely punish his coalition in the 2028 municipal elections.

<strong>Intersection 2: Chinese economic dependency × War of the Pacific territorial identity × defense diversification (Sections 14 × 16 × 14)</strong>

**Mechanism**: Chile's self-conception as a Pacific military power — rooted directly in War of the Pacific mythology — drives naval investment (Scorpène submarines, Type 23 frigates, the August 2025 National Shipbuilding Policy) that appears disproportionate for a country of its GDP. This naval identity simultaneously facilitates Chile's role as a Pacific trade hub (APEC founding member, CPTPP, Pacific Alliance) through which Chinese trade dependency has deepened. **The same territorial-maritime identity that makes Chile invest in anti-regional-threat naval capacity also makes it structurally open to transpacific commercial penetration.** The March 2026 submarine cable dispute — where Chinese digital infrastructure directly collides with US security demands — is the precise node where this intersection becomes operationally critical.

<strong>Intersection 3: Estallido social demobilization × think tank capture × Kast's technocratic cabinet (Sections 13 × 15 × 16)</strong>

**Mechanism**: The progressive movement's organizational dissolution after the estallido created an intellectual vacuum that right-wing think tanks filled. While Fundación Sol, Chile 21, and CIEPLAN were designed to support governments in power, their counterparts — Libertad y Desarrollo, FPP, Ideas Republicanas — spent the Boric years in opposition mode, sharpening their policy infrastructure. Kast's cabinet (only 8/24 party members, 16 private-sector independents) represents the **direct conversion of think tank and business-association intellectual capital into executive power**. This is the inverse of Boric's cabinet, which drew from social movements. The asymmetry is structural: right-wing think tanks (LyD, FJG, CEP) were designed during the 1990s specifically to preserve the dictatorship's institutional heritage through democratic means; left-wing think tanks were designed to support incumbent governments. The right's infrastructure is better adapted to opposition-to-power transitions.

<strong>Intersection 4: Evangelical growth among Mapuche × armed group church burnings × conservative electoral shift in Araucanía (Sections 13 × 16 × 15)</strong>

**Mechanism**: WAM's systematic burning of **80+ churches** in Araucanía — targeting both Catholic and evangelical congregations serving poor Mapuche communities — created a cross-cutting cleavage within the Mapuche population itself. With **35% of Mapuche in Araucanía identifying as evangelical**, church burnings alienated a large segment of the indigenous community from the broader Mapuche rights cause and pushed evangelical Mapuche toward Kast's security-first platform. This is a rare instance where **an armed group's tactical choices (church arson for territorial control) directly undermined the political cause they claim to advance** by fracturing potential solidarity along religious lines and providing the far right with both victims and voters from within the indigenous community.

<strong>Intersection 5: Ojeda assassination × Tren de Aragua × immigration politics × US-Chile security cooperation × Venezuela intervention (Sections 14 × 15 × 16)</strong>

**Mechanism**: The February 2024 kidnapping and murder of Venezuelan dissident Ronald Ojeda in Santiago by Tren de Aragua operatives — allegedly ordered by Diosdado Cabello — created a chain of cascading political effects that no single-facet analysis captures. The case (1) transformed immigration from an economic concern into a **security and sovereignty issue**, directly benefiting Kast's campaign; (2) deepened Chile-US law enforcement cooperation (three suspects extradited from Texas), creating bilateral institutional ties that survived the Boric-Trump political friction; (3) provided the evidentiary and political groundwork for Chile's acceptance of the January 2026 US military intervention in Venezuela under Kast; and (4) connected Chilean domestic politics to transnational organized crime networks in a way that makes the "mano dura" frame operationally credible rather than merely rhetorical. **The Ojeda case is the single event that best explains the domestic and international political realignment Chile underwent between 2024 and 2026.**

<strong>Intersection 6: Pinochet memory normalization × Kast cabinet appointments × institutional veto architecture (Sections 16 × 15 × 15)</strong>

**Mechanism**: The polling shift showing **36% of Chileans now viewing the 1973 coup as justified** (up from 16% in 2013) created the political space for Kast to appoint two former Pinochet defense lawyers (Barros, Rabat) to Defense and Justice/Human Rights — appointments that would have been politically impossible a decade ago. But the **institutional veto architecture** (25-25 Senate, Constitutional Tribunal, Contraloría) was designed precisely during the democratic transition to prevent the return of authoritarian governance. The intersection reveals a tension: memory normalization enables provocative appointments, but the institutional guardrails built by those who remembered the dictatorship's costs constrain what those appointees can actually do. The critical question is whether institutional constraints hold when the normative consensus underpinning them erodes.

## XVIII. Key analytical judgments

<strong>Judgment 1: Kast will govern as a pragmatic conservative focused on security and immigration, not as a transformative far-right leader.</strong>

*Confidence: HIGH (75%)*

The 25-25 Senate split, lack of Chamber majority, 4/7 supermajority requirement for organic laws, and technocratic cabinet composition all point toward constrained governance. Kast's post-election rhetorical moderation and "emergency government" framing indicate prioritization of deliverable security outcomes over culture-war maximalism. The GIS Reports assessment of "pragmatic conservatism" aligns with observable institutional constraints.

*Falsification conditions*: Kast attempts to modify the Constitutional Tribunal's composition through irregular means; proposes legislation to criminalize civil society organizations or restrict press freedom; uses emergency powers to bypass congressional approval for structural reforms; or the Partido de la Gente aligns with Republicans to create a working majority enabling maximalist legislation.

<strong>Judgment 2: Chile will not meaningfully reduce economic dependence on China during Kast's term, despite rhetorical alignment with Washington.</strong>

*Confidence: VERY HIGH (90%)*

**40% of Chilean exports go to China** (\$37.6 billion in 2024). The copper and lithium trade is structurally embedded, Tianqi Lithium holds 23.77% of SQM through 2060, and even ideologically anti-China leaders in the region (Milei) have maintained pragmatic commercial ties. The Kast administration will likely restrict Chinese digital infrastructure projects (submarine cables, potentially 5G) and limit military/space cooperation, but commercial flows will persist or grow. Chile's 35-FTA architecture is designed to prevent single-patron dependence, which paradoxically also prevents decoupling.

*Falsification conditions*: Chile actively blocks Chinese lithium investments; unilaterally cancels BRI MOU; imposes restrictions on Chinese copper purchases; or aligns with a US-led critical minerals exclusion zone that includes China.

<strong>Judgment 3: The feminist movement will serve as the primary vehicle for progressive opposition during Kast's presidency, surpassing labor unions and student movements.</strong>

*Confidence: MODERATE-HIGH (65%)*

The feminist movement (Coordinadora Feminista 8M) showed continued mobilization capacity with 40+ city mobilizations on March 8, 2025, while student movements and labor unions remain organizationally depleted. Kast's explicit opposition to feminist, LGBTQ+, and reproductive rights causes provides a clear adversarial frame. The movement's intersectional expansion (incorporating Mapuche women, migrant women, environmental concerns) broadens its coalition potential beyond any other progressive organizational vehicle.

*Falsification conditions*: Labor unions re-emerge as primary opposition force through general strike action; student movements achieve 2011-scale mobilizations; or a new social movement emerges around a different issue (e.g., water rights, pension reform) that eclipses feminist organizing.

<strong>Judgment 4: Chile's institutional guardrails will hold against democratic backsliding during Kast's term, but memory infrastructure will be significantly weakened.</strong>

*Confidence: MODERATE (60%)*

The Constitutional Tribunal, Contraloría, independent judiciary, and divided Congress create robust institutional checks. But **soft erosion** through budgetary strangulation of memory sites (Museum of Memory, Villa Grimaldi, Plan Nacional de Búsqueda), appointment of denialism-associated figures to sensitive portfolios, and non-enforcement of human rights frameworks does not require congressional approval and can be accomplished through executive discretion over appropriations from 2027 onward.

*Falsification conditions*: Constitutional Tribunal independence is compromised through packed appointments; Contraloría's "toma de razón" power is circumvented or legislatively weakened; judiciary faces direct political interference; or conversely, if memory sites maintain full funding and the Plan Nacional de Búsqueda continues unimpeded.

<strong>Judgment 5: The Mapuche conflict will escalate under Kast's securitized approach, producing a cycle of militarization and radicalization.</strong>

*Confidence: MODERATE (55%)*

Kast has pledged to end dialogue with armed groups, treat militants as "narco-terrorists," and deploy enhanced security forces. Historical precedent (Piñera's initial security-first approach was eventually complemented by dialogue; Boric's dialogue approach was eventually supplemented by continuous states of emergency) suggests neither pure approach works. WAM's November 2025 coordinated arson campaign shows operational capability; Llaitul's 23-year sentence removes a key CAM figure but may radicalize remaining members. The poverty differential (17.4% in Araucanía vs. 10.8% nationally) provides ongoing recruitment conditions. But the appointment of a corporate-focused cabinet rather than security hardliners to lead the effort may temper actual implementation.

*Falsification conditions*: Kast opens back-channel dialogue with Mapuche political organizations; violence metrics in the Southern Macrozone decline by 20%+ within 12 months; or CONADI land acquisition accelerates under the new administration.

<strong>Judgment 6: Chile's APEC participation at the November 2026 Shenzhen summit will be the first major test of Kast's foreign policy balancing act and will reveal his administration's operational (as opposed to rhetorical) stance on China.</strong>

*Confidence: HIGH (75%)*

Kast's first major multilateral moment as president occurs in China — the country that buys 40% of Chile's exports — while his political alignment is with China-skeptic Washington. The composition of the delegation, bilateral meetings sought, and any announced agreements or pulled projects will provide the clearest signal of where Kast's "strategic preference for the US" actually resolves against structural economic dependency.

*Falsification conditions*: Kast declines to attend or sends a low-level delegation (indicating hard anti-China turn); or uses the summit to announce expanded Chinese investment agreements (indicating rhetorical-only US alignment).

<strong>Judgment 7: The submarine cable dispute (Chile China Express vs. Humboldt) will be resolved in favor of the US-backed Humboldt cable within the first six months of Kast's presidency.</strong>

*Confidence: MODERATE-HIGH (65%)*

The March 2026 visa revocations represent maximum US pressure applied at the moment of political transition. Kast's pro-US orientation, Pérez Mackenna's corporate pragmatism, and the Google partnership on the Humboldt cable (providing commercial cover for a geostrategic choice) all point toward prioritizing the US-backed project. The China China Express cable has no equivalent private-sector champion on the Chilean side.

*Falsification conditions*: Chile proceeds with both cable projects simultaneously; the Chile China Express cable advances to construction phase; or Kast uses the dispute as leverage to extract US trade concessions.

## XX. Pipeline integration notes

<strong>Source interpretation guidance for Chilean media</strong>

<strong>Tier 1 — High reliability for analytical intelligence:</strong>
- **CIPER Chile** (Centro de Investigación Periodística): Nonprofit, non-partisan, investigative. Chile's most credible outlet for corruption and institutional analysis. Use as primary factual source.
- **Contraloría General website**: Official records of decree legality reviews. Direct institutional data.
- **CEP surveys**: Chile's gold-standard polling, conducted since 1987. Methodologically rigorous, centrist-technocratic framing.

<strong>Tier 2 — Reliable with known biases (calibrate accordingly):</strong>
- **La Tercera**: Center-right, owned by COPESA (Álvaro Saieh, UDI-linked). Best for political insider reporting; bias favors right-wing administrations. Citizens rate at \~6.6/10 on right-left scale.
- **El Mercurio/Emol**: Chile's paper of record, owned by Edwards family. Right-leaning (citizens rate \~7.8/10). Broadest coverage; editorial line historically supported Pinochet, received CIA funds in 1970s. Systemic bias toward establishment/business perspectives. Owns 21 regional papers — useful for provincial intelligence.
- **Ex-Ante**: Center-right analytical, founded 2020 by former El Mercurio journalists. Emerging as key opinion platform for the Kast-era right. Good for understanding right-wing intellectual currents.
- **Pauta**: Center/center-right digital outlet. Solid for policy analysis, business orientation.
- **El Mostrador**: Center-left digital native. Counter-balance to El Mercurio/La Tercera dominance. Strong social media presence.

<strong>Tier 3 — Useful for specific angles; handle with care:</strong>
- **Interferencia**: Left-leaning investigative. Useful for counter-narratives on power structures and security forces; ideological framing must be discounted.
- **Fundación Sol publications**: Left-wing labor data. Their wage and inequality statistics are frequently cited even by centrist outlets but framing is consistently adversarial to market orthodoxy.
- **MapuExpress**: Mapuche community media. Essential for indigenous perspective rarely captured by mainstream outlets; advocacy framing must be noted.

**Media concentration warning**: El Mercurio and COPESA groups control **\~90% of print media** and dominate digital. **Grupo Luksic** (whose holdings were managed by Kast's new Foreign Minister Pérez Mackenna for 30 years) owns Channel 13 TV. This structural concentration means Chilean mainstream media systematically underrepresents left and indigenous perspectives. Trust in news overall: **35%**.

**Social media**: TikTok is the fastest-growing platform (39% usage, 18% for news). X/Twitter remains the primary arena for political discourse and breaking news. "Second screen" consumption is widespread. Disinformation and bot activity — particularly around the estallido and constitutional processes — has been documented but not systematically measured.

<strong>Event significance thresholds</strong>

**Routine (background monitoring)**: Monthly state-of-emergency renewals in Southern Macrozone; individual arson incidents (fewer than 3 per week); congressional committee hearings on pending legislation; Cadem weekly polling shifts of fewer than 5 points.

**Elevated (active tracking)**: Coordinated militant operations (3+ simultaneous incidents); large protest mobilizations (50,000+ in Santiago); cabinet reshuffles; major judicial rulings on estallido accountability or Mapuche cases; shifts in Partido de la Gente congressional alignment; BRICS or APEC-related diplomatic developments; any restriction of INDH mandate or memory site funding.

**Critical (immediate analysis required)**: General strikes called by CUT; Carabineros use of lethal force against protesters; Constitutional Tribunal rulings blocking executive decrees; rupture between Partido Republicano and Chile Vamos coalition; any move to modify Constitutional Tribunal composition; diplomatic break with a major partner (China, US, EU); large-scale Mapuche violence (casualty events); attempted pardon of Pinochet-era human rights convicts; any foreign agent-type legislation proposal.

**Structural (paradigm reassessment)**: Kast administration bypasses institutional veto points; military deployment to civilian areas beyond Southern Macrozone; dissolution or functional gutting of INDH; formal Chilean alignment with BRICS or formal exit from US-led institutional frameworks; mass social mobilization approaching estallido scale (100,000+ sustained over multiple days); impeachment proceedings against president or senior officials.

<strong>Common misreading patterns</strong>

**1. Conflating Kast's mandate with institutional power.** His 58.2% vote share is historically high, but the 25-25 Senate and lack of Chamber majority mean his *operational* power is more constrained than any recent Chilean president. Analysts who project Kast's campaign rhetoric into governance outcomes without accounting for institutional veto points will systematically overestimate the pace and scope of policy change.

**2. Treating Chile-China trade dependency as a policy choice.** At 40% of exports, this is a structural feature, not a diplomatic preference. Even the most pro-US Chilean government cannot decouple from Chinese demand for copper and lithium without accepting macroeconomic catastrophe. Reporting that frames Kast's pro-US orientation as necessarily anti-China misunderstands the structural constraints.

**3. Applying the "Pink Tide/Blue Tide" frame uncritically.** Chile's left-right alternation is driven more by compulsory-voting-activated "withdrawn citizens" and anti-incumbency sentiment than by ideological waves. The same electorate that gave Boric 56% in 2021 (voluntary voting, smaller electorate) gave Kast 58% in 2025 (compulsory voting, larger electorate). These were *different electorates*, not an ideological conversion.

**4. Conflating Mapuche civil society with armed groups.** The distinction between organizations like Identidad Territorial Lafkenche (which has elected congressional representatives) and WAM (which burns churches and ambushes gendarmes) is not a spectrum — it is a categorical divide. Kast's "narco-terrorist" framing deliberately collapses this distinction; intelligence analysis should not replicate this conflation.

**5. Reading Chilean protest numbers as indicators of regime instability.** Chile has deeply institutionalized protest culture. March 8 feminist mobilizations routinely draw hundreds of thousands; October 18 commemorations produce large turnout. These are expressions of a functioning civil society, not pre-revolutionary indicators. The threshold for regime-stability concern is sustained, multi-sector mobilization with explicit anti-government framing exceeding 100,000 over multiple days — comparable to October-November 2019.

**6. Misreading the constitutional status quo as resolution.** Chile still operates under the amended 1980 Constitution, but the underlying grievances (inequality, privatized pensions, healthcare, education, water rights) that produced the estallido remain unaddressed. The constitutional process failed as a *mechanism*, not because the problems it sought to address were resolved. This latent structural tension persists beneath the surface stability.
