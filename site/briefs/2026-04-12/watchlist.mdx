## Items on Watch

The most significant development to watch is how China responds as its neighbors coordinate their security infrastructure. Japan, South Korea and Taiwan are linking defense systems and sharing technology in ways that could shift regional military balances. If Beijing responds with military moves — new deployments, changed positions, or direct counter-measures to this alliance coordination — it would show the competition is moving from bilateral tensions to alliance-building.

This security coordination connects to American efforts to shift alliance costs. Washington is pushing Japan, South Korea and Australia to shoulder heavier defense burdens beyond current agreements. The trigger is whether this becomes systematic: specific spending targets, required capabilities, or new cost-sharing deals that go beyond existing guidelines. Such moves would reveal the US is restructuring its alliances, not just asking for bigger budgets.

Separately, Western democracies under political stress at home are showing signs of coordinating responses. France, Britain, Italy, Canada and Australia all face similar pressures — polarization, strained institutions, falling trust in government. What matters is whether they develop shared solutions or ways to coordinate their responses to democratic stress. Evidence of such coordination would suggest these countries see democratic fragility as a collective problem needing collective answers.
