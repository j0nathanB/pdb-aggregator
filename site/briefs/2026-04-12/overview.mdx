## Week of April 06, 2026


International institutions are not breaking down—they are becoming choosy. The same shock that triggers smooth coordination on political issues will produce purely national responses on economic ones. This split, now confirmed across all major regions, shows a new pattern where crisis response depends more on the type of challenge than the strength of current frameworks.

Energy disruption from Middle East conflict revealed this pattern. When the same shock hit three Asia-Pacific countries, each responded with a different national plan: Australia used LNG exports for fuel deals with other countries, Japan deployed strategic reserves while quadrupling US imports, Indonesia sought Russian energy cooperation through direct presidential talks. The region failed to coordinate despite shared systems. Europe's energy responses remained fragmented despite the EU's machinery. Yet when Hungary's democratic transition created a political opening, European coordination snapped into action. Ukraine gained an EU integration breakthrough, Poland's position strengthened, and Czech and Latvian responses were prepared in advance through synchronised EU channels. The same system that failed on energy worked smoothly on politics.

Military alliances show this selectivity most clearly—combat cooperation strengthens even as diplomatic relationships fragment. Pakistan activated its Strategic Mutual Defense Agreement with Saudi Arabia for the first time, deploying fighter jets and troops to King Abdulaziz Air Base during Iranian attacks. The military commitment proved reliable. Meanwhile, NATO allies maintain security cooperation while publicly rejecting US diplomatic approaches: Britain shelved its Chagos handover after American pressure, Norway criticised Washington's Hormuz blockade threats, Sweden disagreed with US strategy. The alliances hold for actual fighting while political cooperation fails.

International institutions adapt to crisis pressure rather than simply collapse under it. Military needs trump political disagreements. Political transitions enable coordination while economic shocks trigger national responses. The question is whether this choosy approach can sustain the international system or whether the gaps between different areas will eventually pull the whole structure apart.
