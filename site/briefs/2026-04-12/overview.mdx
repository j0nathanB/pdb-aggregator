## Week of April 06, 2026


When Middle East energy disruptions hit this week, every region responded alone. Australia cut separate fuel deals using its gas exports. Japan quadrupled oil imports from the US and tapped strategic reserves. Prabowo Subianto, Indonesia's president, flew to Moscow for Russian energy cooperation. Each chose a different path to the same problem, and none worked with anyone else. The pattern held everywhere — American allies pursued separate deals, European countries ignored shared EU rules. Faced with the same shock, every government turned to national solutions.

This confirms a permanent shift in how the world handles crises. The machinery for working together built over decades does not work when it is needed most. Whether the region has deep bonds like Europe or loose links like the Americas makes no difference. When pressure hits, countries look inward first and outward never. The world's systems for handling economic crises have not just weakened — they have fundamentally failed.

Yet Europe offers a different pattern when the crisis is political rather than economic. Hungary's democratic transition this week prompted swift, effective responses across both Western and Eastern Europe. The EU sent congratulations through official channels. Ukraine gained its breakthrough, Poland's position strengthened, and Czech and Latvian responses came ready-made. Remove Hungarian obstruction, and European cooperation suddenly works. This suggests the EU works for political alignment but lacks tools for economic crisis response.

Alliance systems show the same pattern through a military lens. Pakistan used its defense agreement with Saudi Arabia this week, deploying fighter jets and troops to King Abdulaziz Air Base during Iranian attacks — the first military deployment under their pact. Meanwhile, NATO allies work together on security while rejecting US diplomatic approaches: Britain shelved the Chagos handover after American pressure, Norway criticized Hormuz blockade threats, Sweden disagreed with US strategy. Military commitments prove reliable even as political cooperation fragments.

The world order is not breaking down uniformly — it is splitting by type of crisis. Military alliances hold when fighting demands it. Political bodies cooperate when obstacles are removed. But economic crisis response has turned to national solutions, regardless of how integrated the region claims to be. This represents not failure but evolution, where different parts of the world order respond to different types of pressure. The question is whether this selective response represents adaptation or the first stage of broader breakdown.
