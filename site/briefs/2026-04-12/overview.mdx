## Week of April 06, 2026


**Crisis Breaks Cooperation Selectively**

International cooperation does not break down evenly in crisis — it splits by type. Some forms strengthen while others collapse. Economic shocks trigger national responses even among close allies, but political and military cooperation can deepen simultaneously. This split may show the international system adapting to pressure rather than failing.

The Middle East energy crisis showed that economic shocks now trigger national responses across every region, whatever coordination exists. Australia used liquefied natural gas exports for fuel deals with individual countries, Japan used strategic reserves while quadrupling imports from the United States, and Indonesia pursued Russian energy cooperation through direct presidential talks. Each response ignored regional partners. Even Europe, despite its institutional machinery, produced no coordinated response. The pattern held across Asia-Pacific, the Americas, and Western Europe — shared shocks push countries toward national responses.

Yet other forms of international cooperation flourished during the same period. European coordination expanded beyond crisis response when Hungary's democratic transition removed a key obstacle. Western Europe sent congratulations through European Union channels while Eastern Europe achieved policy breakthroughs. Ukraine gained its EU integration breakthrough, Poland strengthened its position, and the Czech Republic and Latvia prepared responses in advance. This was cross-regional coordination when political rather than economic issues were involved.

Alliances showed a similar split between operational strength and diplomatic breakdown. Pakistan activated its Strategic Mutual Defense Agreement with Saudi Arabia, deploying fighter jets and troops to King Abdulaziz Air Base during Iranian attacks — the first actual military deployment under their pact. Meanwhile, Western European NATO allies maintained security cooperation while publicly rejecting US diplomatic moves. Britain shelved its Chagos handover after American pressure, Norway criticised Hormuz blockade threats, and Sweden disagreed with US NATO strategy. Military commitments proved reliable while political coordination broke down.

This split suggests that international institutions are adapting to crisis pressure rather than breaking down. Economic coordination fails because states put immediate national needs first during resource shocks. Political coordination succeeds when it advances shared values without immediate costs. Military cooperation endures because operational needs override diplomatic disagreements. The result is not collapse but specialisation — each type of cooperation operating by its own logic under pressure. Whether this represents lasting adaptation or temporary crisis management will determine the shape of the international system that emerges from current global tensions.
