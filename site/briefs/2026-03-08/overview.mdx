## Week of March 02, 2026

The American security order is fracturing along regional lines as allies build separate machinery for managing shared threats. The Iran crisis has become the first test of how well these different approaches work under pressure, and the results suggest that America's partners are no longer betting everything on Washington's protection.

Each region has responded to the Iran crisis in ways that reveal the limits of its security deals. In Western Europe, unilateral American actions have exposed how little say European allies actually have within NATO structures. Across the Middle East and South Asia, countries that rarely coordinate — Turkey, India, and others — have found themselves sharing the same vulnerabilities and working together in ways they never did before. In Asia-Pacific, governments have defaulted to separate bilateral deals with Washington rather than multilateral responses, even though the region has spent years deepening alliance ties on paper. The same crisis, three different responses.

This pattern extends far beyond Iran. Middle powers everywhere have adopted the same strategy: hedge. Canada diversifies into the Indo-Pacific while keeping its American partnership intact. Mexico cooperates with Washington on managing crises but criticises American policies in international forums. Brazil positions itself as a Global South leader while maintaining trade ties to both superpowers. The Asia-Pacific shows the same pattern — countries maintain their American alliances while building separate relationships that preserve their options. This is not regional coordination. It is adaptation. Middle powers have concluded that siding completely with any great power is too risky, and they are all making the same bet on flexibility.

The clearest sign comes from Europe, where France has begun offering nuclear deterrence to allies who doubt American commitment. Norway is openly discussing nuclear cooperation with Paris. Sweden has joined French-led deterrence talks. Both countries remain NATO members, but they are exploring what happens if American extended deterrence proves unreliable. France is positioning itself as Europe's alternative nuclear guarantor, and Nordic countries that spent decades relying on American protection are listening. If this gains traction, Europe could end up with competing nuclear systems within the same formal alliance — French and American — while the facade stays intact.
