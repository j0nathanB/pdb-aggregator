## Week of February 23, 2026

The structures holding alliances together continue to fragment — governing parties splitting, coalitions collapsing, institutions buckling — but the alliances themselves carry on. Several democracies continue facing serious political trouble at home, from elite ruptures in Western Europe to constitutional fights on the eastern front line. Yet NATO meetings proceed, defence coordination continues, and military cooperation proceeds on schedule. This selective resilience masks a deeper problem: alliances can manage the ordinary but break apart when it matters most.

Countries with different political systems, in different regions, facing different local pressures, are all cracking up at once. This goes beyond normal electoral cycles or local scandals. Democratic systems face shared pressures they cannot absorb. When governing parties weaken across five Western European countries while institutions buckle across four frontline Eastern European states, the stress is systemic. Alliance coordination now depends on politically weakened governments that may lack the authority to deliver on international commitments, even when they want to.

The Iran crisis exposed how this domestic weakness connects to international fragmentation. When the United States and Israel struck Iranian targets, allied responses split along regional lines rather than converging around shared principles. The Americas fell back on different approaches that ignored hemispheric ties. Western Europe chose to go its own way over unified response. Asia-Pacific kept separate deals rather than coordinated action. American alliances now work as separate regional blocs, not an integrated global system. Countries with weakened governments found it easier to hedge than to stand together.

This selective resilience — institutional continuity during routine operations, fragmentation during crises — marks a shift in how alliances function. The machinery built up enough momentum to run itself for planned activities: defence budgets, joint exercises, bureaucratic coordination. But crisis solidarity requires political will that damaged governments cannot summon and regional blocs cannot bridge. The result is alliances that look strong on paper but fragment when tested, creating the worst of both worlds for deterrence and crisis management.
