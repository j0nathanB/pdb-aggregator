## Week of March 09, 2026

Three crises hit this week, each exposing the same flaw: when pressure mounts, international cooperation collapses. America's allies are breaking ranks across every region. Seventeen democracies face political turmoil at once. And when Iran's oil shock hit global markets, major economies acted alone instead of together.

Allies are defecting everywhere. Canada has rejected American sanctions and promised to defend Greenland against annexation. Brazil revoked visas for Trump advisers. Chile backs Washington in public but faces domestic pressure that prevents action. This matches the rebellion already under way in Europe — France's counter-offensive, Germany's power-politics doctrine — and cost disputes across Asia-Pacific. America's post-war alliance system is failing in every region simultaneously. The problem is not specific grievances but America's ability to manage alliances under pressure.

Democratic governments are buckling in sync. In Western Europe, ruling parties face pressure in six of seven major countries — France's constitutional crisis, Germany's CDU weakness, Britain's Green Party gains, Italy's referendum risks, Spain's contested foreign policy, Sweden's far-right surge. Add existing troubles in Eastern Europe and the Americas, and seventeen democracies now face political pressure across three regions. The timing suggests deeper forces at work, weakening governments just when international action is needed most.

The Iran crisis showed how far cooperation has broken down. Japan became the first G7 country to tap strategic oil reserves alone rather than coordinate with allies. South Korea, Australia, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, India and Pakistan each acted separately, using different channels and timing despite facing the same shock. When a shared energy crisis hit, countries chose solo responses over joint action. The result is not coordination but twenty countries solving the same problem in twenty different ways.
