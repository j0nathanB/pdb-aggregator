## Week of January 26, 2026

Allied countries are splitting over the basics of burden-sharing, and the splits are hardening along regional lines.

In Europe, the Czech Republic has rejected NATO's 3.5% spending target outright, while Romania secured €16.6 billion in EU defence funding and Finland aims for 3% of GDP by 2029. France, Germany and Spain are each articulating versions of strategic autonomy — separately, and without American participation. They agree on the direction but not the details, and Washington is not in the room.

In Asia, the pressures point in opposite directions. Japan is deepening its operational planning for a Taiwan contingency. South Korea is pursuing what it calls a "full-scale restoration" of relations with China. Both are responding to American unreliability, but their answers are incompatible.

In the Americas, there is no regional logic at all. Canada is openly defying Washington, Mexico is quietly accommodating it, and Brazil is balancing between the two — three responses to the same pressure with no coordination among them. Turkey, Saudi Arabia and the UAE show the same pattern in the Middle East: Turkey positions itself as a mediator, Saudi Arabia triangulates, and the UAE declares neutrality on Iran.

The result is that Washington now faces three distinct alliance architectures with different operating assumptions, rather than one system it can manage from the centre. And as middle powers pursue separate deals with great powers rather than acting collectively, they lose whatever leverage a coalition might have given them. There are early signs of this pattern across several regions, though some of the evidence rests on single-source reporting and should be read cautiously.

Something to watch is whether the European and Asian cracks begin to connect, whether middle powers attempt any cross-regional coordination, and how Washington adapts to managing allies that no longer agree on what the alliance is for.
