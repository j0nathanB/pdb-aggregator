## Week of February 02, 2026

Allied countries are splitting over who should pay for defence and what they should defend, and the cracks are hardening along regional lines. In Europe, the Czech Republic has rejected NATO's 3.5% spending target outright. Romania secured €16.6bn in EU defence funding. Finland aims for 3% of GDP by 2029. In Asia, the pressures pull in opposite directions: Japan is deepening its operational planning for a war over Taiwan while South Korea pursues what it calls a "full-scale restoration" of relations with China. Both are responding to American arm-twisting, but their answers cannot be squared.

Each region is building its own machinery for managing this pressure, and none of the machines fit together. In Europe, countries are forging separate plans that point in roughly the same direction — France's counter-offensive, Germany's power-politics doctrine, Spain's go-it-alone migration policy — but differ on the details, and Washington is not in the room. In the Americas, each country has struck a different deal with the United States and none of them are joined up. In the Asia-Pacific, each ally has its own arrangement with Washington, each shaped by how much favour it enjoys.

Middle powers have given up on acting together and are hedging alone. Canada defies American pressure openly, Mexico goes along in practice, and Brazil plays both sides — all without talking to one another. The Middle East follows the same pattern: Turkey positions itself as a mediator, Saudi Arabia tries to keep every door open, and the UAE sits out the fight over Iran. No co-ordination, no collective leverage.

This makes it all but impossible for smaller powers to band together, and forces each to come to terms with the great powers on its own. The fragmentation hands opportunities to those great powers, who can pick off allies one by one. The United States, for its part, must now manage three alliance systems that work on different terms rather than a single coherent one. When allies cannot agree on the basics of who pays and what for, the alliance becomes a forum for managing disagreement rather than a vehicle for action.
