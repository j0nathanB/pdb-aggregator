## Week of February 02, 2026

Allied countries are splitting over the basics of burden-sharing and strategic priorities, and the splits are hardening along regional lines. In Europe, the Czech Republic has rejected NATO's 3.5% spending target outright, while Romania secured €16.6 billion in EU defence funding and Finland aims for 3% of GDP by 2029. In Asia, the pressures point in opposite directions: Japan is deepening operational planning for a Taiwan contingency while South Korea simultaneously pursues what it calls a "full-scale restoration" of relations with China. Both are responding to American pressure, but their answers are incompatible.

Different regions are developing incompatible institutional mechanisms for managing this pressure. Europe is coordinating strategic autonomy through parallel national articulations — France's counter-offensive, Germany's power politics doctrine, Spain's autonomous migration policy — without American participation. They agree on the direction but not the details, and Washington is not in the room. The Americas pursues systematically divergent bilateral approaches with no regional coordination. Asia-Pacific adapts through separate bilateral frameworks, each tailored to differential alliance treatment.

Middle powers are abandoning coordinated responses entirely, instead pursuing individual hedging strategies. In the Americas, Canada defies American pressure explicitly, Mexico accommodates operationally, and Brazil pursues strategic balancing — all independent responses. The Middle East shows the same pattern: Turkey positions as mediator, Saudi Arabia pursues triangulation, and the UAE declares neutrality on Iran. No coordination, no collective leverage.

This eliminates the possibility of meaningful coalition-building among secondary powers and forces individual accommodation with great power preferences. The fragmentation creates opportunities for great powers to exploit divisions while the United States must now manage three distinct alliance architectures with incompatible operating principles rather than a unified system. When allies cannot agree on burden-sharing fundamentals, the alliance becomes a framework for managing disagreement rather than coordinating action.
