## Regional Summary

**Bold Abroad, Broken at Home**

The Paris summit this week was the clearest sign yet that Europe can act without the United States when it decides to. Fifty nations gathered to plan a naval mission through the Strait of Hormuz, and Washington was not invited. France and Britain will co-lead the military planning. Italy dropped its long-standing requirement for a UN Security Council mandate to join. The Élysée called it a "third way" between American maximum pressure and an Iranian blockade, and 50 governments signed on. By some distance, it was the clearest proof in years that Europe could act alone. The problem is that the governments making this assertion cannot pass their own legislation.

France illustrates the gap most sharply. Sébastien Lecornu, the prime minister, suffered what French outlets called his "black week" — the sharpest parliamentary setback since he took office. His own coalition's absenteeism, not the opposition, defeated his unemployment insurance bill. He retreated on May Day work legislation after trade union threats, withdrew an antisemitism bill, and warned publicly of "worrying demobilisation" among deputies; privately, coalition members told reporters he had made them "look like fools." His approval fell to 32%. That same week, Emmanuel Macron turned the death of a French soldier in a roadside ambush near Ghanduriyah into diplomatic pressure, publicly blaming Hezbollah — sharper language than France usually uses in such incidents — and summoning the Lebanese prime minister to the Élysée. The confidence abroad and the dysfunction at home are not accidents of the same week; they are the same government's two faces. Meanwhile, Jordan Bardella, the National Rally's president, lunched with the full executive board of the Movement of French Enterprises (Medef), France's main employers' federation — the first time his party had been received that way — a signal that business has begun treating the National Rally as a government-in-waiting while the current one cannot govern.

Italy makes the same point in sharper form. Giorgia Meloni spent the week repositioning on nearly every major relationship Italy has. She refused US bombers use of Sigonella, attended the Paris summit, suspended a defence cooperation memorandum with Israel, and — through her defence minister, Guido Crosetto — publicly questioned whether the UN mission in Lebanon still makes sense, the language with which withdrawals tend to begin. Four consequential decisions in one week, each pulling Italy away from its earlier positions. The American president responded by attacking Ms Meloni in the pages of Corriere della Sera, Italy's newspaper of record — no modern US president has done such a thing to a sitting allied leader in her own country's press — and threatened to withdraw security guarantees. Ms Meloni came out personally stronger: 80% of Italians view the American president unfavourably, and even the opposition defended her. But her deputy prime minister, Matteo Salvini, was simultaneously addressing a badly attended rally in Milan, calling for remigration and a return to Russian gas — positions that put him at odds with Ms Meloni — while her coalition partner Forza Italia brought its own demonstrators to the same square as a direct rejoinder. A government repositioning on four fronts at once while its own coalition pulls in opposite directions is placing bets it may not be able to settle.

Britain is the inverse case: a government trying to hold the Atlantic relationship together just as it destroys the apparatus for doing so. Peter Mandelson took the Washington post without passing the required security vetting. Keir Starmer sacked Olly Robbins, the Foreign Office's most senior civil servant, claiming Mr Robbins had kept him in the dark — an explanation every opposition leader rejected. King Charles and Queen Camilla arrive in Washington on April 27 for a state visit designed to bypass the friction between Downing Street and the White House. That channel depends on coordination between Whitehall officials and their Washington counterparts. The apparatus for it has just been gutted: the ambassador's vetting status is disputed, the permanent secretary is gone, and the government's communications have been consumed by the crisis. Kemi Badenoch compressed the choice into four words — "lying or grossly incompetent" — and the phrase ran from Reuters to Al Jazeera. Labour's 411-seat majority makes a confidence vote impossible, but parliamentary arithmetic cannot restore governing authority that is draining away.

The Hormuz coalition's durability will be tested precisely when it matters — when military planning turns concrete, when parliamentary authorisations are needed, and when governments are asked to sustain commitments through domestic turbulence they are already struggling to manage. France cannot pass unemployment insurance. Britain has gutted its Foreign Office. Italy's government is moving in two directions at once. Friedrich Merz, the German chancellor, who said publicly it would be "desirable" for the United States to be involved, has already signalled that the non-American premise is contested even among the summit's participants. Acting without Washington proved the easy part. Holding together the governments that agreed to do so is another matter.

### <img src="https://flagcdn.com/fr.svg" alt="France flag" style={{ width: "24px", height: "18px", display: "inline", verticalAlign: "middle", marginRight: "8px" }} /> France

France enters a period of simultaneous external activation and domestic regime deterioration. The Hormuz conference marks the clearest operationalization of strategic autonomy doctrine to date: France co-led a 50-nation coalition explicitly excluding the US to plan a non-belligerent maritime security mission, converting rhetoric into institutional architecture. The FINUL soldier's death in Lebanon added an operational dimension to French security engagement — France is conducting real mine-clearance missions under hostile conditions — while Macron's direct Hezbollah attribution and Lebanese PM summons demonstrate willingness to deploy diplomatic pressure through the military commitment. Domestically, the 'black week' represents the sharpest intra-coalition breakdown since Lecornu's October appointment: his own majority defeated his own bills through absenteeism, the Attal-Lecornu rivalry is now publicly visible as a pre-2027 fracture line, and approval reached a record low of 32%. RN's business normalization moved from private dinners to formal institutional encounters with Medef's executive bureau. The government is now demonstrably unable to legislate even without active opposition mobilization, while the RN systematically acquires business elite legitimacy. The economic transformation narrative faces mild implementation pressure as Lecornu's legislative capacity erodes. The overarching dynamic: France is more active externally (Middle East crisis creating both threats and leadership opportunities) and more fragile internally (coalition decomposition accelerating toward 2027) — the two trends are partially connected through the fuel price crisis reducing domestic popular support for a government championing long-term strategic commitments.

**Key developments:**

- **Diplomatic:** Macron and Starmer co-chaired a Paris conference with approximately 50 countries and international organizations, including France, Germany, UK, and Italy in person and the remainder by videoconference, to plan a 'strictly peaceful and defensive' maritime security mission for the Strait of Hormuz. The US was explicitly excluded: the Élysée stated France 'will not enter a coalition with the Americans simply because we are not parties to the conflict.' German Chancellor Merz dissented publicly, stating it would be 'desirable' for the US to be involved. The mission is conditional on ceasefire consolidation and will be developed through a follow-on military planning conference in London. Macron described the initiative as a 'third way' between US maximum pressure and Iranian blockade.
- **Diplomatic:** Sgt-Chef Florian Montorio of the 17th Parachute Engineer Regiment was killed April 18 in a roadside ambush in southern Lebanon during a FINUL mine-clearance mission near Ghanduriyah; three others were wounded, two seriously. Macron stated on social media that 'all evidence suggests Hezbollah bears responsibility' and demanded 'immediate arrests' from Lebanese authorities. FINUL opened an investigation, with initial findings indicating 'non-state actors (presumably Hezbollah).' Hezbollah denied involvement. Macron announced he would receive Lebanese PM Nawaf Salam at the Élysée on April 21. Montorio's remains were repatriated April 19.
- **Security:** Minister of Armed Forces Catherine Vautrin confirmed that Montorio was killed 'in the region of Deir-Kifa in Lebanon' while 'on a mission to open a route to an isolated FINUL post that had been cut off for several days by fighting in the area.' He was 'caught in an ambush by an armed group at very close range' and 'immediately hit by direct small arms fire.' He was 'evacuated under fire by his comrades, who were unable to revive him.' FINUL confirmed the patrol was 'tasked with demining a road in the village of Ghanduriyah in order to restore communications with isolated FINUL positions.' Two of the three wounded soldiers were seriously injured.
- **Security:** The Élysée formally announced the 'Strait of Hormuz Maritime Navigation Initiative' on April 17, aimed at supporting and securing merchant vessels transiting the Gulf and providing insurers and shipowners visibility to resume maritime activity. France and the UK announced joint co-leadership of the military planning process, with a London military planning conference to follow. Starmer confirmed 'dozens of countries have offered to contribute assets.' The mission is explicitly conditional on ceasefire consolidation and will be 'strictly peaceful and defensive.' France is providing the political and institutional architecture; the UK is providing co-leadership and basing for planning.
- **Domestic:** In a single parliamentary week: (1) the unemployment insurance bill — an agreement on voluntary redundancy terms — was defeated by coalition absenteeism ('un revers majeur' per Les Echos); (2) Lecornu abandoned May 1st work legislation after union censure threats, allowing florists and bakers to open without sanctions rather than forcing a vote; (3) the antisemitism bill was withdrawn; (4) Lecornu warned publicly of 'preoccupying demobilization' among deputies. Behind closed doors, coalition deputies accused him of making them 'look like fools' ('on nous pisse dessus'). Gabriel Attal boycotted the Monday coalition leaders' dinner at Matignon. Lecornu's approval fell 4 points to 32%; Macron's approval also declined amid the fuel price crisis. Le Parisien reported Lecornu 'didn't expect it to go up in flames' — indicating inadequate parliamentary intelligence within his own bloc.
- **Domestic:** On April 20, Bardella lunched with all 15 members of Medef's executive bureau plus representatives of federation sectors including construction and industry — a formal, first-for-RN institutional encounter. Bardella arrived with new economic adviser François Durvye and stated 'this is not an audition' — inverting the power dynamic. Bardella and Le Pen sent a joint letter to business leaders the same morning asking them to list regulatory obstacles, framing a future 'ordonnance de simplification' ready for publication if RN wins in 2027. Reuters reported that at Le Pen's April 7 Drouant dinner (with LVMH's Arnault among ~6 executives), the discussion 'turned tense' over her euroscepticism and pension reform reversal plans. Multiple executives remained 'unconvinced' by RN economic credibility. CFDT's Marylise Léon accused Medef of 'cynicism.' However, Medef president Patrick Martin confirmed RN can no longer be ignored: 'We will have to talk to representatives of the National Rally.'
- **Economic:** Marianne published a critical assessment of Lecornu's electrification and Made-in-France program, framing it as a 'grande illusion' — opinion, not reportage, but indicative of elite media skepticism about implementation viability. Simultaneously, France's G7 Presidency programming included the Franco-Japanese Green Finance Forum (co-organized by Banque de France and French Embassy Tokyo, April 9) and a critical minerals G7 session at the OECD in Istanbul (April 29). These confirm routine institutional statecraft activity but do not represent new structural commitments.
- **Institutional:** G7 Presidency programming active: environment ministerial (April 23), women and resilience ministerial (April 23), B7 business leadership conference hosted by MEDEF (April 22), U7+ university summit (April 14), AFD Paris Dialogue (April 28), Finance in Common G7 event (April 29). The Paris Hormuz conference, while analytically categorized under alignment_diplomatic and security_defense, created a new multilateral framework — joint declaration by France, Germany, UK, Italy — that represents institutional architecture outside existing NATO/EU structures.

- **Unexpected:** Trump publicly accuses Iran of targeting a French vessel in the Strait of Hormuz. If confirmed, a direct attack on a French vessel would transform the Hormuz crisis from an economic/navigational issue into a direct military incident involving France, with immediate implications for the Hormuz initiative's framing as 'non-belligerent' and for Franco-Iranian relations. Trump's accusation aligns with his pressure campaign to force allies into the US-Iran conflict framing. The Élysée has not officially confirmed the incident from available reporting; this requires monitoring. The claim appearing first through Trump's public statements rather than French official channels introduces uncertainty about its accuracy.

- **Notable absence:** New Caledonia autonomy consultation process — referenced as ongoing in prior two weekly entries — Quiet week for New Caledonia; no coverage visible in story map. Middle East crisis appears to have displaced attention. Not analytically significant at one-week absence.
- **Notable absence:** Follow-through on Lecornu's stated electrification program commitments (10B euro commitment announced prior week) — implementation details or legislative progress — No implementation progress visible; Marianne critique and legislative defeats suggest the program is facing political and practical obstacles. Absence of positive implementation news while critical coverage appears is a mild negative signal for the assessment's sustainability.

Italy under PM Meloni has crossed a structural threshold in its transatlantic alignment posture this week. The combination of the Sigonella refusal for US Iran war bombing missions, the papal defense against Trump, the Israel defense MoU suspension, and the E4 Hormuz alignment represents the most significant Italian diplomatic repositioning since the 2022 Ukraine war — and the first time Italy has materially constrained US operational access to its territory since the alliance's founding. The transatlantic 'bridge' strategy that anchored Meloni's international positioning has effectively collapsed under the weight of three compounding drivers: Italy's 2027 electoral calendar (80% Italian unfavorable view of Trump), direct threats to Italian peacekeepers (UNIFIL attacks by both Israel and Hezbollah), and domestic energy price shocks from the Iran war (diesel above €2/liter). Italy is simultaneously deepening European alignment (E4 format, Macron bilateral warming, parliamentary-conditioned Hormuz commitment) and reviewing its most exposed security commitments (UNIFIL viability questioned by Crosetto). Domestically, the Trump rupture has paradoxically strengthened Meloni's cross-partisan position while the Salvini Patriots rally — featuring pro-Russian gas and anti-European-army messaging alongside a Forza Italia counter-demonstration — reveals that coalition fracture has deepened from managed policy disagreement to open public competition between coalition partners. Italy remains institutionally stable and structurally committed to NATO and EU architecture, but the week marks a genuine directional shift: for the first time since 2022, Italy's active choices are being made on European rather than transatlantic terms.

Spain has entered its most diplomatically activist phase in months while simultaneously experiencing peak domestic political pressure across immigration, coalition dynamics, and electoral competition. The Barcelona IV 'En Defensa de la Democracia' summit has repositioned Spain from passive anti-Trump signaling to active global coalition-building, with Sánchez convening progressive heads of state from five continents and resolving the seven-year Spain-Mexico colonial-history freeze — all conducted alongside formal China bilateral engagement at premier level within the same week. This dual-track posture (progressive multilateralism + China engagement) represents the operational expression of Spain's strategic autonomy reflex under US retrenchment. Domestically, the governing coalition executed its most consequential unilateral action in recent memory — the mass regularization of up to 500,000 undocumented migrants via Royal Decree-Law — which simultaneously serves as demographic policy, electoral contrast ahead of Andalusia (May 17), and a direct challenge to PP/Vox immigration restrictionism. The PP-Vox Extremadura coalition formalization institutionalizes Spain's right-wing structural dependency on Vox for regional governance and provides an explicit template for national-level succession. Andalusia polls showing PP near absolute majority and PSOE at historic lows define the near-term political trajectory. Economic fundamentals remain strong with Spain's Economy Minister actively shaping Euro Area institutional design at IMF/World Bank fora. Security and defense posture remains one of selective alliance participation: active NATO command engagement and Ukraine contact group participation alongside the March 2026 base-denial posture's continuation. The overarching dynamic is a minority government under maximum domestic pressure deploying its remaining instruments — decree-law authority, international progressive platforms, bilateral diplomatic normalization — while parliamentary arithmetic and approaching regional elections constrain its options. The May 17 Andalusia outcome will be the first major empirical test of whether the government's dual domestic-international strategy is yielding political dividends or accelerating its terminal trajectory.
