## Regional Summary

**Built Abroad, Broken at Home**

Volodymyr Zelensky's accusation that Russia had "played the president of the United States" — posted in English on X for a global audience — crossed a line Ukraine had held since the current American administration took office. It was not diplomatic clumsiness; it was a bet that Europe is now reliable enough to say out loud what every NATO ally has been thinking. This week's events across Central-Eastern Europe suggest the bet is well-founded in some capitals and dangerously exposed in others — and that the difference turns less on defence budgets than on whether governments are fighting for their populations or against their own institutions.

The European military commitment is real. At the Ukraine Defence Contact Group meeting in Berlin, the UK pledged 120,000 drones worth £752m; Germany committed a €4bn package including Patriot missiles; Belgium, Spain, and Norway contributed roughly €1.6bn more. The \$5.5bn total was entirely European, entirely without Washington. What had been gap-filling is becoming architecture: Mr Zelensky used the forum to push allies toward a European-wide ballistic missile defence system, framing it as a collective need rather than a Ukrainian request, and signed defence agreements in Berlin, Oslo, Rome, and The Hague in four days. Russia's response was to publish the names and addresses of European companies making weapons for Ukraine. Dmitry Medvedev called the list "potential targets for Russian armed forces" — the first time Russia had explicitly threatened European civilian industrial infrastructure by name. The same week brought the year's deadliest aerial assault: 659 drones and 44 missiles over 24 hours, killing at least 18 people, including a child in Odesa, where a drone and then a ballistic missile struck the same residential building in succession.

The week's sharpest domestic contest played out in Warsaw, where Donald Tusk, the prime minister, stood at the Sejm podium and read from classified intelligence — a move with no clear precedent in post-1989 Polish politics. The intelligence, gathered by the Internal Security Agency, showed that a cryptocurrency exchange linked to Russian organised crime had funded Karol Nawrocki and opposition politicians, and that Mr Nawrocki had seen it before he twice vetoed legislation that would have regulated the platform. The Sejm still fell short of the three-fifths majority needed to override the veto; Jarosław Kaczyński and Mariusz Błaszczak abstained rather than publicly defend it, delivering the result Mr Nawrocki needed without casting a vote in his favour. Russia does not need visible operatives to shape Central-Eastern European politics; it needs only politicians with reasons to obstruct. Hungary's election told the opposite story. Péter Magyar's Respect and Freedom Party won 141 of 199 parliamentary seats on a record turnout of 79.56% — the largest supermajority in democratic Hungary's history. The EU Commission sent its most senior officials to Budapest to meet Mr Magyar's team before he had taken power, bypassing Viktor Orbán, the outgoing prime minister, entirely. Mr Magyar reversed the outgoing government's veto of the €90bn EU Ukraine loan at once. Hungary is rejoining the architecture that Poland helped build; the question is whether Poland's own institutions will hold long enough to notice.

The Czech Republic shows what deliberate institutional erosion looks like before it becomes irreversible. In a single week, Andrej Babiš's government excluded the president from the NATO summit delegation in Ankara, refused to provide a military aircraft for the Senate Speaker's Taiwan visit in an explicit tilt toward Beijing, and introduced legislation to replace Czech Television's licence fee with a state budget line — subordinating its editorial independence to annual political decisions without requiring a direct order. Each move alone might look like a skirmish; together they describe a government no longer testing limits one dispute at a time. Structural checks remain: the Senate holds an opposition majority of roughly 59 of 81 seats, and the Constitutional Court carries a decade's worth of the previous president's appointments. But those checks move more slowly than Mr Babiš is, and their first real test — a meeting between him and the president over the Ankara delegation — comes next week. Romania's coalition crisis unfolded in the same week that Russian military hackers worked through Air Force email accounts at NATO airbases; the Social Democratic Party called a 5,000-member vote on whether to bring down the prime minister, its leader touring regional meetings and comparing the coalition's stability to North Korea's.

Mr Zelensky's statement made the week's underlying claim explicit: Europe's centre of gravity has shifted, and Europe can hold it. On military spending, that may be true. On the political conditions needed to sustain them, it is less clearly so. A European security architecture capable of bearing that load requires that the states inside it do not hollow themselves out through constitutional erosion, Russian-linked political funding, and manufactured coalition crises. Mr Magyar's Hungary is moving in the right direction at striking speed. Poland's governing coalition is fighting the same battle more slowly, against entrenched opposition. The Czech Republic and Romania are the week's warnings: the gap between what European leaders pledge in Berlin and what their governments do at home is precisely the space that Russia is working to widen.

### <img src="https://flagcdn.com/ua.svg" alt="Ukraine flag" style={{ width: "24px", height: "18px", display: "inline", verticalAlign: "middle", marginRight: "8px" }} /> Ukraine

Ukraine this week crossed a visible threshold in its diplomatic reorientation: Zelensky publicly described Russia as having 'played' the Trump administration, called for a European security architecture explicitly designed around US absence, and secured over \$5 billion in European military commitments at Ramstein while US envoys visited Moscow without visiting Kyiv. The European security partnership is now operating as a functional replacement for US grant-based military support rather than a supplement to it — the structural claim in §14 about the EU assuming a security patron role it was never designed to perform is now operational, not prospective. In the security domain, Ukraine's drone campaign has escalated to strategic-level economic effects: documented strikes on refineries, Baltic Sea ports, and Caspian platforms have reduced Russian daily oil export capacity (contested figure: ~880,000 barrels) and prompted Russia to explicitly threaten European defense companies by name — the first extension of Russian deterrence threats beyond Ukraine's borders in this conflict. Russia simultaneously launched its deadliest aerial barrage of the year (18 killed). Domestically, the Kyiv supermarket shooting (6 killed, SBU terrorism investigation probing Russian links, patrol police chief resignation) introduced an acute internal security dimension absent from prior weeks, while civic petitions signal accumulated wartime governance grievances: mobilization without endpoint, language enforcement pressure, and IDP land loss. The overall posture has shifted from managed engagement with multiple partners toward an accelerating European pivot, more openly adversarial US relationship, and deepening strategic-level conflict escalation on both sides.

**Key developments:**

- **Diplomatic:** The 34th Ramstein Ukraine Defence Contact Group meeting (Berlin, April 15), co-chaired by the UK and Germany with Defence Minister Fedorov, yielded \$4 billion in air defense commitments and \$1.5 billion in drone funding. The UK announced its largest-ever drone package (120,000 drones, £752 million). Germany committed a €4 billion defense package including Patriot missiles. Belgium and Spain pledged €1 billion combined. Norway and Ukraine signed a drone cooperation declaration (Norway also committing €560 million for frontline drone support). Zelensky also pushed allies to develop a European-wide ballistic missile defense system, framing it as a collective European security requirement rather than a Ukraine-specific need. Zelensky visited Berlin, Oslo, Rome, and The Hague across April 14-17, securing bilateral defense-industrial agreements in parallel with the multilateral Ramstein commitments.
- **Diplomatic:** Zelensky publicly condemned the Trump administration's fourth extension of a waiver allowing Russian oil to be sold despite Western sanctions, stating the measure 'sustains Russia's illusion the war can continue' and that Russia 'played the Americans again — played the president of the United States.' He identified 110+ Russian shadow fleet tankers carrying 12 million tonnes of oil whose sales would provide \$10 billion to Moscow's war budget. The US argued the extension was needed to prevent energy market disruption from the Iran war. Separately, razomua.media reported US envoys Witkoff and Kushner had visited Moscow three times without any visit to Kyiv, with Zelensky indicating readiness to receive them 'even tomorrow.' Zelensky also argued that if the US withdraws from NATO, European democracies need a new security architecture encompassing Norway, the UK, Turkey, and Ukraine — explicitly framing a European security order without the US.
- **Security:** Ukrainian drone forces struck Samara-region refineries (Novokuibyshevsk and Syzran), a Crimea oil depot (22 drones against Sevastopol, confirmed oil tank fire), the Vysotsk Baltic Sea port's Lukoil terminal, and Caspian drilling platforms. Drone forces commander Brovdi stated that strikes on Primorsk, Ust-Luga, Sheskharis, and Tuapse had reduced total daily Russian oil shipments by approximately 880,000 barrels. Zelensky stated strikes inflicted \$2.3 billion in lost Russian oil revenue in March; Syrskyi reported 76 Russian industrial targets struck in March. Russia's Defence Ministry responded by threatening 'unpredictable consequences' and published addresses of European companies involved in joint weapons production with Ukraine — Medvedev characterized the list as 'potential targets for Russian armed forces.' Germany, Norway, Netherlands, and Belgium all announced new drone investment agreements with Ukraine this week. The SBU separately struck two Russian landing ships and a warship in Crimea.
- **Security:** Russia launched 659 drones and 44 ballistic and cruise missiles over 24 hours, killing at least 18 people (including a 12-year-old child) and wounding 118 across Kyiv, Odesa, Dnipro, and Zaporizhzhia. In Odesa, eight were killed when a drone strike on one residential building was followed by a ballistic missile strike on the same location. In Kyiv, four were killed including the child found in building rubble. Foreign Minister Sybiha described the attacks as 'war crimes that must be stopped.' The attack followed the expiration of a 32-hour Easter ceasefire declared by Putin the prior week. Zelensky, speaking in Rome during the attack, described Russia as 'betting on war' and stated the attack proved Russia did not deserve any easing of global policy or sanctions.
- **Domestic:** A 58-year-old man (identified by prosecutors as Dmytro Vasylchenkov, a Ukrainian citizen born in Moscow) opened fire on pedestrians in Kyiv's Holosiivskyi district on April 18, killing four on the street and taking hostages in a Velmart supermarket. He killed one hostage during a 40-minute standoff before police fatally shot him. A woman wounded on the street died later in hospital, bringing the total to six dead; 14 were injured including a 12-year-old boy. The SBU opened a terrorism investigation. The Guardian reported: the attacker had Russian bank accounts until at least 2021, a Russian phone number, traveled to Russia in 2016, and posted anti-Ukrainian and antisemitic content online, denying Ukraine's right to exist. He had previously lived in Bakhmut, Donetsk region. Kremlin operatives have recruited 800+ Ukrainians in the past two years for infrastructure and draft office attacks. Interior Minister Klymenko stated police attempted negotiation; Prosecutor General Kravchenko classified the incident as a terrorist offense. On April 19, the head of Ukraine's patrol police resigned after two officers were criticized for allegedly fleeing the scene; Zelensky demanded a 'full review' of patrol police conduct.
- **Domestic:** A petition filed April 14 requesting Cabinet-initiated criminal liability legislation for 'Ukrainophobia' (as defined in Law No. 3005-IX, which currently lacks criminal enforcement) crossed the 25,000-signature threshold requiring a mandatory Cabinet formal response. Proposed penalties include imprisonment for public promotion of Ukrainophobic narratives via media or internet, with aggravating circumstances for officials or organized groups. Separately, a petition from self-mobilized 2022 volunteers states they are compelled to continue serving with no defined service term limit; they self-identify as having volunteered willingly but now face indefinite military obligation. A third petition requested a wartime moratorium on rural school closures, arguing children in some communities must travel 20-25+ km under active air alert conditions without guaranteed shelter access.
- **Economic:** The Trump administration extended the period during which Russian oil already loaded on vessels can be sold despite sanctions, citing energy supply disruption from the Iran war. Zelensky identified 110+ shadow fleet tankers carrying 12 million tonnes of oil, with anticipated proceeds of approximately \$10 billion for Moscow's war budget. Separately, a Cabinet of Ministers petition filed April 17 reached 25,000 signatures (mandatory Cabinet response threshold) requesting a state IDP land compensation mechanism — proposing compensation certificates and targeted bonds financed through international aid and future reparations, referencing the 'Ye-ZEMLYA' initiative with 25,000+ applications from IDP landowners already collected.
- **Institutional:** The Ramstein UDCG produced \$4B in air defense commitments and \$1.5B in drone funding, with Poland and the Netherlands also providing Patriot missiles. Norway and Ukraine signed a drone cooperation declaration — moving beyond financial contributions toward joint production and technology-sharing. Germany separately committed €300 million in Ukraine's long-range strike capability and investment in 5,000 mid-range attack drones. Zelensky used the forum to sound the alarm on Patriot missile shortages, describing the air defense situation as something that 'couldn't be any worse.' The meeting was co-chaired by the UK and Germany, reflecting the post-US Ramstein architecture.

- **Unexpected:** Russia threatens European defense companies by name, publishing addresses as 'potential targets'. Russia's Defence Ministry published a list of addresses of European companies involved in joint weapons production with Ukraine; Medvedev characterized these as 'potential targets for Russian armed forces.' This is an escalation beyond Ukraine's borders into direct threats against European civilian industrial infrastructure — a threshold crossing that directly implicates NATO Article 5 considerations and significantly raises the stakes of European defense-industrial cooperation with Ukraine. This development was not anticipated in the prior assessment and may represent a new escalatory dynamic requiring monitoring.

- **Notable absence:** US-Ukraine diplomatic contact: Given prior week's assessment that 'peace negotiation channels show signs of renewed activity,' a meeting between US envoys and Ukrainian officials was structurally anticipated. — Razomua.media reported US envoys Witkoff and Kushner visited Moscow three times without any visit to Kyiv, with Zelensky signaling willingness to receive them 'even tomorrow.' The absence of US-Ukraine diplomatic contact while US-Russia contact continues represents a structural shift in the peace process architecture — the US channel is moving toward Moscow, not mediating between the parties. This directly contradicts the prior week's 'renewed activity' framing and triggers a self-correction.
- **Notable absence:** New developments on Hungary government policy toward EU-Ukraine accession following last week's significant election result. — No coverage of Hungarian government policy toward Ukraine this week. The 38-source Ramstein story dominated European security coverage without any Hungary EU accession angle. The structural opportunity identified last week remains valid but has not produced observable policy developments yet. This is not unexpected given the timing — policy changes from a new government typically take weeks to manifest.

Czech Republic's posture has undergone a qualitative intensification this week as three concurrent institutional erosion vectors activated simultaneously: public broadcasting licence fee abolition moved from coalition program to draft legislation (with international press coverage and strike committees forming at Czech Television); the government formally excluded President Pavel from the NATO Ankara summit delegation via official aircraft allocation resolution; and PM Babiš explicitly declared a 'pragmatic over values-based' foreign policy doctrine backed by the concrete action of denying government aircraft to the Senate Speaker for a Taiwan trip. NATO Secretary General Rutte's unannounced Prague visit — his public posture suggests it was prompted by Czech compliance concerns — confirms that the defense spending dispute has reached a threshold requiring personal intervention at the highest alliance level. Structural safeguards remain intact: the Senate's opposition majority (~59/81 seats), the Constitutional Court stocked by Pavel's appointments for approximately a decade, and the PR electoral system preventing supermajority capture continue to constrain full institutional capture. However, the simultaneity of this week's actions — across media independence, constitutional representation, and democratic solidarity norms — marks a transition from episodic friction to what appears to be coordinated advancement of the coalition's institutional reshaping agenda. The April 22–24 Pavel-Babiš meeting on the Ankara summit delegation and Vystrčil's forthcoming response to the Taiwan aircraft denial are the immediate near-term indicators.

- **Diplomatic:** Rutte's unannounced visit — which President Pavel publicly noted NATO chiefs typically make when there is a 'problem' — confirms the Czech defense spending dispute has reached a threshold requiring personal diplomatic intervention. Babiš pledged fulfillment but offered no binding numbers. The Czech government counts road projects at other ministries to claim ~2.07% GDP; the fiscal watchdog and President Pavel have both stated this accounting does not meet NATO criteria. The visit occurred days after the government quietly approved summit aircraft allocations excluding Pavel.

### <img src="https://flagcdn.com/hu.svg" alt="Hungary flag" style={{ width: "24px", height: "18px", display: "inline", verticalAlign: "middle", marginRight: "8px" }} /> Hungary

Hungary has completed the certification of its historic political transition — Tisza holds 141 of 199 parliamentary seats (53.18%), the most decisive supermajority in democratic Hungary's history — and the first week of the transition has simultaneously activated restoration dynamics and Fidesz-era entrenchment resistance. The alignment reorientation is proceeding faster than anticipated: an unprecedented EU Commission pre-government delegation (von der Leyen's chief of staff Seibert) has already opened formal cooperation talks, Poland is confirmed as the first foreign visit, and Russia has been explicitly repositioned from strategic partner to contractual counterpart. The critical economic constraint — the end-August 2026 deadline for €10.4bn in RRF funds — is now an active bilateral negotiation rather than a structural variable, and Magyar's four-step plan (EPPO accession, judicial independence, press freedom, academic freedom) provides the reform roadmap that Brussels is prepared to accept. On the domestic institutional front, the Fidesz-era apparatus is demonstrating active resistance: Sulyok signed Trócsányi to a rector term through 2032 in the transition week itself, and the Constitutional Court's mandated role in presidential removal confirms that institutional restoration will be contested and measured in years. Magyar's confrontational posture — demanding Sulyok's resignation the same day he was mandated to form a government, announcing state media suspension in live interviews — is consistent with a high-aggression opening strategy but carries sequencing risks if the CC is triggered before EPPO legislation advances. The May 9 inaugural parliament session sets the formal clock. The primary uncertainties for the coming weeks are: whether EPPO enabling legislation advances before the August RRF deadline; whether Sulyok resolves voluntarily or becomes the first constitutional removal attempt in democratic Hungary's history; and whether Magyar's cabinet composition signals genuine institutional depth or continued personal-vehicle governance.

**Key developments:**

- **Diplomatic:** Commission President von der Leyen's chief of staff Björn Seibert, accompanied by multiple director-generals, held two days of informal talks in Budapest with Tisza representatives including future FM Anita Orbán, finance minister-designate Kármán, and economy minister-designate Kapitány. Both sides confirmed agreement to work together; the Commission described the visit as an early opportunity for practical discussions on unlocking €17bn in frozen EU funds. Magyar announced a four-step plan centred on anti-corruption (including EPPO accession), judicial independence, press freedom, and academic freedom. Sources confirmed the meeting was explicitly driven by the end-August deadline for the €10.4bn RRF tranche. Significantly, the Commission declined to meet the outgoing Orbán government in the same period — itself a pointed diplomatic signal.
- **Diplomatic:** Magyar confirmed Warsaw would be his first foreign destination, explicitly citing Poland's experience repairing EU relations as the model Hungary would follow. He held a phone call with UK PM Keir Starmer, who thanked him for his support for Ukraine. On Russia, Magyar said he would answer Putin's calls but not initiate contact, and that Hungary must honour existing energy contracts — framing inherited Russian dependencies as constraints to manage rather than relationships to cultivate. Slovak PM Fico announced plans to personally confront Magyar over migration interference allegations, creating a near-term friction point in the Visegrád register.
- **Economic:** The Commission delegation's talks were explicitly framed around the end-August 2026 deadline for Hungary's €10.4bn RRF tranche, which Hungary risks losing entirely without an agreement. The Commission has blocked €17bn of the €27bn earmarked for Hungary. Magyar's four-step plan — anti-corruption measures (including EPPO accession), judicial independence, press freedom, academic freedom — goes beyond the Commission's existing requirements according to Tisza sources. Bruegel fellow Zsolt Darvas noted that all relevant legislative work 'can be done in a single day' with sufficient political will, involving judicial selection and powers reform. Magyar said he agreed with von der Leyen to start informal consultations before government formation. The talks also covered Hungary's block on the €90bn EU Ukraine loan; Magyar confirmed he would honour the December agreement Orbán had vetoed.
- **Institutional:** Magyar explicitly named EPPO accession as the first and most important anti-corruption measure in his four-step reform plan, stating that 'joining EPPO' was the central institutional commitment that would unlock EU funds. This was directly presented to the Commission delegation. Tisza sources said the party's programme 'goes beyond what the Commission has required.' Separately, the inaugural parliament session is targeted for May 9, with cabinet swearing-in by mid-May. Government source ground truth confirms preparatory logistics talks began April 17.
- **Institutional:** President Sulyok — with outgoing Cultural Minister Hankó Balázs's countersignature — signed a five-year rectorship extension for former Justice Minister Trócsányi László at Károli Gáspár Reformed University, effective February 2027 to January 2032. The new government could have been consulted, but the appointment was made before it could take office. Separately, Telex.hu's constitutional law analysis (citing constitutional lawyer Stánicz Péter) confirmed that removing Sulyok even with a two-thirds majority requires a Constitutional Court ruling — because the CC (itself Fidesz-packed) holds the final decision-making authority in presidential removal proceedings. Magyar can initiate the process with a two-thirds parliamentary vote, but the CC can block the final removal. One alternative flagged: constitutional amendment to expand CC membership to dilute existing justices.
- **Domestic:** Hungary's National Election Office certified the final result after counting postal, overseas mission, and transferred votes: Tisza 141 of 199 seats (53.18%), Fidesz-KDNP 52, Mi Hazánk 6. Turnout was a record 79.56%. Tisza gained 3 additional seats over the preliminary count by flipping Paks, Dombóvár, and Nyírbátor constituencies. Fidesz won only 10 of 106 single-member constituencies, compared to 87 in 2022. Magyar described the result as 'an unprecedented majority, an unprecedented mandate, and at the same time, responsibility.' Orbán's first post-defeat interview came Thursday on the Patrióta YouTube channel, where he described 'pain and emptiness,' took full personal responsibility, and said he would continue leading Fidesz subject to a party congress vote.
- **Domestic:** In and after their April 15 Sándor Palace meeting, Magyar told Sulyok he was 'unworthy' of embodying the unity of the Hungarian nation and 'unfit to serve as guardian of legality.' Sulyok said he was 'considering' the arguments; his former communications director told media he would not resign voluntarily. Magyar said if Sulyok does not resign voluntarily after the new government is formed, Tisza would use constitutional amendments to force his removal, along with other 'puppets' including Constitutional Court members. Constitutional lawyers confirmed the removal process requires the Constitutional Court itself — composed of Fidesz-era justices — as the final arbiter, creating a structural bottleneck. Sulyok's term runs to March 2029.
- **Domestic:** Magyar gave his first state media appearances in 18 months — on Kossuth Rádió and M1 TV — accusing presenters of spreading lies and comparing coverage to North Korean and Nazi-era propaganda. He announced his government's first actions would include suspending MTVA news broadcasts and passing a new media law and creating a new media authority. He explicitly modeled the approach on Poland's 2023 public broadcaster intervention. Magyar described the outgoing system as controlling 'around 400 media outlets' — a reference to the KESMA network. The state media authority pushed back, claiming it had extended 'multiple invitations' to Magyar during the campaign.
- **Security:** President Sulyok immediately dismissed Brigadier General Bodó Bernadett at the initiative of outgoing Interior Minister Pintér Sándor, according to the Hungarian Official Gazette. The dismissal was framed as 'mutual agreement' — eliminating the obligation to provide justification. Bodó had previously been moved from a national security counterterrorism post (TIBEK) to a police general role when TIBEK's jurisdiction was reorganised under Rogán Antal's supervision. The dismissal pattern fits the broader Fidesz last-minute personnel management observed across the transition week.

- **Unexpected:** Tisza flips Paks constituency in final count — symbolic win in Hungary's nuclear heartland. Paks is the host town of Hungary's existing nuclear power plant and the planned Paks II Rosatom expansion. Its constituency flip to Tisza was not in the preliminary count and was confirmed only in the final tally. While a single constituency result is minor electorally, the symbolic dimension is significant: the town most associated with Hungary's structural Russian energy dependency is now represented by a party pledged to reduce that dependency. The pipeline should note whether the incoming Paks MP's positions on Paks II become a local pressure point on the government's nuclear policy deliberations.
- **Unexpected:** EU Commission explicitly declines to meet outgoing Orbán government during Budapest delegation visit. The decision by the Seibert delegation not to meet Orbán government officials — while meeting a party not yet in power — is a pointed institutional signal that goes beyond normal diplomatic protocol. It functionally delegitimizes the outgoing government's claim to continued relevance in EU-Hungary relations and signals that Brussels has made its institutional choice about the transition. This is more aggressive than expected behavior from the Commission, which typically maintains formal relations with sitting governments through the end of their mandates. It may complicate any Fidesz attempt to frame itself as a continuing EU interlocutor in opposition.

- **Notable absence:** Magyar to name first seven cabinet ministers ('the following Monday' after April 19, per Story 2) — Magyar stated he would announce his first seven ministers the Monday following the week's close. This falls just outside the analysis window (April 20). The absence within this week is not analytically significant but the announcement — when it comes — will be the first concrete data point on Tisza's institutional depth and the distribution of governmental authority. Watch Indicator 5 (Tisza party institutionalization) activates with the first cabinet list.
- **Notable absence:** Any government signal on Paks II construction status or Rosatom contract — Per §19 Watch Indicator 4, Paks II construction status is the single most consequential infrastructure decision signal. No statement was made this week — consistent with expectations given no government has yet formed. Absence is not analytically significant at this stage. The pipeline should expect a Paks II position signal within 60 days of government formation. Orbán's post-defeat interview mentioned the failure to finish Paks II as a campaign error, suggesting it remains politically salient for Fidesz.
- **Notable absence:** Any signal regarding SPO (Sovereignty Protection Office) dissolution or investigation suspension — The SPO was expected to be abolished under Magyar (per [STRUC-05] and §0 actor list). No announcement was made this week. Absence is not analytically significant given the government has not yet formed, but the pipeline should track whether SPO dissolution is announced in the first legislative package — its inclusion or exclusion would signal the depth of commitment to dismantling regime-specific instruments versus only the MTVA media outlet.

### <img src="https://flagcdn.com/ro.svg" alt="Romania flag" style={{ width: "24px", height: "18px", display: "inline", verticalAlign: "middle", marginRight: "8px" }} /> Romania

Romania's governing coalition stands three days from a self-imposed reckoning, and the party threatening to blow it up has fewer options than its public posture suggests.

The Social Democrats (PSD) have called an internal party consultation for April 20 — billed as a "Moment of Truth" — in which roughly 5,000 members including parliamentarians, county leaders, and local officials will vote on whether to pull the party from government. PSD says the coalition "in its current form cannot continue," and the party has threatened mass ministerial resignations if Ilie Bolojan, the prime minister, does not step down before the April 23 cabinet meeting. Mr Bolojan has publicly refused, citing constitutional legitimacy, and constitutional experts back him: even if PSD exits, he may continue as caretaker prime minister for up to two years without a parliamentary confidence vote. Nicușor Dan, the president, has blocked the only obvious alternative: he has flatly refused to appoint a government backed by the far-right Alliance for the Union of Romanians (AUR) with Călin Georgescu as prime minister. George Simion, AUR's leader, confirmed Georgescu as a prime-minister candidate this week by meeting with him — but Mr Dan's veto closes that path. PSD and AUR also lack the parliamentary votes to pass a censure motion without the Save Romania Union (USR) or the Democratic Alliance of Hungarians in Romania (UDMR), neither of which will cooperate. Cătălin Predoiu, the National Liberal Party (PNL) deputy prime minister and interior minister, kept to his normal schedule this week — attending official ceremonies on April 15 — signalling that PNL, USR, and UDMR ministers are not preparing to follow PSD out. The caretaker path is open. Whether PSD's consultation amounts to a genuine rupture or an elaborate bid to extract concessions without actually leaving is the question before April 20.

Russian military intelligence hackers — the Fancy Bear group, also known as APT28 — compromised at least 67 email accounts belonging to Romanian Air Force personnel, including at NATO-integrated airbases. Reuters investigated the breach, which is part of a broader intelligence collection campaign across NATO's southeastern flank. Romania's defence ministry said the breach had been contained, a claim that cannot be verified from open sources. The context matters: the Romanian Intelligence Service (SRI) has been without a permanent civilian director for more than two and a half years. A confirmed penetration of military email systems at NATO facilities during this gap is more than a security incident — it compounds an existing vulnerability. A competing reading holds that Fancy Bear's campaign spans multiple southeastern NATO members, suggesting routine signals collection against the alliance rather than an escalation targeting Romania specifically. The institutional context is no less troubling for that.

Fiscal pressures continue on a slower clock. The Ministry of Energy confirmed a €150m tranche for energy storage batteries under Romania's EU recovery fund programme, and Mr Dan received Dragoș Pîslaru, the minister of investments and European projects, at the presidential palace — suggesting the president is personally watching EU fund disbursement. Romania must hit major milestones by August 2026 or risk losing up to €10bn in recovery funds; disbursement stood at roughly 37.6% as of October 2025. Individual tranches help at the margins but leave the structural gap intact. A government that enters extended caretaker status before August 2026 would put that deadline under real pressure — which means the April 20 coalition vote carries fiscal stakes well beyond domestic politics.

Almost all Poland coverage this week came from a single institution — the Institute of National Remembrance (IPN). The governing College nominated Mateusz Szpytma for the presidency. Under the statute, the nomination now goes to Karol Nawrocki, the president, who makes the formal appointment. Mr Szpytma is a historian and former vice-president of the institute; Mr Nawrocki ran it from 2021 to 2025 and shaped it to advance national-conservative historical narratives. The institute has a budget of roughly €88 million a year and more than 2,000 staff; its president shapes how Poland officially narrates Volhynia, the Holocaust, the Cursed Soldiers and the Round Table — all politically live issues. The appointment is not yet made, but the channel is open.

The week's other IPN events were less consequential but not unrelated. On April 19, the institute marked the 83rd anniversary of the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising — a scheduled event, but one that fell immediately after the previous week's Confederation swastika incident, in which party figures staged antisemitic parliamentary theatre on Yom HaShoah and the American ambassador rebuked them publicly. The commemoration proceeded without disruption. The institute also attended a Visegrád Four (V4) conference on historical memory with Czech, Slovak and Hungarian counterparts. The V4 is dead as a security or political format, but cultural and historical cooperation survives. The conference may serve Mr Nawrocki's stated interest in reviving the V4: the institute's engagement with Hungarian counterparts on memory politics may be advancing his agenda rather than the government's.

The larger questions from last week remain dark. The April 28-30 no-confidence vote against the climate minister — the most immediate test of coalition cohesion, flagged last week as carrying real strain — falls just outside this cycle's date range, and no preparatory coverage reached this desk. The Confederation diplomatic fallout and the Constitutional Tribunal oath standoff likewise produced no new evidence. Those threads carry forward unresolved.
