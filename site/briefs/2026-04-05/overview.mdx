## Week of March 30, 2026

Five Western European countries rejected American demands to join military action against Iran this week, the most united European resistance to Washington since the 2003 Iraq War. Germany declared war against Iran illegal in a presidential statement, Britain adopted a defensive-only approach despite American pressure, Italy denied the United States base access for Iran operations, Spain launched 'No to War' campaigns, and Norway criticized NATO leadership — all within days of each other. The timing and methods suggest planning, not coincidence. Europe can still act together when it wants to resist Washington, even as its democracies face internal pressure and economic coordination breaks down.

The energy shock from the Middle East war showed how broken global coordination has become. Countries across three regions launched crisis responses to the same shock but coordinated with no one. Eastern Europe intervened in fuel markets — Estonia cut growth forecasts, the Czech Republic intervened directly in markets, Latvia provided emergency aviation loans. Asia-Pacific responded on a larger scale — Korea's 26.2 trillion won budget, Japan's strategic reserve mobilization, Australia's fuel crisis response, Indonesia keeping subsidies going. Every country faced the same shock. None coordinated with any other. The world's economic machinery has no working crisis response for energy price shocks, forcing each country to go it alone.

Far-right parties made breakthroughs across Europe's major democracies this week, the biggest populist advance since the 2010s migration crisis. France's National Rally maintains front-runner status for 2027 presidential elections, Germany's Alternative for Germany overtook the governing Union in national polls for the first time, and Sweden normalized the Sweden Democrats with guaranteed cabinet representation after 2026. These gains across different electoral systems and political cycles show that democracy itself is under pressure, not just having a bad election cycle. The same forces are hitting European democratic institutions, reshaping European governance as populist parties move from opposition to power across multiple major countries.

The pattern shows coordination now works selectively: Europe can coordinate resistance to American leadership but cannot coordinate responses to shared economic threats. Countries launch crisis responses when facing energy shocks but coordinate sophisticated responses when rejecting American military demands. The machinery of resistance works better than the machinery of cooperation, even as the democratic foundations that make either possible crack under pressure across the continent.
