## Week of March 30, 2026

Five Western European countries rejected American demands to join military action against Iran this week, the most united European resistance to Washington since the 2003 Iraq war. Germany declared war against Iran illegal in a presidential statement. Britain adopted a defensive-only posture despite American pressure. Italy denied base access for Iran operations, Spain launched "No to War" campaigns, and Norway criticised NATO leadership -- all within days of each other. The timing suggests planning, not coincidence. Europe can still act together when it wants to resist Washington, even as its democracies fracture internally.

Yet that unity does not extend to economics. The energy shock from the Middle East war hit countries across three regions, and each responded alone. Estonia cut growth forecasts. The Czech Republic intervened in fuel markets. Latvia provided emergency aviation loans. In Asia-Pacific the scale was larger -- South Korea's 26.2 trillion won budget, Japan's strategic reserve release, Australia's fuel crisis response, Indonesia's continued subsidies -- but the pattern was the same. Every country faced the same disruption. None talked to any other.

Far-right parties compounded the strain. France's National Rally holds front-runner status for the 2027 presidential elections. Germany's Alternative for Germany overtook the governing coalition in national polls for the first time. Sweden normalised the Sweden Democrats with guaranteed cabinet representation after 2026. These gains span different electoral systems and political calendars, which makes them harder to dismiss as one country's problem.

The result is a Europe that can coordinate refusal but not response. The machinery for resisting American leadership works. The machinery for managing a shared economic crisis does not. And the democratic institutions that underpin both are weakening across the continent.
