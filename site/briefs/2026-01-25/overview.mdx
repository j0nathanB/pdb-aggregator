## Week of January 19, 2026

The Trump administration's coercive diplomacy has set off the most serious test of Western alliances since the Iraq war. Three linked developments — allied resistance, democratic fragmentation, and a global squeeze between security spending and fiscal limits — suggest that the post-war order is bending in ways that will not easily snap back.

Start with Europe. Faced with threatened tariffs, European governments coordinated their response and forced a suspension. This matters less for the tariffs themselves than for what it shows: collective pushback against American pressure still works. The episode sets a precedent. Allied countries can constrain unilateral American action when they act together, and this template will be tested again. Whether the coordination holds under sustained pressure, and how Washington responds — with escalation or retreat — will shape transatlantic relations for years.

Yet the picture inside these democracies is less reassuring. Rather than uniting their publics, outside pressure is pulling them apart. Seven major democracies now show acute political instability, through coalition tensions, minority government, constitutional workarounds, or party-system breakdown. The old assumption that foreign threats rally citizens behind their leaders looks outdated. Democratic complexity, it turns out, makes Western countries more brittle under pressure, not more resilient. Fragmented politics at home also limits what leaders can do abroad, just when alliances need bold action most.

Meanwhile, across the Asia-Pacific, governments are pouring vast sums into economic self-reliance: Taiwan into semiconductors, Japan into rare earths, South Korea into artificial intelligence, Indonesia into infrastructure. The bills run to hundreds of billions. But Europe's frontline states offer a cautionary tale. Finland has entered EU deficit procedures partly because of the cost of its F-35 fighters — a sign that ambitious security spending and fiscal health are hard to sustain together. The Asian programmes may hit the same wall. Debt ratios, credit ratings, and the sheer competition for scarce resources will determine how far these sovereignty drives can go.

Together, these three trends point in one direction. The Western-led order still has institutional muscle, but it is straining under a combination of external coercion, internal division, and the rising cost of self-reliance. How well middle powers manage these pressures — balancing alliance loyalty, domestic politics, and fiscal limits — will define the next phase of global politics.
