## Week of March 16, 2026

The machinery built for Western coordination is breaking down, but not as expected. Instead of allies pulling away from the United States, they are pulling away from each other. Middle powers across every region are now keeping their options open rather than choosing sides, alliances are splitting along geographic lines, and even countries pursuing the same goals cannot cooperate. What began as tension between the West and its rivals has become tension within the West itself.

Every middle power with options is now keeping them open. Canada pursues European alternatives while managing US cooperation. Brazil pushes regional independence through the Community of Latin American and Caribbean States (CELAC). Chile is torn between pro-US alignment and domestic constraints. Mexico sticks to its balancing act. Western Europe asserts more independence, Asia-Pacific countries offer conditional partnership. This is not regional variation but global adaptation — middle powers have concluded that flexibility beats alignment, even under crisis pressure that was supposed to force binary choices.

The Iran crisis has turned this abstract pattern into concrete alliance splits. Western European allies all reject expanded Middle East commitments. Germany refuses naval support for Strait of Hormuz operations while demanding more independence from Washington. Norway needed the NATO secretary-general to step in and manage friction. Britain faces worsening relations with Trump over Iran. But Eastern European allies embrace the same requests — Estonia offers Hormuz assistance, Romania approves US airbase use for Iran operations, Latvia signals readiness for NATO Middle East operations. The alliance is splitting not between members and Washington but between members themselves, along geographic lines.

Even countries pursuing the same goals cannot coordinate. Germany wants more independence from the US and seeks Asia-Pacific alternatives to American dominance. But this push for independence is creating stress in European defence cooperation, forcing the French president to intervene to save the FCAS fighter programme. Both countries want less dependence on America and both reject US demands on Iran, yet their separate independence drives threaten European defence integration. Independence and integration point in similar directions but work by different rules that clash under pressure. The result is European fragmentation even as Atlantic dependence shrinks — internal tensions that make managing Western unity twice as hard.
