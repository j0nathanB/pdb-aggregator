## Week of February 16 – February 22, 2026

Alliance systems are breaking apart and rebuilding themselves as American dominance fades. The clearest sign is a puzzle: seventeen countries now face serious political trouble at home — governments falling, constitutions under attack, institutions at war with each other — yet their international partnerships work as well as ever. Chaos at home is not stopping countries from working together abroad, suggesting alliances now run on autopilot.

This change is clearest in how regions respond to American pressure. Europe is pushing back in unison — governments criticise Washington's policies and build up their militaries at the same time. The Americas take different approaches: Canada is looking for other partners while Mexico works more closely with the United States. In the Middle East, India, Turkey and the UAE are all playing both sides. Countries no longer arrange themselves around America like spokes on a wheel. Instead, they work through regional groups.

But not every region is adapting. Saudi Arabia and the UAE, partners since the 1980s, are now shooting at each other in Yemen and filing formal complaints. Their split has wrecked the Gulf's security system and opened the door for other powers to muscle in.

The pattern suggests alliances in a multipolar world survive through managed breakdown rather than total collapse. When governments fragment, international partnerships continue. When global frameworks fragment, regional ones emerge. But when regional partnerships collapse entirely — as in the Gulf — countries must pick new sides rather than simply adapt.
