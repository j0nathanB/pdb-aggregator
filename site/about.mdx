## From the Editor

On [January 20, 2026](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/01/davos-2026-special-address-by-mark-carney-prime-minister-of-canada/), Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney named a rupture in the rules-based international order. The old world order is dead, he said, but the middle powers have the capacity to build a new one grounded in human rights, sustainable development, solidarity, and the sovereignty and territorial integrity of states.

The Middle Powers Monitor emerges from that reality. Its goal is to track the old international order as it evolves into a new one. As this shift occurs in defence commitments, trade policy, and domestic politics, no single story or outlet captures it. While a reader who subscribes to Reuters, the BBC, Foreign Affairs and a handful of regional papers will find no shortage of good reporting on these topics, they will find that there are not enough hours in the week to read the volume needed to hold the full picture together across 28 countries. So it is built on the model of an intelligence brief: wide sourcing across dozens of outlets, tight selection, and enough context to make sense of it — so that twenty minutes on a Monday morning leaves a reader with a clearer picture of the world than a week of scattered reading would.

## Coverage

Each country earns its place not by GDP, G20 membership or geographic balance, though the list covers 14 of 20 G20 members and spans five regions. The selection turns on a single question: is this country doing the work of sustaining the liberal international order, or merely saying that it does?

The Monitor covers Europe most closely because that is where the question is being answered in practice, from the states that must replace American defence capacity to the frontline countries whose security is at stake. The Indo-Pacific is included because it is where the next test of deterrence is taking shape. The swing states — India, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Indonesia — matter because their hedging collectively determines whether the order contracts into a Western bloc or keeps its global reach. The Americas are covered thinly but deliberately: Canada as the sole committed North American defender, Brazil as the Global South's leading diplomatic voice, and Mexico and Chile as tests of whether the liberal international order has any hold in Latin America at all.

The United States, China, Russia, Iran and North Korea are excluded. So is Israel. Their gravitational pull warps middle-power analysis: hegemons set the field; disruptors break it; unique cases defy the framework. Countries whose trajectories are already settled (Argentina under Milei, for instance) are set aside in favour of those where the question of alignment remains open.

## Analytical Framework

The Monitor assesses each country weekly across five signal categories drawn from how international relations scholarship understands middle-power behavior: the ways states align, arm, trade, build institutions, and manage domestic politics.

- **Diplomatic alignment** covers how a country positions itself relative to other states and manages those relationships. It takes in bilateral and multilateral dealings, alliance commitments, and the means a state uses to play competing powers against each other.

- **Security and defence** looks at a country's military posture, what it is buying, where it is deploying forces, and how closely it cooperates with allies on intelligence and operations.

- **Economic statecraft** examines how a country uses trade policy, sanctions compliance, investment flows and energy strategy to advance its broader aims.

- **Institutional engagement** tracks how actively a country takes part in international organisations, whether it honours its treaty commitments, and what role it plays in setting norms.

- **Domestic constraints** captures the internal forces that shape a government's room to act, e.g., regime stability, public opinion, electoral pressures and the relationship between civilian leaders and the military.

### Design decisions

The Monitor uses LLMs to read and pull together large amounts of text. They are well suited to the task: no human reader can hold the week's output from dozens of outlets across 28 countries, but a language model can, and can do it consistently.

It publishes weekly. The daily news cycle is already well covered; a weekly rhythm is frequent enough to stay current but slow enough to find the pattern beneath the noise.

The layout borrows from technical documentation rather than news design. News layouts serve the stories of the day. Documentation layouts serve reference material, and a 28-country analytical brief is reference material.

## Methodology

The Monitor's analysis works in layers. Each country gets a full analysis. Regional synthesis draws out patterns that cut across borders, and a global summary identifies what the country and regional pictures, taken together, reveal. Country dossiers provide the structural context behind the weekly analysis: the institutional landscape, key actors, blind spots and historical patterns that shape how signals should be read.

The Monitor does not pretend to replace expert judgement. AI agents can read widely, but they cannot place a phone call, read a room or know what a source meant but did not say. Then again, human analysts have their own blind spots. The intelligence failures around September 11th, the certainty about Iraqi weapons that did not exist, the near-universal surprise at the Soviet collapse were not failures of reading but of reasoning, groupthink and institutional incentive. The Monitor will get things wrong too, but its errors are bounded: everything it produces is grounded in the week's reported facts, and nothing in it comes from a source the reader cannot check. Where the analysis falls short, we would rather be clearly wrong than vaguely right.

Carney was right that the old order is dead. The question the Monitor tracks each week is narrower and, for now, more useful: which countries are building what comes next, and which are only saying they will? The gap between those two answers is where the future of the liberal international order is being decided.

The Monitor publishes every Monday. It is free to read and will remain so. Corrections, objections and tips are welcome at editor@middlepowers.fyi.
