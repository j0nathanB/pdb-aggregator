## 8. ENVIRONMENTAL & CLIMATIC STRUCTURE

Pakistan's environmental conditions are not background factors — they are active, recurring stress tests of state capacity, regime legitimacy, and fiscal resilience. The country's exposure to climate-driven disasters is among the most acute of any major nation, and its institutional capacity to manage this exposure is among the weakest.

**Historical climate-governance interactions: the monsoon as annual referendum.** Pakistan's governance is cyclically tested by the South Asian monsoon, which delivers nearly three-quarters of the country's annual rainfall between June and September. 
This season's intense monsoon rains caused flooding that killed hundreds and displaced millions of people in Pakistan — an increasingly frequent occurrence.
 The political mechanism is direct: monsoon failure or excess translates into agricultural loss, food price inflation, displacement, and emergency fiscal demands that overwhelm existing budgets. 
According to the Pakistan Economic Survey 2024-25, major crops such as wheat and cotton contracted by 13.5 percent, restricting the overall GDP growth rate by 0.6 percent.
 Each flood event also generates a secondary public health crisis — diarrheal diseases, malaria, dengue, and malnutrition surge in the aftermath — that further taxes state capacity.

The 2022 monsoon floods represent the template for this dynamic at catastrophic scale. 
Unprecedented monsoon rains submerged nearly one-third of Pakistan, displacing more than 33 million people and destroying more than 2,000 health facilities.
 
Extensive flooding submerged approximately a third of the country, killed more than 1,700 people, caused $14.8 billion worth of damage, as well as $15.2 billion of economic losses, and pushed nine million people into poverty.
 The institutional response was revealing: 
the response was marked by delayed mobilization, fragmented governance, and weak coordination across federal, provincial, and humanitarian actors, with relief efforts hampered by inequitable allocation of resources and poor integration of community voices.


This is not a historical anomaly. 
Floods across Pakistan in 2011, 2012, 2020, 2023, and 2024 would have been major stories in most other countries, but seem like the new normal there.
 In August-September 2025, 
relentless monsoon rains and glacial floods devastated Pakistan, killing over 800 people, displacing tens of thousands, and destroying homes, roads, bridges, crops, and livestock.
 
A pattern is emerging of repeated flood damage that is permanently undermining Pakistan's ability to feed itself.


**Transboundary water dependencies: the Indus as existential geography.** Pakistan's water security is inseparable from its relationship with India. The Indus River system — the country's lifeline — originates in Indian-controlled territory. The Indus Waters Treaty of 1960, brokered by the World Bank, had survived four wars and six decades of hostility as one of the world's most durable transboundary water agreements. 
The temporary suspension of one of the world's most conflict-resilient water-sharing agreements is an extraordinary development; for over six decades, the IWT endured various diplomatic breakdowns, violent escalations, and full-scale wars between India and Pakistan without being seriously questioned by either party.


That changed on 23 April 2025. 
Following the Pahalgam terrorist attack, the Government of India suspended the treaty, citing national security concerns and alleging Pakistan's support of state-sponsored terrorism.
 
India has insisted the treaty will remain suspended until Pakistan stops supporting what it calls cross-border terrorism, with India's Home Minister Amit Shah vowing that India will "never" restore the Indus Waters Treaty.
 Pakistan's response has been to frame the suspension as a potential casus belli. 
Pakistan has reportedly warned that any attempt by India to disrupt the flow of water from shared rivers could be considered an act of war, and that it could attack India with nuclear weapons.


The immediate hydrological impact is debated. Some experts regard the suspension as largely symbolic in the short term — India lacks the current infrastructure to rapidly redirect western rivers. But the suspension's most potent immediate effect may be informational: 
India can halt the flow of information; the IWT requires the parties to share data on project development, river flows, and hydrological conditions, and by suspending the treaty, India can also cease data sharing, depriving Pakistan of flood warnings and potentially imperiling Pakistani lives and livelihoods.
 In the medium term, India has the upstream geography and fiscal capacity to build storage and diversion infrastructure that would materially reduce flows to Pakistan, constituting a slow-moving but potentially devastating form of coercion.

First, glacial melt and glacier lake outburst floods (GLOFs): 
Pakistan is home to 13,032 glaciers, and has the largest volume of glacial ice for any country outside polar regions.
 
A 2024 study indicated that the pace of climate change in the Hindu Kush-Himalaya-Karakoram region was faster than the global average.
 
Some 2 million Pakistanis are exposed to potential future glacial lake outbursts.

Second, intensification of monsoon rainfall: 
World Weather Attribution scientists found that the recent monsoon rains were intensified by up to 15% due to human-induced climate change.
 The thermodynamic mechanism is that a warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, which is released in increasingly intense cloudbursts when monsoon winds rise over the Himalayas.

Third, deforestation amplifying flood damage: 
From 2001 to 2024, Pakistan lost 95.3 square kilometers of forest cover.
 
Pakistan lost nearly 8 percent of its tree cover in this period.
 This is an institutionally mediated constraint — a capable state could reverse deforestation through forest management — but in practice, political pressure from real estate developers and timber interests has accelerated the problem.

Fourth, heat stress: Pakistan already experiences some of the world's most extreme heat events. 
A heatwave in 2024 killed almost 600 people.
 Extreme heat events reduce agricultural labor productivity, increase energy demand for cooling, and create public health emergencies — particularly in Sindh and Punjab during pre-monsoon summer months.

**Environmental policy as a foreign policy variable.** 
Pakistan ranks among the top 10 most climate-vulnerable nations, but it contributes less than 1 percent of global emissions.
 This asymmetry — bearing the costs of climate change while contributing negligibly to its causes — has become a central plank of Pakistan's international climate diplomacy. Pakistan positions itself as a climate justice advocate in international negotiations, framing vulnerability as grounds for compensation and adaptation financing from wealthy emitters. 
Following the 2022 floods, Pakistan hosted a global donor conference where approximately $10 billion was pledged, but by 2024 Pakistan had received only $2.8 billion of those pledges.
 
A former head of Pakistan's central bank said the country would need annual investments of $40–50 billion until 2050 to address its escalating climate challenges.

CPEC energy infrastructure creates a tension with this climate justice narrative: Pakistan's CPEC-financed coal plants lock in carbon emissions and capacity payment obligations, while Pakistan simultaneously demands developed-world financing for climate adaptation — a contradiction that Western interlocutors periodically exploit.

**Institutional capacity for environmental adaptation.** Pakistan's National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) coordinates disaster response, but its capacity is overwhelmed by the scale and frequency of climate events. 
Unless governance gaps are addressed, Pakistan will remain ill-prepared for future disasters of similar or greater scale.
 The Meteorological Department provides forecasts but early warning systems for GLOFs and flash floods remain rudimentary — lives in 2025 were saved by a shepherd's phone call, not by institutional warning systems. Agricultural adaptation programs exist on paper but face implementation constraints from fiscal austerity (IMF program requirements) and the feudal land tenure system that discourages investment by tenant farmers who do not own the land they cultivate.

## 11. INTERNATIONAL INSTITUTIONAL COMMITMENTS & LEGAL FRAMEWORKS

## 12. MILITARY & SECURITY SECTOR DNA

GHQ (General Headquarters) in Rawalpindi is the actual nerve center of Pakistani governance, not the Prime Minister's office in Islamabad. The Army's internal culture is characterized by: a strong esprit de corps rooted in the "martial races" recruitment tradition; deep institutional memory of the 1971 defeat (which drives the obsession with institutional cohesion and national integrity); and a belief in its own role as the guardian of Pakistan's ideological and territorial existence — a role that transcends and supersedes democratic politics.

The Navy's strategic significance is limited by Pakistan's short coastline and the fact that the primary threat axis is land-based. Inter-services organizations such as the ISI and the propaganda arm ISPR receive almost the same budget as the PAF.
 This extraordinary allocation — an intelligence agency receiving budget equivalent to an entire air force — reflects the ISI's actual institutional significance.

*Paramilitary forces:* The Pakistan Rangers (deployed in Sindh for urban security, particularly Karachi) and the Frontier Corps (deployed in Balochistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa for counter-insurgency) are formally under the Interior Ministry but operationally directed by the Army. Their combined strength is approximately 500,000 personnel.

**Military economic interests: the Milbus empire.** The military's economic holdings, described in Section 0 and Section 6, constitute a structural feature of the highest importance. The military operates as a commercial conglomerate through the Fauji Foundation ($5.9 billion in assets), Army Welfare Trust, Defence Housing Authority (Pakistan's largest land developer), Bahria Foundation, and Shaheen Foundation. These entities span fertilizers, banking, cement, petroleum, real estate, food processing, and power generation — often with preferential tax treatment and regulatory exemptions.

The economic holdings have profound institutional consequences. First, they give the military a revenue base independent of the civilian budget, reducing civilian leverage over military behavior. Second, they make the military a stakeholder in economic stability — not because of civic virtue, but because economic crises threaten the value of military-commercial assets. Third, they create structural resistance to economic reform: privatization of state enterprises, tax reform, energy subsidy removal, and land reform all threaten military economic interests. Fourth, they provide a vast patronage network that generates loyalty among retired officers — military retirees populate DHA management, Fauji Foundation boards, and AWT enterprises, creating an extended military class with ongoing institutional loyalty.

**Doctrinal orientation: full spectrum deterrence under stress.**


Pakistan operates under a nuclear doctrine that it calls "full spectrum deterrence," aimed mainly at deterring India, which Pakistan identifies as its primary adversary.
 The doctrine has four key features:

First, no first use is explicitly rejected. 
Lt. Gen. (Ret.) Khalid Kidwai stated: "Pakistan does not have a No First Use policy, and I'll repeat that for emphasis."
 This rejection is strategic — designed to compensate for India's conventional military superiority by keeping the nuclear threshold ambiguous.

Second, Pakistan has invested in tactical nuclear weapons (TNWs), specifically the Nasr (Hatf-9) short-range ballistic missile, 
designed to counter military threats below the strategic level
 — a direct response to India's perceived "Cold Start" doctrine for rapid conventional operations into Pakistani territory.

Third, 
Pakistan's nuclear arsenal was estimated to comprise approximately 170 warheads in 2024, with projections suggesting a potential increase to 200 by 2025.
 
Pakistan focused on the development of tactical or battlefield nuclear weapons as a means to offset India's conventional military superiority.


Fourth, the May 2025 conflict stress-tested this doctrine. 
The world watched anxiously as India and Pakistan exchanged ballistic missiles during what is now remembered as the 88-hour war of May 2025, with observers fearing uncontrolled escalation.
 
Pakistan's deterrence logic was undermined during the four-day conflict, when India ignored Islamabad's nuclear signaling and established what Prime Minister Modi described as a "new normal."
 This is potentially the most consequential development in Pakistan's security architecture in decades: if India demonstrates willingness to conduct conventional military operations despite Pakistani nuclear threats, the entire deterrence framework that has underpinned Pakistani strategic thinking since the 1990s requires fundamental reassessment.

**Defense spending: the post-conflict surge.** 
Pakistan has increased its defense spending by more than 20 percent — the most substantial hike in a decade — with an allocation of 2.55 trillion rupees ($9 billion), amounting to 1.97 percent of GDP, up from 1.7 percent in the previous budget.
 
Adding defense pensions would inflate the defense budget by 25-30 percent.
 The true total defense expenditure, including pensions, the Armed Forces Development Programme, and undisclosed nuclear and strategic programs, is significantly higher than the stated budget. 
Pakistan receives the bulk of its military hardware from China at bargain-basement prices; after Operation Sindoor, Pakistan is set to procure fifth-generation J-35 fighter aircraft from China.
 This procurement dependency reinforces the structural China lock-in identified in Section 6.

**The 27th Constitutional Amendment as institutional self-protection.** The 27th Constitutional Amendment (November 2025), creating the Chief of Defence Forces position and granting Asim Munir lifelong immunity, represents the most explicit constitutional codification of military supremacy in Pakistan's history. Previous military rulers (Ayub, Zia, Musharraf) governed through direct martial law; Munir has achieved equivalent authority through constitutional amendment, a "coup-less coup" that integrates military dominance into the formal legal architecture.

**Behavior under regime stress: historical patterns.** Pakistan's military has demonstrated a consistent behavioral pattern along the escalation spectrum outlined in the dossier framework:

(a) *Behind-the-scenes influence:* This is the default mode — the military shapes policy through GHQ-PM channel, ISI media management, and behind-the-scenes guidance on foreign and economic policy. This is the current operating mode.

(b) *Public institutional statements:* The ISPR issues public statements that signal institutional positions on political controversies, serving as a warning to civilian politicians. DG-ISPR press conferences on political matters are escalation indicators.

(c) *Selective non-compliance:* The military has historically refused to implement civilian orders it disagrees with — particularly regarding India policy, Afghan policy, and militant management — without formally defying civilian authority.

(d-e) *Institutional neutrality or defection:* The May 9, 2023 episode — in which PTI supporters attacked military installations following Imran Khan's arrest — is the most relevant recent precedent. The military's response was a massive crackdown that demonstrated zero tolerance for challenges to institutional authority. There is no recent precedent for military neutrality toward a civilian government; the military has always intervened to shape outcomes.

(f) *Direct seizure of power:* Pakistan has experienced four military coups (1958, 1969, 1977, 1999). The 27th Amendment has arguably made formal coups unnecessary — the military now exercises equivalent power through constitutional mechanisms. A formal coup would be needed only if the constitutional architecture were threatened (e.g., if a future political movement sought to repeal the 27th Amendment or if the military's economic interests faced existential challenge).

The military's institutional redlines include: threats to its commercial empire (Milbus); attempts to subordinate military appointments to genuine civilian control; exposure of officer corps corruption or human rights violations in Balochistan; and any resolution of the Kashmir dispute that the military has not endorsed.

**STRUCTURAL CLAIMS:**
1. The Pakistan Army is simultaneously the country's dominant political institution, its largest business conglomerate, and the guarantor of its nuclear arsenal; this triple role means that any challenge to the military in one domain (economic reform, political accountability, arms control) is perceived as a threat to all three, triggering comprehensive institutional resistance. **[HIGH CONFIDENCE]**
2. The 27th Constitutional Amendment's creation of the CDF position and granting of lifelong immunity represents a qualitative shift from informal military dominance to constitutional codification; this raises the cost of any future democratic transition, as reversal would require constitutional amendment — a higher bar than merely resisting behind-the-scenes influence.
3. The May 2025 conflict's demonstration that India can conduct conventional military operations despite Pakistani nuclear signaling constitutes the most significant challenge to Pakistan's "full spectrum deterrence" doctrine since its formulation; any post-conflict doctrinal review, procurement shifts (particularly regarding air defense and TNW delivery systems), or nuclear posture adjustments should be interpreted as responses to this doctrinal crisis.
4. Pakistan's defense hardware dependency on China (JF-17, prospective J-35, air defense systems, naval platforms) is now structural and accelerating post-2025 conflict; this makes China the effective guarantor of Pakistan's conventional military capability, providing Beijing with leverage that extends beyond the economic domain into the security architecture.
5. The ISI's budget allocation equivalent to an entire service branch (the PAF) quantifies its institutional significance; the ISI is not an intelligence agency in the Western sense but a parallel governance institution with operational authority over domestic politics, media, militant management, and foreign covert operations.
6. A formal military coup is currently structurally unnecessary and therefore unlikely — the 27th Amendment has achieved the military's institutional objectives through legal means. The scenario that would trigger consideration of formal military intervention would be a political movement capable of amending the constitution to roll back military prerogatives — a development that would require unprecedented political mobilization and inter-party coordination.
