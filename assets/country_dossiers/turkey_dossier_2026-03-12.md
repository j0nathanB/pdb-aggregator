# Turkey: structural country dossier

**Turkey occupies the single most consequential geopolitical position of any middle power in the current international system.** Straddling Europe and Asia, controlling the only naval chokepoint between the Mediterranean and Black Seas, and bordering the arc of instability from Syria to Iran, Turkey under Recep Tayyip Erdoğan has exploited the vacuum left by US retrenchment to pursue strategic autonomy — simultaneously deepening NATO obligations, purchasing Russian air defense systems, building a globally competitive drone industry, and positioning itself as the indispensable broker in conflicts from Ukraine to Syria. With **86 million people**, NATO's second-largest military, and a $1.4 trillion economy, Turkey is too large to ignore, too strategically located to abandon, and too erratic to fully trust. The country's internal contradictions — an increasingly authoritarian presidential system layered atop democratic institutions, a historic Kurdish peace process unfolding alongside the imprisonment of the leading opposition presidential candidate, and an orthodox economic stabilization program at the mercy of a single leader's patience — make it simultaneously a stabilizer and destabilizer of the regional order.

## Section 0: Key actors and institutions primer

### The hyper-presidential system Erdoğan built

Turkey's **2017 constitutional referendum** (approved 51–49%) replaced the parliamentary system with an executive presidency effective 2018. The president now serves as both head of state and government, can issue executive decrees, appoints and dismisses all ministers without parliamentary confirmation, directly appoints **12 of 15 Constitutional Court judges** and **6 of 13** members of the Council of Judges and Prosecutors (HSK), and directly commands the military chain. The prime minister position was abolished entirely. This architecture concentrates power to a degree unmatched in any NATO member state.

The current cabinet, assembled after Erdoğan's May 2023 re-election (52.18% in the runoff against Kemal Kılıçdaroğlu), reflects the dual imperatives of economic stabilization and security consolidation. **Mehmet Şimşek** (Treasury and Finance) signals market orthodoxy; **Hakan Fidan** (Foreign Affairs), the former MIT intelligence chief, signals that intelligence-led diplomacy drives foreign policy; **Yaşar Güler** (Defense), a former Chief of General Staff, signals military subordination to civilian control. The Communications Directorate under **Fahrettin Altun** functions as a de facto propaganda ministry, controlling media access and messaging.

### Parliament, judiciary, and the erosion of institutional checks

The Grand National Assembly (TBMM) retains 600 seats. After the May 2023 elections, the People's Alliance (AKP 268 + MHP 50 + smaller allies) holds approximately **323 seats** — sufficient for ordinary legislation but short of the 360 needed for a constitutional referendum, a gap that drives much of Turkey's current political maneuvering. The opposition comprises CHP (169), DEM Party (61), İYİ Party (43), New Welfare (5), and TİP (4). Speaker **Numan Kurtulmuş** (AKP) presides.

The judiciary has been **regime-captured** through the HSK, whose 13 members are now effectively controlled by the executive. Over 4,000 judges and prosecutors were purged after the 2016 coup attempt. The Constitutional Court, under new president **Kadir Özkaya** (since 2024), has shown sporadic independence — most dramatically in the Can Atalay case, where the Court of Cassation refused to comply with the Constitutional Court's release order, filing criminal complaints against nine justices in an unprecedented constitutional crisis. Atalay, a TİP MP sentenced to 18 years over the Gezi trial, remained imprisoned throughout 2024–2025. The HDP/DEM closure case, filed in June 2021, remains pending before the court as of early 2026, its progress seemingly calibrated to the Kurdish peace process.

### The political party landscape after AKP's 2024 reckoning

The **March 2024 municipal elections** delivered AKP's worst result since its founding: **35.5% nationally** versus CHP's **37.8%** — the first time AKP lost the popular vote in any nationwide contest. CHP won 35 of 81 provinces, including all five of Turkey's largest cities. **Ekrem İmamoğlu** won Istanbul with 51.1%, and **Mansur Yavaş** took Ankara with a devastating 60.4%. AKP retained only 24 provincial municipalities, down from 39.

The CHP under **Özgür Özel** (who replaced Kılıçdaroğlu in November 2023) has transformed from a rigid Kemalist party into a broader catch-all opposition. İmamoğlu announced his presidential candidacy in February 2025, was **arrested on March 19, 2025** on corruption and terrorism charges, and faces a potential sentence of **828–2,352 years**. His arrest triggered Turkey's largest protests since Gezi (2013). Despite imprisonment, **15.5 million people** voted to confirm him as CHP's candidate. His trial began on March 9, 2026.

**Devlet Bahçeli's MHP** holds disproportionate influence with only ~50 seats because it supplies the votes that give AKP its governing majority. Bahçeli, 77, made the most dramatic pivot in Turkish political history in October 2024 by calling on imprisoned PKK leader **Abdullah Öcalan** to address parliament and dissolve the PKK — a reversal widely interpreted as strategically linked to constitutional amendment ambitions.

The **New Welfare Party** under Fatih Erbakan emerged as a spoiler in 2024 (6.2% nationally), siphoning conservative voters from AKP. The DEM Party navigates between the peace process with the ruling alliance and solidarity with the CHP opposition, aware that its 56 votes may be needed for any constitutional change.

### Security apparatus: post-coup architecture

The 2016 failed coup triggered the most comprehensive restructuring of Turkey's security state since the republic's founding. The Turkish Armed Forces (**~355,000 active personnel**, NATO's second-largest) were subordinated to the Defense Ministry; the Chief of General Staff now reports through the ministry to the president. Military high schools were abolished, officer candidates now come from civilian schools including İmam Hatip religious schools, and the Gendarmerie and Coast Guard were transferred to the Interior Ministry. Current Chief of General Staff is **Gen. Selçuk Bayraktaroğlu** (appointed August 2025).

The National Intelligence Organization (MİT), now led by **İbrahim Kalın** (former presidential spokesperson, PhD from George Washington University), has expanded from a primarily domestic agency to an intelligence-diplomacy powerhouse operating across Syria, Libya, Ukraine, and beyond. The Defense Industry Presidency (SSB), led by **Haluk Görgün** (former ASELSAN CEO), oversees an ecosystem of **3,500+ companies** and **~100,000 employees** generating **$8.5 billion in defense exports** in 2025 — up from $248 million in 2002. The defense sector's localization rate has risen from 20% to **80%**.

### Economic gatekeepers and the patronage economy

Turkey's economic power structure operates on three tiers. The traditional secular big capital — **Koç Group** (Turkey's largest conglomerate: Ford Otosan, Tüpraş, Yapı Kredi) and **Sabancı Holding** (Akbank, cement, energy) — remains institutionally stable but politically marginalized, represented by TÜSİAD. The AKP-aligned conservative business class, represented by MÜSİAD, provides the party's economic base. And the **"Gang of Five"** (Cengiz, Limak, Kalyon, Kolin, MNG) — construction conglomerates awarded contracts worth reportedly **$203 billion** over AKP's tenure — represent pure regime-dependent crony capital.

The Central Bank (TCMB), under **Fatih Karahan** (appointed February 2024, PhD from UPenn, former Fed NY economist), has maintained orthodox monetary policy since mid-2023 after five governors were fired or forced out in five years. The Turkey Wealth Fund (TVF), with **$360 billion in assets** under management and chaired by Erdoğan himself, controls stakes in Turkish Airlines, Borsa Istanbul, three state banks, Türk Telekom, and Turkcell — functioning as a political control vehicle exempt from Court of Accounts oversight.

The **Diyanet** (Directorate of Religious Affairs) has been transformed from Atatürk's instrument for controlling religion into a massive apparatus for projecting political Islam. Under President **Ali Erbaş**, Diyanet commands a 2025 budget of **₺130 billion (~$3.3 billion)** — exceeding the Interior, Foreign Affairs, and Energy ministries — employs **~143,000 staff**, oversees **89,000+ mosques**, and operates in 145 countries. Its exclusively Sunni Hanafi orientation structurally marginalizes Alevis (15–25% of the population).

Turkey's media landscape is **~90% government-aligned** per Reporters Without Borders. The 2018 Demirören Group acquisition of Doğan Media (Hürriyet, CNN Türk) eliminated the last major independent media conglomerate. RSF ranks Turkey **159th of 180** in press freedom. Independent outlets (Halk TV, Medyascope, BirGün) face constant RTÜK fines and broadcasting bans. In September 2025, Can Holding's media assets (Habertürk, Show TV) were seized by authorities.

### Stability classification

The fundamental analytical question is which institutions would survive a change of government. The TBMM, CHP, Koç/Sabancı groups, NATO basing infrastructure, and the formal state bureaucracy are **institutionally stable**. The hyper-presidential system, HSK's composition, MİT's expanded mandate, SSB's leadership, the TVF, Diyanet's current scope, the Gang of Five's commercial position, and Gulf investment flows are all **regime-specific** — meaning they are tied to the Erdoğan/AKP political project and would face restructuring or collapse under a different government. The Constitutional Court, TSK (restructured but durable), and the Central Bank occupy contested middle ground — formally independent institutions with regime-compromised governance.

## Section 1: Formation trauma and founding mythology

### Sèvres as the wound that never heals

The foundational trauma of the Turkish state is not a battle or a defeat but a **document**: the Treaty of Sèvres, signed on August 10, 1920. Its provisions would have reduced Anatolia to a rump state — an independent Armenian state carved from eastern provinces, a Kurdish autonomy zone with a path to independence, Greek control of Smyrna and eastern Thrace, Italian and French zones of influence in southern Anatolia, internationalized Straits, financial controls imposed by Allied powers, and an Ottoman army capped at 50,700 men. The treaty was never ratified, but the **map** — showing Turkey dismembered — became permanently seared into national consciousness.

**"Sèvres syndrome" (Sevr sendromu)** operates as what political psychologist Vamık Volkan calls a "chosen trauma": a collective historical memory so powerful that it functions as a core element of national identity across the political spectrum. It manifests as a persistent belief that external powers — especially the West — are perpetually conspiring to partition Turkey, and that internal minorities (particularly Kurds) serve as instruments of this conspiracy. Erdoğan invoked it in 2019 when framing Mediterranean energy policy: "Thanks to this military and energy cooperation, we overturned the Treaty of Sèvres." Bahçeli invoked it in 2015 to oppose the Kurdish peace process, claiming it "will lead to the collapse of the Turkish Republic."

The **Treaty of Lausanne** (July 24, 1923), negotiated by İsmet İnönü against Lord Curzon over eight months, is the corrective triumph narrative. It secured full sovereignty, recovered eastern Thrace, abolished capitulations, imposed no military restrictions, and — critically — **erased** the provisions for Armenian statehood and Kurdish autonomy that Sèvres had promised. Atatürk called it "a diplomatic victory unheard of in Ottoman history." The republic was proclaimed on October 29, 1923.

But Lausanne contains its own suppressed traumas. Minority protections (Articles 37–45) were restricted to **non-Muslim minorities** — Greeks, Armenians, and Jews — using Ottoman millet categories that legally classified Kurds as part of the Muslim majority, erasing their distinct identity from the state's founding document. The compulsory population exchange of approximately **1.6 million people** (1.2 million Greek Orthodox from Turkey, 400,000 Muslims from Greece) was organized by religion, not ethnicity. Turkey's Christian population collapsed from 20–25% in 1914 to 0.3–0.4% today.

### Three competing narratives fighting over one country

Contemporary Turkish politics operates through **three competing historical mythologies** that generate the emotional fuel for political conflict:

**The Kemalist narrative** holds that Atatürk saved Turkey from Sèvres through military genius, then modernized it through revolutionary transformation. The Six Arrows (Altı Ok) — Republicanism, Nationalism, Populism, Statism, Secularism, Revolutionism — were institutionally embedded through the abolition of the caliphate (March 3, 1924), adoption of the Swiss Civil Code (1926), Latin alphabet transition (1928), and women's suffrage (1934). The military served as guardian through interventions in 1960, 1971, 1980, and 1997. This narrative is carried by CHP, the urban secular class, and what remains of the Kemalist judiciary.

**The AKP/Islamist-conservative counter-narrative** frames the Kemalist republic as an alien Western imposition that severed Turkey from its authentic Ottoman-Islamic civilization. Erdoğan's "New Turkey" (Yeni Türkiye) positions AKP as liberating the pious Muslim majority from elite Kemalist tutelage. This narrative draws intellectual lineage from **Necip Fazıl Kısakürek** (1904–1983), who viewed Kemalist reforms as cultural surrender to Europe. Its concrete manifestations include the **Hagia Sophia reconversion** to a mosque (July 10, 2020 — deliberately on the Lausanne anniversary), the expansion of İmam Hatip schools from 450 with 84,000 students (2002) to **4,000+ with 1.3 million students** (2017), the removal of evolution from biology textbooks (2017), the construction of a **1,100-room presidential palace** on Atatürk's protected Forest Farm, and the elevation of the Conquest of Istanbul (May 29) as a rival national celebration. Surveys showed approximately **43% of Turks** believed Lausanne contained "secret clauses" that would expire in 2023.

**The Kurdish counter-narrative** holds that the Turkish Republic was founded on Kurdish erasure. Starting in the 1920s, Kurds were officially designated "Mountain Turks" (Dağ Türkleri) who had supposedly lost their language through geographic isolation. Kurdish language, publications, and cultural expression were banned. Uprisings were crushed — Sheikh Said (1925), Ararat (1930), and the **Dersim massacre** (1937–38, in which 13,000–70,000 Alevi Kurds were killed). In 1994, parliamentarian Leyla Zana was sentenced to 15 years for adding a Kurdish sentence to her oath of office. For Kurds, Lausanne represents a second partition — Kurdistan divided among four states — and **6,000 Kurds rallied in Lausanne on the treaty's centenary** in July 2023 to make this point.

## Section 2: Geographic determinism and persistent strategic problems

### The Straits grant Turkey permanent leverage over great powers

Turkey's control of the **Bosphorus** (31 km long, minimum width 700 m) and **Dardanelles** (61 km long) constitutes perhaps the most consequential geographic chokepoint in international politics. Over **84,600 ships** carrying 1.58 billion gross tons transited the straits in 2025 — roughly three times the Panama Canal's traffic. Approximately 20% are oil tankers carrying ~3% of global crude supply.

The **Montreux Convention** (July 20, 1936), to which the United States is not a signatory, governs this passage. Its provisions cap non-Black Sea warship tonnage in the Black Sea at **45,000 tons**, limit their stay to 21 days, prohibit capital ships and aircraft carriers, and grant Turkey the right to close the straits to belligerent warships in wartime. When Russia invaded Ukraine on February 24, 2022, Turkey designated the conflict a "war" and **closed the straits to all belligerent warships** — preventing Russia from reinforcing its Black Sea Fleet from the Mediterranean while simultaneously blocking NATO from sending warships into the Black Sea. No nation challenged Turkey's interpretation. This single decision made Turkey the functional arbiter of Black Sea naval balance.

Russia's strategic calculus depends on Montreux: the convention prevents NATO from flooding the Black Sea with overwhelming naval power while protecting Russia's warm-water fleet. Any revision would fundamentally alter the regional balance. The **Kanal İstanbul** project — a proposed 45 km artificial waterway parallel to the Bosphorus — was conceived partly to create a channel outside Montreux's scope. As of early 2026, however, the canal itself has **not begun construction** (only an associated bridge has foundation work underway), no financing consortium has committed, 77% of the public opposes it, and it functions primarily as a real estate development vehicle. Legal analysts note that ships using the canal would still need to transit the Dardanelles, which is covered by Montreux, making a full bypass impossible without a second canal.

### Eastern borders and the arc of instability

Turkey shares **822 km of border with Syria, 378 km with Iraq, and 534 km with Iran** — the entire arc of Middle Eastern instability. Since 2016, Turkey has launched four major cross-border operations into Syria (Euphrates Shield, Olive Branch, Peace Spring) and ongoing Claw operations in northern Iraq, where it maintains an expanding network of permanent bases along the Iraqi border. The October 2025 parliamentary extension of the Iraq mandate — the longest since 2016 — signals an indefinite military presence.

Turkey's overriding security preoccupation is the Kurdish corridor: a contiguous PKK/YPG-controlled territory stretching from the Qandil Mountains (Iraq-Iran border) through Sinjar to northeastern Syria and potentially to the Mediterranean. Every Syrian military operation has been designed to sever this corridor. After **Assad's fall on December 8, 2024** — Turkey's biggest foreign policy windfall in decades — this preoccupation intensified as Turkey demanded dismantling of Kurdish autonomous structures in northeastern Syria.

### Maritime claims and Blue Homeland ambitions

The **"Blue Homeland" (Mavi Vatan)** doctrine, developed by Admiral Cem Gürdeniz in 2006, claims approximately **462,000 km²** across the Black Sea, Aegean, and Eastern Mediterranean. Turkey, which has not signed UNCLOS, argues that small Greek islands near the Turkish coast — particularly **Kastellorizo/Meis** (a 12 km² island 2 km from Turkey and 570 km from the Greek mainland) — should not generate vast exclusive economic zones. The 2019 Turkey-Libya maritime MoU created an EEZ corridor that was condemned by the EU, US, Greece, Egypt, and Arab League as violating international law. The summer 2020 naval standoff with France and Greece — involving a collision between Turkish frigate *Kemalreis* and Greek frigate *Limnos* — demonstrated that maritime disputes can escalate to near-conflict.

In the Aegean, the territorial waters dispute carries existential weight: at 6 nautical miles, Greece controls ~40% of the Aegean and Turkey ~7.5%. At 12 nm (permitted under UNCLOS), Greek waters would expand to **~71.5%**, nearly eliminating high seas and preventing Turkish warships from leaving İzmir without transiting Greek waters. Turkey has declared a Greek extension to 12 nm a **casus belli** since 1995.

### Water, earthquakes, and energy geography as permanent constraints

Turkey controls the headwaters of both the **Euphrates** (~90% of flow at source) and **Tigris** (~44% of flow at source). The GAP project's 22 dams have reduced Euphrates flow to Syria by approximately 40% and Tigris flow to Iraq by up to **56–60%** following the completion of the Ilısu Dam in 2020. Iraq faces a projected water deficit of 10.8 billion m³/year by 2035; the Euphrates could run dry in Iraq by 2040. Water functions as a permanent instrument of Turkish regional leverage.

The **February 6, 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes** (M7.8 and M7.5) killed **53,537 people** in Turkey, caused $104 billion in damage (9% of GDP), and exposed catastrophic governance failures — systematic building code violations enabled by construction amnesties that Erdoğan himself promoted in a 2018 speech in the earthquake epicenter. Istanbul, with 17 million people sitting on the North Anatolian Fault, faces estimates of up to **90,000 deaths** in a major earthquake, with ~90,000 buildings at collapse risk.

Turkey's position astride every major energy transit route — BTC pipeline (1 million bbl/day from Azerbaijan), TANAP/TAP Southern Gas Corridor (16 bcm expanding to 31 bcm), TurkStream (31.5 bcm from Russia), and the Kirkuk-Ceyhan pipeline (resumed at 230,000 bpd in September 2025 after a 2.5-year hiatus) — makes it indispensable to global energy markets while remaining **~70% dependent on energy imports** itself.

## Section 3: Imperial legacy and institutional inheritance

### Ottoman DNA embedded in the modern state

The Ottoman **millet system** — which organized non-Muslim subjects into self-governing religious communities (Greek Orthodox, Armenian, Jewish) — produced a legacy that directly shapes modern Turkey. The Treaty of Lausanne adopted millet categories by restricting minority protections to non-Muslim groups, legally classifying Kurds as part of the Muslim majority and denying them minority status. This Ottoman-derived framework means that Turkey's 15–20 million Kurds, 15–20 million Alevis, and other groups have no official minority recognition.

The centralized **provincial administration** mirrors the French préfet system — 81 provinces, each governed by a centrally appointed vali (governor) who serves as the president's direct representative. No elected regional government layer exists. This structure was explicitly designed to prevent federalism or ethnic autonomy and remains the institutional backbone of the unitary state. The Council of State (Danıştay) is modeled directly on the French Conseil d'État.

The **"deep state" (derin devlet)** concept has Ottoman roots traceable to the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP) and its paramilitary Special Organisation (Teşkilât-ı Mahsusa), which operated during the Balkan Wars and World War I. These networks were never fully dismantled — they were reconstituted as intelligence organizations (Karakol → MİT), merged with NATO Cold War stay-behind operations, and surfaced in the **1996 Susurluk scandal** (a car crash that exposed police-mafia-intelligence collusion). The AKP initially weaponized the Ergenekon/Sledgehammer cases (2007–2012) to break Kemalist military power, then reversed course when the cases were revealed to be partly driven by fabricated evidence from Gülenist prosecutors.

### The most radical legal transplant in modern history

In the 1920s, Turkey executed perhaps the most dramatic legal transplant any nation has attempted — replacing Islamic law wholesale with European codes. The **Swiss Civil Code** (adopted 1926) replaced the Mecelle, abolished polygamy, mandated civil marriage, and granted women equal inheritance rights. The **Italian Penal Code** (1889 Zanardelli Code) was adopted for criminal law, the **German Commercial Code** for trade, and **French administrative law** for state governance. These transplants created a uniquely hybrid system that persists: the Swiss framework survives in the revised 2001 Civil Code, the German model remains in the 2012 Commercial Code, and the French administrative structure is intact.

### The great shift in elite formation

The most consequential institutional change of the AKP era has been the transformation of elite formation pathways. The **old pipeline** — military academies (Harbiye/Kuleli) producing a Kemalist secular officer class that served as guardian of the republic through four interventions — has been dismantled. Military academies were abolished after 2016, replaced by the National Defense University under the Education Ministry. Officer candidates now come from civilian schools, including İmam Hatip religious schools.

The **new pipeline** runs through İmam Hatip schools, originally established in 1924 to train mosque officials. Erdoğan himself is an İmam Hatip graduate. Under AKP, enrollment exploded from 84,000 students in 450 schools (2002) to **1.3 million in 4,000+ schools** by 2017, with 1,447 general high schools converted to İmam Hatips between 2011 and 2014. Meanwhile, post-2016 purges expelled **130,000+ public sector employees**, including 40% of top-ranking generals, 36% of all judges, and 6,081 academics, systematically replacing the Kemalist-Gülenist institutional class with AKP loyalists. This represents the most significant change in Turkish elite reproduction since the republic's founding.

Erdoğan has positioned Turkey for quasi-caliphal soft power projection through the **Diyanet's international expansion** — 2,000+ mosques abroad, DİTİB controlling 900+ mosques in Germany alone, operations in 145 countries, and a mega-mosque building program spanning five continents. This places Turkey in direct competition with Saudi Arabia and the UAE for Sunni Islamic leadership, offering a "less culturally alien" Ottoman-heritage Islam to global audiences.

## Section 4: Ethnic, sectarian, and linguistic cleavage structure

### The secular-religious cleavage remains structurally dominant

The **secular-religious divide** is Turkey's master cleavage — the one that structures all others. It maps onto geography (coastal cities versus interior Anatolia), class (urban professionals versus the Anatolian conservative bourgeoisie), education (secular universities versus İmam Hatip graduates), and lifestyle (alcohol consumption, gender mixing, headscarf politics). The **February 28, 1997 "postmodern coup"** — when the military forced Islamist PM Erbakan to resign after a nine-hour National Security Council session — was the climactic confrontation. The headscarf ban that followed became the central symbol of Turkey's culture war.

The AKP has **decisively won** the institutional battle on this cleavage. The military's tutelary role is eliminated. The headscarf ban is reversed — even CHP's Kılıçdaroğlu proposed constitutional protection for it in 2023. İmam Hatip schools are a permanent fixture. Yet counter-currents persist: a 2018 Education Ministry report documented growing **deism** among İmam Hatip students, and the CHP's 2024 landslide suggests a new political center forming that is secular in governance but not confrontationally anti-religious. Full re-secularization in the Kemalist sense is implausible; a stabilized "conservative democracy" with softened edges is the likelier trajectory.

### The Kurdish question enters an extraordinary new phase

Kurds constitute an estimated **15–20% of Turkey's 86 million people**, concentrated in southeastern provinces (Diyarbakır, Şanlıurfa, Van, Hakkari) and increasingly in western megacities (Istanbul hosts the world's largest urban Kurdish population). The PKK insurgency, launched in 1984, has killed approximately **40,000 people** over four decades.

The 2013–2015 peace process collapsed in July 2015 when the June elections saw HDP cross the 10% threshold with **13.12%**, costing AKP its majority. Urban warfare erupted across southeastern cities — Cizre, Nusaybin, Sur — with hundreds of civilian deaths and massive destruction. **Selahattin Demirtaş**, HDP's charismatic co-chair, has been imprisoned since November 2016 despite three ECHR rulings ordering his release. In May 2024, he received a **42-year sentence** in the Kobani trial.

The 2024–2025 peace process represents a qualitative break. After Bahçeli's October 2024 call to Öcalan, a DEM Party delegation met Öcalan on İmralı on December 28, 2024 — his first contact after nearly four years of total isolation. On **February 27, 2025, Öcalan issued a historic call for the PKK to disarm and dissolve**. On March 1, the PKK declared a ceasefire. In May 2025, the PKK held its 12th congress (with Turkish security guarantees, Öcalan participating by phone) and announced four decisions: end of armed conflict, dissolution of PKK structures, cessation of all activities in Iraq, Iran, Syria, and Turkey, and transition to legal political struggle. On **May 12, 2025, the PKK formally dissolved itself**, ending its 40-year insurgency. Symbolic weapons destruction began in July. A parliamentary commission was formed to draft legal reforms.

The process's contradictions are glaring. Turkey demands full disarmament while continuing military operations in Syria and Iraq. İmamoğlu sits in prison. DEM mayors continue to be replaced by government-appointed trustees. Demirtaş remains imprisoned in his ninth year. The widespread interpretation is that Erdoğan needs DEM's **56 parliamentary votes** to reach the 360-seat threshold for a constitutional referendum that could reset his term limits. Whether this instrumentalism can produce genuine reconciliation — or merely co-opt Kurds into reinforcing autocracy — is Turkey's most consequential open question.

### Alevis, smaller minorities, and the refugee backlash

Turkey's **Alevi population** (estimated 15–25%, or 15–20 million people) practices a syncretic faith blending Shia Islam, Sufi mysticism, and pre-Islamic traditions. Their worship houses (cemevi) are **not recognized as places of worship**; they are forced to fund the Sunni Diyanet through taxes. Historical persecutions — Dersim 1937–38, Maraş 1978, the **Sivas Madımak Hotel fire** (July 2, 1993, 35 killed) — drive overwhelming alignment with CHP (70–90%). Erdoğan established the Alevi-Bektaşi Culture and Cemevi Presidency in 2022, but it classifies Alevism as cultural rather than religious, leaving the structural grievance unresolved.

Remaining non-Muslim minorities have been reduced to near-extinction: **~2,500 Greeks** (from 100,000+ in the 1950s), **60,000–80,000 Armenians**, and **~15,000 Jews** (emigration accelerating since October 2023). The Ecumenical Patriarchate operates under severe restrictions; Halki Seminary has been closed since 1971.

The Syrian refugee population has declined from a peak of **3.7 million** to approximately **2.6 million** as of mid-2025, with over 550,000 returning after Assad's fall. Anti-refugee sentiment — the highest globally per UNHCR's 2024 survey — manifested in the July 2024 Kayseri riots and cuts across all political affiliations. Industries dependent on Syrian labor (textiles, agriculture, construction) now face workforce shortages as returns accelerate.

## Section 5: Demographic tides and generational dynamics

### Turkey's fertility collapse undermines the Erdoğan narrative

Turkey's demographic transition has accelerated beyond all projections. The total fertility rate has plummeted from **2.07 in 2017** (just below replacement) to **1.48 in 2024** — a historic low. In 2024, **71 of 81 provinces** had below-replacement fertility, up from 57 in 2017. Only Şanlıurfa remains above 3.0. Live births fell to 937,559 in 2024. Erdoğan's 2008 exhortation for families to have "at least three children" has been flatly contradicted by economic reality — inflation, housing costs, job insecurity, and declining real incomes are the actual determinants. The government declared 2025 the "Year of the Family" and introduced modest financial incentives, but family spending remains just **0.7% of GDP**, the lowest in the OECD.

Population projections tell a stark story. The current **86.1 million** (end 2025) is expected to peak at approximately **91–94 million** around 2050, then decline to below 77 million by 2100 under the UN's central scenario. The median age has risen from 25.8 in 2000 to **34.0 in 2023** and is projected to reach 44.8 by 2050. Turkey's demographic dividend window — the period when the working-age share of the population is favorable for economic growth — will close in the **early 2030s** when the elderly population exceeds 15%.

### Brain drain as a vote of no confidence

Between 2016 and 2023, Turkish emigration surged from 69,326 to **291,377 citizens annually**. The post-2016 purges expelled 6,081 academics, cancelled their passports, and barred them from employment — 37 committed suicide. EU Blue Cards issued to Turkish citizens increased **tenfold** (from 2,242 in 2012–2016 to 22,028 in 2018–2023). Doctors requesting certificates for overseas practice rose from 59 (2012) to **3,025 (2023)** — a 60-fold increase. An estimated **63% of Turkish youth** express a desire to live abroad.

The Kurdish demographic differential remains politically significant: **Şanlıurfa's fertility rate (3.28)** is more than double the national average (1.48), and 44% of its population is under 18. However, the urbanization of Kurds — millions have migrated to Istanbul, Mersin, Adana, and İzmir — is itself reducing this differential as Kurdish migrants adopt urban behavioral patterns.

Turkey's diaspora in Germany (approximately **2.8–3 million** people of Turkish background) constitutes a significant electoral bloc. In the 2023 presidential runoff, **67.2% of German-based Turkish voters** supported Erdoğan, driven by conservative rural origins, diaspora nationalism, and institutional cultivation through DİTİB mosques and AKP-aligned organizations. By contrast, Turkish voters in the US supported Erdoğan at only 17%, reflecting a more professional, secular diaspora composition.

### Youth unemployment and the NEET crisis

Turkey's youth employment picture is alarming: **31.3% of 18–24-year-olds** are NEET (not in employment, education, or training) — more than double the OECD average and the highest in Europe. Broadly defined youth unemployment (including discouraged workers) stands at **37.3%**. For young women, the figure reaches **46.7%**. University graduate unemployment is 24.9%. Women's labor force participation remains **35.8%** — the lowest in the OECD — and Turkey withdrew from the Istanbul Convention on preventing violence against women in July 2021. Turkey ranks **124th of 146** countries in the World Economic Forum's Gender Gap Report.

## Section 6: Economic structure, financial architecture, and dependency patterns

### A $1.4 trillion economy running on construction, cars, and drones

Turkey's **nominal GDP of approximately $1.4–1.5 trillion** (2025) makes it the world's 16th-largest economy and the largest in the Middle East. The sectoral mix skews toward services (58%) and industry (18%), with agriculture at just 2.7% of GDP but 15% of employment. Turkey is the world's **12th-largest auto producer** (1.4 million vehicles in 2024, 75% exported), with plants operated by Ford, Fiat, Hyundai, Toyota, Renault, and the indigenous EV startup TOGG. Tourism generated a record **$65.2 billion** from 64 million visitors in 2025, making Turkey the world's fourth most-visited country. The defense industry — led by **Baykar** (run by Erdoğan's son-in-law Selçuk Bayraktar), TAI/TUSAŞ, Aselsan, and Roketsan — generated **$8.5 billion in exports** in 2025, positioning Turkey as the 11th-largest global defense exporter.

### The central bank survived Erdoğan's war on interest rates — barely

Turkey's monetary crisis began when Erdoğan imposed his heterodox thesis that "interest rates cause inflation." Between 2019 and 2023, he fired or forced out **five central bank governors** who resisted rate cuts, triggering a catastrophic cycle: the policy rate was slashed from 24% to 8.5% while inflation soared to **85.5%** (October 2022). The lira lost approximately 80% of its value against the dollar between 2018 and 2023. The KKM (currency-protected deposits) scheme — guaranteeing lira depositors against exchange rate losses — accumulated an estimated **$60 billion in costs** before being terminated in August 2025.

The post-June 2023 pivot to orthodoxy, led by Şimşek and successive governors Erkan and then Karahan, has achieved partial stabilization. Rates were hiked from 8.5% to **50%** by March 2024, with an emergency 350-basis-point hike to 46% in April 2025 after the İmamoğlu arrest triggered market panic (the CBRT spent over **$50 billion** defending the lira). Inflation has declined from 85.5% to approximately **31%** (February 2026), and net reserves have swung from deeply negative to **$50 billion positive**. Moody's upgraded Turkey to Ba3 in July 2025. The lira, however, continues its managed depreciation at approximately **44 TL/USD** (March 2026), and the year-end 2025 inflation target of 24% was missed. The fundamental fragility remains: everything depends on Erdoğan's continued tolerance of orthodox policy as the 2028 election approaches.

### Trade, sanctions, and the dependency web

Turkey's trade profile reveals structural dependencies. The EU remains the largest export destination (Germany alone absorbs $22 billion), but **China ($49.6 billion)** and **Russia ($42.4 billion)** dominate imports. The chronic current account deficit — **$25.2 billion** in 2025 — is structurally driven by energy imports. Turkey's energy import dependency stands at approximately **70%** of total consumption, with Russia supplying ~41% of natural gas, though this share is declining as LNG imports diversify (US, Qatar, Algeria) and the **Sakarya Black Sea gas field** ramps up production toward a target of 14.6 bcm/year by 2028.

The S-400 purchase from Russia has imposed lasting costs: removal from the F-35 program (Turkey had paid $1.4 billion and was a manufacturing partner) and CAATSA sanctions on the Defense Industry Presidency. Turkey is now pursuing **40 new F-16 Block 70 jets** (~$7 billion) from Lockheed Martin and signed a **€9 billion deal for 20 Eurofighter Typhoons** from the UK in late 2025. The Halkbank Iran sanctions case — involving $20 billion in cash and gold transfers — remains active after the Supreme Court rejected sovereign immunity claims. Turkey's navigation of Russia sanctions post-Ukraine, including accusations of facilitating parallel imports, creates ongoing secondary sanctions risk.

The post-earthquake reconstruction burden totals approximately **$104 billion** (9% of 2023 GDP). External debt stands at **$565 billion**, with foreign-currency-denominated corporate debt representing the primary vulnerability rather than sovereign debt (which remains moderate at 30–35% of GDP).

## Section 7: Infrastructural inheritance and resource pathways

### Istanbul Airport and the mega-infrastructure model

**Istanbul Airport** (IGA) has become the physical embodiment of Turkey's bridging ambitions. With **84 million passengers** in 2025 (Europe's busiest by flight count for four consecutive years), it serves 330+ destinations via 116+ airlines, with 48% of passengers making long-haul transfers. Phase 2 expansion to 120 million capacity was completed in late 2025, with an ultimate target of 200 million passengers. Combined with Sabiha Gökçen's 48 million, Istanbul's airports handled **132 million passengers** in 2025.

Turkey's broader transportation infrastructure reflects an aggressive PPP/BOT model. The **1915 Çanakkale Bridge** (opened 2022, world's longest suspension span at 2,023 m), **Osmangazi Bridge** (2016), **Eurasia Tunnel** (2016, undersea road crossing of the Bosphorus), and **Marmaray** (rail tunnel under the Bosphorus, 2013) have physically stitched Europe and Asia together. High-speed rail covers **1,385 km** across three routes (Ankara-Istanbul, Ankara-Konya, Ankara-Sivas), with expansion to 4,122 km targeted by 2027. However, guaranteed minimum revenue provisions in PPP contracts have created fiscal risks when actual usage falls short of projections, burdening the treasury.

### Energy infrastructure between Russian dependency and diversification

Turkey's energy infrastructure embodies its geopolitical contradictions. As a transit hub, Turkey earns $1.5 billion annually from gas transit (projected to double to $3 billion by 2028) through TANAP, TurkStream, BTC, and the Kirkuk-Ceyhan pipeline. The **Akkuyu nuclear power plant** — four Russian-built VVER-1200 reactors totaling 4,800 MW under a unique Build-Own-Operate model where Rosatom holds ~99% equity — will supply **~10% of Turkey's electricity** when operational (Unit 1 commissioning expected 2026). Critics argue this deepens Russian leverage over Turkish energy at the precise moment Turkey is trying to diversify.

That diversification is real but incomplete. Turkey's LNG regasification capacity has expanded to **51–58 bcm/year** (second in Europe after Spain) through five terminals, theoretically sufficient to meet entire national demand without pipeline imports. BOTAŞ signed nine new LNG purchase agreements in September 2025, including a landmark 20-year $4.3 billion deal with US-based Mercuria. Renewable energy has surged: installed solar capacity reached **25.1 GW** in 2025 (surpassing natural gas for the first time), wind hit 14.8 GW, and renewables accounted for **99% of 6.3 GW** in net capacity additions. The Sakarya gas field produced 9.5 mcm/day by April 2025, with Phase 2 targeting 20 mcm/day by 2026 and ultimate production of 40–45 mcm/day by 2028.

### The Middle Corridor as strategic reorientation

The **Middle Corridor** — the Trans-Caspian route from Turkey through Georgia, Azerbaijan, across the Caspian Sea to Central Asia — represents Turkey's most ambitious infrastructure-as-geopolitics initiative. The **Baku-Tbilisi-Kars railway** (opened 2017, capacity expanded from 1 million to 5 million tons after a $250 million Georgian section upgrade in May 2024) saw container traffic increase **20%** year-on-year in 2025, with container transit from China increasing 20-fold since early 2024.

The Russia-Ukraine war dramatically accelerated interest in the Middle Corridor as sanctions and political risk made the Northern Corridor through Russia less viable. Azerbaijan's transit cargo grew from 3.8 million tons (2019) to 6.8 million tons (2023). Combined with the Marmaray freight tunnel (connecting Asian and European rail networks through Istanbul) and the under-construction Halkalı-Kapıkule high-speed line to the Bulgarian border, Turkey is positioning itself as the physical gateway linking **China, Central Asia, and Europe** — bypassing both Russia and Iran.

### Digital infrastructure and censorship architecture

Turkey's digital infrastructure serves dual purposes: connectivity and control. Mobile penetration stands at 108% (92.2 million subscriptions), internet penetration at 88.3%, and 5G spectrum auctions were completed in late 2025 with commercial launch mandated by April 2026. But the censorship architecture is equally developed: Deep Packet Inspection systems are deployed on major ISPs, social media was throttled for 42 hours during the March 2025 İmamoğlu protests, and the March 2025 Cyber Security Law grants authorities broad data access and criminalizes "false information" about data leaks. Turkey is classified as "Not Free" by Freedom House for internet freedom and ranks 159th in press freedom.

## Conclusion: Turkey navigates the void

Turkey's structural position in an era of US retrenchment is defined by five intersecting dynamics:

**Strategic indispensability despite democratic erosion.** Turkey controls the Straits, hosts NATO's nuclear-sharing arrangement at Incirlik, anchors the alliance's southeastern flank, and operates the Middle Corridor connecting Europe to Central Asia. No amount of democratic backsliding — the İmamoğlu imprisonment, media capture, judicial subordination — has produced meaningful Western consequences. The UK signed a €9 billion Eurofighter deal in late 2025 despite the crackdown; Germany lifted its veto months after İmamoğlu's arrest.

**Multi-vector autonomy replacing alliance loyalty.** Turkey simultaneously buys Russian air defense systems and British fighter jets, closes the Straits to Russian warships while growing bilateral trade to $42 billion, provides drones to Ukraine while hosting Russian tourists, and expands its defense industry to reduce dependency on any single supplier. This is not inconsistency — it is a coherent strategy of **managed indispensability** designed for a world where no single patron can be relied upon.

**The Kurdish peace process as regime survival mechanism.** The PKK's dissolution — if consolidated — would resolve Turkey's most enduring security threat. But the process is instrumentally linked to Erdoğan's need for DEM Party votes to change the constitution and potentially extend his rule beyond 2028. Whether genuine reconciliation can emerge from authoritarian instrumentalism is the defining question for Turkey's domestic trajectory.

**Economic orthodoxy on borrowed political time.** The Şimşek-Karahan stabilization has achieved real results — inflation halved from 85% to 31%, reserves rebuilt, credit ratings improved. But seven central bank governors and five finance ministers in a decade demonstrate that institutional commitments depend entirely on one person's patience. The April 2025 episode — when the İmamoğlu arrest forced the CBRT to burn $50 billion defending the lira — showed how political decisions can instantly override economic logic.

**Demographic and generational pressures building beneath the surface.** A fertility rate of 1.48, a closing demographic dividend window, 31% NEET rates among young adults, 500,000+ educated emigrants since 2016, and the lowest female labor force participation in the OECD represent structural constraints that no amount of infrastructure spending or defense exports can overcome. Turkey has roughly **10–15 years** before aging demographics foreclose the growth options that its current strategy depends upon. Whether it uses that window for genuine institutional reform or squanders it on authoritarian consolidation will determine whether Turkey remains a rising middle power or joins the ranks of countries that failed to escape the middle-income trap.

# Turkey: Analytical Intelligence Dossier — Five-Domain Assessment

Turkey occupies a singular structural position in global affairs: a NATO nuclear-sharing ally courting BRICS, a G20 economy with a corruption score matching Belarus, and a defense-industrial powerhouse whose military still bleeds from a self-inflicted purge. This dossier maps the institutional DNA, fault lines, and trajectories across five domains — environment, illicit networks, information control, international commitments, and the military-security complex — using data through early 2026. The overarching finding is one of **compounding institutional degradation masked by selective modernization**, particularly in defense exports and infrastructure, while democratic accountability mechanisms have been systematically dismantled.

---

## DOMAIN 1: Environmental and climatic structure

### Turkey's agricultural powerhouse faces an existential water crisis

Turkey ranks **7th globally in agricultural production value** (~$74 billion gross output) and first in Europe, leading world production of hazelnuts (~70% of global supply), apricots, cherries, and figs. Agriculture contributes **5.59% of GDP** (2024) and employs roughly 18% of the workforce across 2.6 million mostly small family operations on **38.1 million hectares** of agricultural land. The regional crop map is sharply defined: the Black Sea coast produces tea and hazelnuts in its 1,000–2,500mm annual rainfall; Çukurova and southeastern Anatolia produce 43.7% of Turkey's cotton (833,000 tons, 2021/22, centered on Şanlıurfa); Central Anatolia's Konya "breadbasket" grows wheat and barley on 300–350mm of semi-arid rainfall; and the Aegean and Mediterranean coasts supply olives, citrus, and figs.

This agricultural base now confronts what may be its gravest threat. The **2025 drought** — the worst in over 50 years — saw national precipitation drop **27%** below the 30-year average, with southeastern Anatolia recording a **>60% rainfall decline**. July 2025 brought the hottest temperatures in 55 years, reaching **50.5°C**. President Erdoğan estimated **23 billion TL ($557 million)** in insured agricultural losses alone, while 420,000 uninsured farmers saw incomes collapse. A staggering **75% of Turkey's lakes have disappeared** in the past 60 years, and a 2025 UN report projects **88% of Turkey's land faces desertification risk by 2030** without drastic action. Groundwater in the Konya Basin drops over one metre per year due to water-intensive cropping.

### The GAP project reshapes rivers — and regional geopolitics

The **Southeastern Anatolia Project (GAP)** — 22 dams and 19 hydroelectric plants across nine provinces — represents one of the world's largest integrated development schemes, with **$16 billion invested** of an estimated $32 billion total cost. The flagship **Atatürk Dam** (completed 1990, 184m height, third-largest earth-and-rock fill dam globally) and the controversial **Ilısu Dam** (operational ~2020, which flooded the 10,000-year-old city of Hasankeyf and displaced 25,000 people) anchor the system. Energy investment is **91.2% realized**, generating 7,483 MW of installed capacity.

However, the irrigation target tells a different story: only **33.7% of the planned 1.82 million hectares** are irrigated, and most use inefficient flood methods rather than drip irrigation, wasting roughly half the water to evaporation. The geopolitical consequences are severe. Turkish dams have curtailed **Euphrates inflows by up to 40%** and **Tigris flows by over 50%** to downstream Iraq and Syria. No binding water-sharing treaty exists between the three nations, and water remains a persistent bilateral friction point. Turkey's Foreign Minister pledged 90 m³/second flows to Iraq, but **73% of Turkey's water supply goes to irrigation** — a figure that would halve under modern techniques.

### Climate commitments earn a "critically insufficient" rating

Turkey ratified the Paris Agreement on **October 7, 2021** — the last G20 country to do so — while unilaterally declaring it would implement the accord "as a developing country" despite its Annex I classification. The **2053 net-zero target** (chosen for the Republic's 130th anniversary) is undermined by a Long-Term Strategy released at COP29 showing emissions **peaking only by 2038**, leaving just 15 years to reach zero. The updated NDC (November 2025) targets 42% below business-as-usual by 2035, translating to **643 MtCO₂e** — still **32% above 2022 levels**. Climate Action Tracker rates Turkey **"Critically Insufficient."**

The contradiction is structural. Turkey **overtook Germany in 2024 to become Europe's largest coal-fired electricity producer**, with coal generating **36% of electricity**. The 2022 National Energy Plan calls for **1.7 GW of additional domestic coal capacity** by 2030, and fossil fuel subsidies exceeded **25 billion TL ($4.4 billion)** in 2019 alone. Yet renewables are also surging: installed solar capacity reached **19.6 GW** by end 2024 (doubling in 2.5 years), wind hit 12.5 GW, and renewables constitute **59.4% of installed capacity** — though they generated only 45.5% of electricity consumed. A Climate Law passed in **July 2025** establishes a pilot emissions trading system beginning 2026 but omits any coal phase-out provision.

### The 2023 earthquake exposed institutional hollowing

The **February 6, 2023 earthquakes** (Mw 7.8 and Mw 7.7) killed **53,537 people** in Turkey, injured over 107,000, displaced 3.5 million across 11 provinces, and caused **$103.6 billion** in economic losses (~9% of GDP) — the largest disaster-related loss in modern Turkish history. As of late 2025, **455,357 homes and workplaces** had been delivered, with **360,455 people still in container housing** (including >150,000 in Hatay alone). Criminal investigations encompass **2,380 suspects**, and **116,696 administrative lawsuits** have been filed against the state.

**AFAD** (Disaster and Emergency Management Authority) performance drew devastating criticism. Effective search and rescue took **48+ hours** in many areas. AFAD's own Hatay headquarters collapsed. The agency's general manager of disaster response, İsmail Palakoğlu, was a **theologian** from the Directorate of Religious Affairs with no disaster management background. Multiple provincial AFAD heads were imam hatip graduates or relatives of AKP politicians. The 2018 dissolution of protocols enabling automatic military deployment delayed troop mobilization. The SWP Berlin assessment concluded: "The slow and uncoordinated response revealed a weakening of state capacity related to the change in the political system and the decline of public institutions."

Environmental contamination from **116–210 million tons of rubble** remains a serious concern, with outdoor asbestos levels reaching **2.57 fibres/ml** (standard: 0.1) near demolition sites. Turkey banned asbestos only in 2010, and extensive legacy use persists in older building stock.

### Economic development overrides environmental protection

The pattern recurs across multiple flashpoints. The **Kaz Dağları (Ida Mountains) gold mining controversy** saw Alamos Gold's subsidiary fell four times more trees than declared in its environmental assessment (~195,000 vs. 13,400 claimed), provoking 5,000-person protests and 270,000+ petition signatures. In 2025, politically connected **Limak Holding** won approval for a Balıkesir gold mine requiring 370,000 tons of cyanide annually. The **Çöpler gold mine disaster (2024)** — a heap collapse deemed "entirely preventable" by expert panels from ODTÜ and İTÜ — connected to Çalık Holding, whose former CEO is Berat Albayrak, Erdoğan's son-in-law. The **2021 Mediterranean wildfires** burned approximately **157,000 hectares** across Antalya and Muğla provinces — nine times the annual average — killing nine people, with severe criticism of government preparedness. Major wildfires returned in **July 2025**, killing five firefighters and five AKUT rescue workers in Eskişehir Province.

## DOMAIN 2: Illicit networks and shadow governance

### Turkey anchors the global heroin highway

Turkey functions as the critical transit node — the **"anchor point"** per Interpol — for Afghan-origin heroin reaching European markets via the Balkan Route. The UNODC estimates approximately **9.6 tons of pure heroin equivalent** transit annually from Turkey-based traffickers to 10+ European destination countries. Turkey's 2024 Interior Ministry narcotics report reveals the escalating scale: **309,028 drug incidents**, 374,948 suspects detained, and ~105 metric tons seized. Methamphetamine seizures surged **54% year-over-year** to 33,833 kg (a record), cocaine seizures rose 23.2% to 3,082 kg, and **15.9 million Captagon pills** were intercepted — primarily Syrian-origin, entering via Hatay and Gaziantep. Drug-related deaths reached **427** (up 42.3%).

The trafficking corridors are well-established: heroin enters through eastern land borders (Van, Hakkari) from Iran; cocaine increasingly arrives through container ports (**Mersin, Ambarlı, İzmit, Tekirdağ**); and Captagon transits from Syrian production centers. The Middle East Forum and Nordic Monitor note that "Turkey has never mounted a serious, sustained crackdown on upper-tier narcotics trafficking," with investigations overwhelmingly targeting low-level dealers while major traffickers and politically connected intermediaries remain untouched.

### Sedat Peker's videos cracked open the state-mafia nexus

Beginning **May 2, 2021**, fugitive crime boss **Sedat Peker** released approximately 10 YouTube videos from Dubai, collectively garnering over **100 million views**, that systematically alleged deep interconnections between AKP politicians and organized crime. Key allegations targeted:

- **Interior Minister Süleyman Soylu**: police protection, tip-offs about investigations, political partnership
- **Former Interior Minister Mehmet Ağar**: described as head of the "deep state"; implicated in the 1996 assassination of journalist Kutlu Adalı and international drug trafficking
- **Tolga Ağar** (Mehmet Ağar's son, AKP MP): accused of the rape and murder of Kazakh student Yeldana Kaharman
- **Erkam Yıldırım** (son of former PM Binali Yıldırım): alleged cocaine smuggling trips to Venezuela
- **SADAT** (Adnan Tanrıverdi's PMC): alleged arms shipments to al-Nusra Front

A Yöneylem poll found **52.6% of Turks believed Peker's allegations were true**; only 22.5% considered them slander. No substantive parliamentary inquiry was launched, and no major prosecutions resulted. Peker remains in the UAE as of early 2026, with Turkey's extradition request via Interpol Red Notice unanswered. Reports suggest UAE authorities restricted his activity under pressure from Ankara.

### The deep state evolved — it didn't disappear

The state-criminal nexus is not a matter of organized crime infiltrating the state, but of **mutual instrumentalization** dating to Cold War stay-behind operations. The **1996 Susurluk scandal** exposed this architecture in a single car crash: a Mercedes near Balıkesir contained a deputy police chief, an Interpol-wanted Grey Wolves hitman (Abdullah Çatlı, carrying a state-issued fake passport), Çatlı's girlfriend, and a DYP parliamentarian who led anti-PKK village guards. The parliamentary investigation confirmed illegal networks but stopped short of naming senior authorizers.

Under AKP rule, the old Kemalist deep state networks were initially weakened through Ergenekon and Balyoz trials (2008–2013). But after the **December 2013 corruption probe** threatened Erdoğan directly — police raids targeted 52 AKP-connected suspects, finding **$4.5 million in shoeboxes** at Halkbank CEO Süleyman Aslan's home and seizing $17.5 million in bribe money — the government pivoted. Erdoğan labeled the investigation a "judicial coup," dismissed 700+ police chiefs, removed dozens of prosecutors, and shut down the probe entirely by May 2014. The mafia figures proved useful: the **April 2020 COVID amnesty** released ~90,000 prisoners including crime boss **Alaattin Çakıcı** (responsible for 41 deaths, former MIT operative), while journalists and political prisoners remained incarcerated. Çakıcı now issues public policy statements and meets political leaders openly.

### Halkbank: $20 billion in sanctions evasion, no criminal admission

The **Halkbank/Reza Zarrab case** (U.S. v. Zarrab, SDNY) represents the most documented instance of Turkish state-facilitated sanctions evasion. Turkish state-owned Halkbank served as the **"sole repository"** for approximately **$20 billion in Iranian oil/gas proceeds**, laundered through gold transactions orchestrated by Iranian-Turkish trader Reza Zarrab. Gold exports to Iran surged from $55 million (2011) to **$6.5 billion** (2012). Zarrab testified that Erdoğan **"personally gave orders to start transactions on behalf of Iran."**

On **March 9, 2026**, DOJ and Halkbank filed a deferred prosecution agreement in which Halkbank **does not admit criminal wrongdoing** and pays no judicial or administrative fines — merely appointing an independent compliance monitor. DOJ cited Turkey's role in the October 2025 Gaza ceasefire as a factor. Under standard Treasury guidelines, civil fines could have reached **$40 billion**. Halkbank shares surged 10% on the announcement.

Turkey's Transparency International score has plummeted from **50/100 (rank 53rd) in 2013** to **31/100 (rank 124th) in 2025** — a loss of 19 points and 71 rank positions, now tying with Belarus and Uzbekistan. No independent anti-corruption body exists; the judiciary lacks independence; whistleblower protections are effectively nonexistent.

## DOMAIN 3: Information ecosystem and media structure

### Ninety percent of national media answers to the palace

Approximately **90% of Turkey's national media** operates under direct or indirect government control, according to RSF's 2025 assessment. Six conglomerates dominate — **Demirören, Kalyon/Turkuvaz, Albayrak, Ciner (now seized), Doğuş, and İhlas** — all with owners deeply tied to Erdoğan and the AKP through construction, energy, and government contract relationships.

The critical turning point was the **March 2018 sale of Doğan Media Group** — Turkey's last major independent media conglomerate — to the Demirören Group for **$916 million**, financed by a **$675 million state-bank (Ziraat) loan**. Demirören now controls Hürriyet, Posta, Kanal D, CNN Türk, and DHA news agency. The **Kalyon Group/Turkuvaz Media** empire (Sabah, ATV, A Haber) is managed by Serhat Albayrak, brother of Erdoğan's son-in-law Berat Albayrak — YouTube has labeled ATV/A Haber as "funded in whole or in part by the Turkish government." Post-2016, over **160 media outlets were forced closed** (62 newspapers, 17 websites, 15 TV channels, 5 news agencies), and TMSF (state deposit insurance fund) seized hundreds of companies.

The most recent seizure occurred in **September 2025**, when 121 companies under Can Holding (Ciner Group's media successor, including Habertürk TV, Show TV, Bloomberg HT) were taken over by prosecutors on charges of criminal organization and tax evasion. Pro-government columnists from Yeni Şafak and Sabah were immediately installed as managers.

### Independent media survives in a shrinking oxygen supply

A handful of outlets persist under extraordinary pressure. **Medyascope** (founded 2015 by Ruşen Çakır) operates as Turkey's first independent online broadcasting platform with diversified reader-funded revenue. **Halk TV** received **14 RTÜK sanctions** in 2025, including a 10-day broadcast blackout and 5% revenue fines. **TELE1** saw its editor-in-chief Merdan Yanardağ arrested on espionage charges and a trustee appointed after 15 sanctions in 2025. **Gazete Duvar closed permanently in March 2025**, citing financial difficulties from Google algorithm changes — illustrating how digital dependency creates new vulnerabilities. YouTube has become the critical alternative channel, with journalists like Nevşin Mengü (ex-CNN Türk) operating independent channels serving Gen Z audiences.

**RTÜK** — the broadcast regulator, with 9 members elected proportional to parliamentary seats, ensuring AKP-allied majority — imposed **99 sanctions in 2025** worth approximately **$5.3 million** in fines, with **25 total broadcast blackout days** concentrated on three opposition channels. The press advertising agency (BİK), controlled by presidential appointees, withholds state advertising from critical outlets and refuses to disclose allocation data.

### Turkey has become one of the world's worst environments for journalism

RSF ranks Turkey **159th of 180 countries** (2025) in press freedom — down from 99th when the AKP took power in 2002. Erdoğan was named a 2025 **"Press Freedom Predator"** by RSF. Between **37 journalists** (MLSA count) and 24 (CPJ count) were imprisoned as of 2025, with Turkey having ranked as the world's #1 jailer of journalists from 2016–2018. Charges typically include "membership in terrorist organization," "insulting the president" (Article 299, with 250+ journalists prosecuted and 77 convicted), and "spreading misleading information" under the 2022 disinformation law (4,590 investigations by 2024).

Internet censorship has reached industrial scale. Since 2007, **1,264,506 websites or domain names have been blocked** — with 311,091 added in 2024 alone (a record). During the March 2025 İmamoğlu protests, authorities imposed a **42-hour total social media shutdown**, triggering a 1,100% surge in Proton VPN signups. Freedom House scores Turkey **31/100 ("Not Free")** — the lowest in Europe.

Kurdish media faces the most severe repression. **Mezopotamya Agency (MA)** operates through rotating domains as its main site is repeatedly blocked; eight MA journalists were sentenced to **six years each** in July 2024. **Jin News** — an all-female Kurdish outlet — has seen its director imprisoned and the publication shut down and reopened multiple times under successive names.

## DOMAIN 4: International institutional commitments

### NATO's most unpredictable ally straddles every fault line

Turkey joined NATO on **February 18, 1952** and maintains its **second-largest standing army** in the alliance. Incirlik Air Base in Adana hosts an estimated **20–50 US B61 nuclear gravity bombs** under nuclear sharing arrangements, making Turkey one of five NATO allies participating in nuclear sharing (alongside Belgium, Germany, Italy, and the Netherlands). Turkey's defense spending reached an estimated **2.09% of GDP** in 2024 (~$19.39 billion), crossing the 2% threshold for the first time since 2014, with equipment spending at 31.8% — exceeding the 20% guideline. The June 2025 Hague Summit agreed to a new **5% GDP target by 2035**.

Yet public confidence in NATO is strikingly low: a July 2025 Research Istanbul poll found **72% of Turks do not believe NATO would defend Turkey if attacked**. Turkey's 20-month blocking of Sweden's NATO accession (resolved January 2024) demonstrated Ankara's willingness to weaponize alliance mechanics for bilateral leverage — extracting tighter Swedish anti-terrorism laws and linking parliamentary approval to the US F-16 sale ($23 billion package, later reduced to ~$7 billion after Turkey opted for domestic modernization of existing jets). The S-400 purchase from Russia triggered **CAATSA sanctions** on Turkey's defense procurement agency (December 2020, still in force) and removal from the F-35 program (July 2019), complicating Turkey's nuclear delivery capability as other NATO allies transition to F-35s.

### The EU accession process is dead in everything but name

Of 35 negotiation chapters, **16 have been opened and just 1 provisionally closed** (Chapter 25 — Science and Research, opened and closed the same day in June 2006). Fourteen chapters are blocked due to the Cyprus dispute and unilateral Cypriot vetoes. The last intergovernmental conference was held on **June 30, 2016**. In May 2025, the European Parliament voted **367–74** to reaffirm that accession talks must remain frozen, citing Ekrem İmamoğlu's arrest and continued democratic deterioration. Turkey's alignment with EU Common Foreign and Security Policy had fallen to **just 5% in 2024**. The Customs Union (in force since 1995) modernization talks are at a standstill.

Turkey's relationship with the **Council of Europe** is in crisis. The ECHR has found **2,889 violations** in 3,270 rulings regarding Turkey. In the **Kavala v. Türkiye** case, Turkey has defied the ECtHR's order for Osman Kavala's release for over **3,046 days** as of March 2026 — leading to only the second-ever Article 46(4) infringement proceedings in Council of Europe history. A second infringement hearing is scheduled for **March 26, 2026**. Former HDP co-leader **Selahattin Demirtaş** similarly remains imprisoned despite a Grand Chamber ruling that his detention was politically motivated.

### Turkey plays all sides on BRICS, SCO, and sanctions

Turkey's "multi-vector foreign policy" finds its clearest expression in simultaneous Western alliance membership and Eastern bloc courtship. At the October 2024 BRICS Kazan Summit, Turkey was designated a **"partner country"** rather than receiving the full membership it sought (reportedly blocked by India). As of March 2026, Turkey had **not formally confirmed** acceptance of partner status — keeping options open. Turkey has been an **SCO dialogue partner since 2012** (the first NATO member to hold any SCO status) but has not advanced to observer or full membership, partly due to Chinese caution over the Uyghur issue.

On sanctions, Turkey has **refused to join Western sanctions against Russia** following both the 2014 and 2022 invasions of Ukraine, maintaining extensive energy (TurkStream pipeline, Rosatom Akkuyu plant), tourism, and trade ties. This non-alignment enabled Turkey's mediator role (Istanbul negotiations, Black Sea Grain Initiative) but has drawn criticism for facilitating Russian sanctions evasion through re-export of Western dual-use goods. Turkey also navigated Iran sanctions with similar ambiguity, with the Halkbank case representing the most extreme documented instance. Turkey itself remains under US **CAATSA sanctions** for the S-400 purchase.

## DOMAIN 5: Military and security sector DNA

### A defense industry miracle atop institutional wreckage

Turkey's defense sector tells two simultaneous stories. The first is remarkable: defense industry turnover has grown from **<$1 billion (2000) to >$20 billion (2025)**, with exports reaching a record **$10.56 billion** — up from $248 million in 2002. Self-sufficiency rates climbed from 20% to over 80%. Five Turkish firms now rank in SIPRI's global top 100. Turkey is the **world's largest armed drone exporter** for three consecutive years, with Baykar alone generating **$2.5 billion in revenue** (88% from exports to 36 countries).

**Baykar's ecosystem** defines Turkey's defense brand. The **TB2 Bayraktar** (~$5 million/unit, 355+ produced, combat-proven in Ukraine, Libya, Nagorno-Karabakh, Syria) achieved outsized geopolitical influence. The **Akıncı** HALE UCAV carries SOM cruise missiles and AESA radar, exported to 16 countries including a **$3 billion Saudi deal**. Most remarkably, the **Kızılelma** unmanned fighter jet achieved the **world's first beyond-visual-range air-to-air kill** on November 29, 2025 using a Gökdoğan missile, and conducted autonomous close-formation flight in December 2025 — entering inventory in 2026. **Aselsan** ($3.47 billion revenue) produces radars, electro-optics, and electronic warfare systems; **Roketsan** (>$750 million exports in 2025) manufactures the SOM cruise missile, Hisar air defense family, Atmaca anti-ship missile, and Tayfun quasi-ballistic missile, achieving **>90% domestic missile self-sufficiency**.

### KAAN fighter and naval expansion signal great power ambitions

The **KAAN (TF-X) fifth-generation fighter** conducted its maiden flight on **February 21, 2024** — making Turkey the fourth nation to fly a domestically designed 5th-gen fighter (after the US, Russia, and China). Three prototypes exist, with P1's first flight expected April–May 2026. The engine remains the critical dependency: twin **GE F110-GE-129E** engines serve as interim power, but CAATSA sanctions complicate procurement of the planned 80 additional units. The indigenous **TEI TF35000** engine program targets delivery after 2030. Indonesia signed on as the first export customer (48 jets, June 2025); Pakistan agreed to joint production (January 2025); Saudi Arabia expressed interest in 100 aircraft.

Naval expansion is equally ambitious. **TCG Anadolu** (commissioned April 2023, 27,436 tons, 231m) — converted from a traditional LHD to the **world's first dedicated drone carrier** after Turkey's F-35 expulsion — now operates TB3 drones (first landing November 19, 2024) and will carry Kızılelma. Construction began **January 2, 2025** on the **MUGEM aircraft carrier** (~60,000 tons, 300m), Turkey's first full carrier. As of December 2025, **39 military ships were simultaneously under construction**, including ISTIF-class frigates, Reis-class Type 214TN submarines (TCG Hızırreis commissioned December 20, 2025), and the indigenous **MILDEN submarine program**. The **Altay main battle tank** entered mass production in September 2025 with an indigenous 1,500-hp BATU engine, resolving a long-standing German engine embargo.

### The post-coup purge hollowed out the officer corps

The second story is institutional devastation. After the July 15, 2016 coup attempt, **157 of 358 generals (44%)** were detained; 1,099 officers were dismissed; and 130,000+ civil servants were sacked across all sectors. The **Air Force was most severely damaged**: of 1,301 pilots (668 combat), **772 were dismissed and 113 suspended**, collapsing the cockpit-to-pilot ratio from >1.25:1 to **0.8:1** (fewer pilots than aircraft). Instructor pilots were also purged, devastating the training pipeline. Recovery attempts — including recruiting Pakistani trainers (blocked by US ITAR restrictions), recalling retirees, extending compulsory service from 14.5 to 21 years, and threatening to revoke civilian pilot licenses of former military aviators — have proved insufficient. As of 2025, Turkey **still suffers a pilot shortage** with slow recovery.

The structural reorganization placed the military under direct presidential control: the Chief of General Staff is now appointed by the President, who can order service commanders directly. The Gendarmerie and Coast Guard transferred to the Interior Ministry. Scholars characterize these reforms as **"coup-proofing" rather than democratic civilian oversight**. The current officer corps prioritizes loyalty to the President, with a Eurasianist faction ascendant. SADAT International Defence Consultancy — a PMC close to Erdoğan — trains proxy forces abroad.

### MİT evolved from passive intelligence to operational strike force

Under **Hakan Fidan** (MİT chief 2010–2023, now Foreign Minister), Turkey's National Intelligence Organization transformed from a passive intelligence-gathering body into an operational force with covert capabilities including armed drones, missiles, intelligence ships, and cyber operations. A 2014 law expanded foreign operations authority and granted agent immunity from prosecution. MİT was integrated into cross-border military operations (Euphrates Shield, Olive Branch, Peace Spring) and conducted **201 targeted neutralizations of PKK/YPG fighters in 181 clandestine operations** in 2023 alone. Current chief **İbrahim Kalın** (appointed June 2023), a diplomat-academic with no military background, emphasizes "intelligence diplomacy," AI, and big data.

Turkey now deploys an estimated **60,000+ military personnel abroad** — second only to the United States. Operations span northern Syria (where Turkey controls territory through Syrian National Army proxies of 30,000–80,000+ fighters), northern Iraq (40+ bases, 5,000–10,000 soldiers conducting anti-PKK Claw operations), Libya (drones, advisors, Syrian proxy fighters supporting the GNA), Somalia (**Camp TURKSOM** — Turkey's largest overseas base, training 16,000+ Somali troops, with TB2 drones conducting 19+ confirmed strikes against al-Shabaab), Qatar (Al-Rayyan base), and Northern Cyprus (~30,000 troops permanently since 1974).

### OYAK embeds the military in Turkey's economic fabric

The **Armed Forces Pension Fund (OYAK)**, established in 1961, controls **$35.4 billion in assets** (targeting $60 billion by 2030) across 130+ companies in 24 countries. Holdings include **Oyak-Renault** (automotive), **Erdemir** (Turkey's largest steel producer), cement, energy, logistics, and port operations (~10% of national bulk cargo). OYAK generates **1.7% of Turkey's total taxes and 2.3% of national exports**. This creates deep structural incentives linking the officer corps to industrial and economic policy outcomes — a form of institutional capture that operates independently of democratic oversight.

Defense spending has **more than doubled** in four years, from $13 billion (2021) to a planned $32.6 billion (2025), reaching **2.33% of GDP** in 2026. With NATO's new 5% target for 2035, Turkey would need to more than double again — a trajectory that could further entrench the defense-industrial complex's political influence.

## Conclusion: Structural contradictions define Turkey's trajectory

Five cross-cutting dynamics emerge from this analysis. First, **selective modernization masks institutional decay**: Turkey's defense exports, infrastructure megaprojects, and renewable energy rollout are genuinely impressive, but the institutions meant to provide accountability — judiciary, disaster response, media, anti-corruption bodies — have been systematically hollowed out. Second, **every domain reveals the same centralization pathology**: AFAD failed because it needed presidential approval to act; the military was restructured for loyalty over competence; media was captured through state-bank-financed acquisitions; the 2013 corruption probe was shut down by executive fiat. Third, **Turkey's strategic ambiguity is a deliberate architecture**, not mere indecision — simultaneously hosting NATO nuclear weapons while purchasing S-400s, courting BRICS while seeking EU customs union modernization, arming Ukraine while refusing to sanction Russia. Fourth, **the state-criminal nexus is structural rather than aberrational**, traceable through Susurluk (1996), Ergenekon, Peker's revelations, and the Halkbank settlement to the same underlying pattern of mutual instrumentalization between political power and organized crime. Fifth, **environmental and climatic pressures are approaching irreversibility** — 88% desertification risk, 75% lake loss, record drought, and coal expansion — yet institutional capacity to respond has been degraded by the same politicization afflicting every other domain. The compounding interaction of these five dynamics — not any single one — defines Turkey's structural risk profile through the late 2020s.

## Section 13: Turkish civil society faces systematic dismantlement

Turkey's independent civil society has undergone a trajectory from co-optation (2002–2013) to suppression (2013–2016) to active dismantlement (post-2016). Since the failed coup, the government has shut down more than **1,500 foundations and associations**, frozen assets of 770 NGOs on terrorism-financing accusations, and dismissed over **300,000 public employees** — roughly 10% of the state workforce. Freedom House rates Turkey **33/100 (Not Free)** as of 2025, a 22-point decline since 2014 placing it among the top 10 countries with the sharpest global democratic decline.

### The split bar system and the assault on professional associations

The July 2020 law allowing multiple bar associations in provinces with over 5,000 registered lawyers was designed to dilute the voting power of Turkey's largest, most independent bars — Istanbul, Ankara, Izmir, and Diyarbakır — at the national Union of Turkish Bar Associations. Even small pro-government bars send equal delegate counts, fundamentally shifting the national balance. But the government escalated beyond dilution in 2025: on **March 21, 2025**, an Istanbul court removed the entire executive board of the Istanbul Bar Association, including its president, constitutional law scholar **Prof. Dr. İbrahim Özden Kaboğlu**, after the Bar condemned the killing of two journalists in a Turkish airstrike in northern Syria. The German Bar Association and international legal bodies condemned the ruling as unprecedented.

The Turkish Medical Association (TTB), representing approximately 88% of Turkey's physicians, faces parallel pressure. Its former president **Şebnem Korur Fincancı** was arrested in October 2022 under anti-terror laws for calling for investigation of alleged chemical weapons use. In **November 2023**, an Ankara court dissolved the TTB's Central Council — the first such judicial dissolution since the 1980 coup. Turkey's physician-to-population ratio stands at **239 per 100,000**, far below the EU average of 402, as over 8,000 doctors have emigrated since 2023.

### Labor unions and the unprecedented 2025 strikes

Official January 2025 data records **2.52 million** union members out of 16.86 million registered workers, a **14.97%** unionization rate — well below the 25–30% of the 1970s. The three major confederations reflect Turkey's political polarization: **TÜRK-İŞ** (~1.75 million members, moderate/increasingly oppositional), **HAK-İŞ** (674,000, pro-AKP), and **DİSK** (~327,000, left-wing). The ITUC ranks Turkey among the "10 worst countries for workers."

The summer of 2025 produced an extraordinary rupture. For the first time in Turkish history, **all public sector union confederations — including pro-government ones — struck together**. In July, hundreds of thousands of TÜRK-İŞ members staged a one-day work stoppage affecting railways, highways, mines, power plants, and hospitals — the largest such action since the January 3, 1991 general strike. By August 18, a national strike by public employees spread across nearly every province. The broad unemployment rate had climbed to **32%** (~13 million people) by Q2 2025, driving even regime-aligned unions to break ranks.

### Universities scored 0.09 out of 1.0 on academic freedom

Turkey's academic freedom score fell **83% over 15 years** — from 0.57 in 2002 to **0.09 out of 1.0** on the V-Dem Academic Freedom Index (2024), ranking 164th of 179 countries, behind Qatar, Egypt, and Cuba. A 2016 presidential decree gave Erdoğan sole authority to appoint university rectors, replacing internal elections. In August 2024, Erdoğan appointed rectors to 13 universities; when the Constitutional Court ruled this unconstitutional in June 2024, the authority was reportedly reinstated in defiance.

The **Academics for Peace** case illustrates the pattern: 2,212 academics signed a 2016 petition calling for peace in Kurdish regions; 386 were dismissed, over 700 charged. The Constitutional Court ruled in July 2019 that penalization violated free expression, yet **88% of dismissed academics have not been reinstated** as of June 2023, with the State of Emergency Commission ignoring the Court's binding ruling. At Boğaziçi University, faculty maintained daily protest vigils for over **1,000 consecutive days** after Erdoğan appointed an AKP operative as rector in January 2021.

### GONGOs fill the institutional vacuum

As independent civil society contracts, government-organized NGOs expand. **TÜGVA** (Turkey Youth Foundation), with Erdoğan's son **Bilal Erdoğan** on its executive board, was exposed by leaked documents in October 2021 showing lists of people given references for placement in the judiciary, security forces, and ministries — described as a "parallel state structure." Its US-based affiliate TURKEN Foundation reported assets exceeding **$117 million** and spending of $50.2 million in 2025, with a 21-story Manhattan luxury tower under construction. **KADEM** (Women and Democracy Association), co-founded by Erdoğan's daughter **Sümeyye Erdoğan Bayraktar**, promotes AKP's gender agenda and played a role in Turkey's 2021 withdrawal from the Istanbul Convention. **Ensar Foundation**, whose chair is a school friend of Bilal Erdoğan, was embroiled in a 2016 scandal involving the sexual abuse of 45 children in one of its dormitories.

### The PKK peace process transforms Kurdish civil society

The most dramatic civil society development is the Kurdish peace process. On **October 22, 2024**, ultra-nationalist MHP leader **Devlet Bahçeli** stunned Turkey by publicly calling for imprisoned PKK leader **Abdullah Öcalan** to address parliament. On **February 27, 2025**, Öcalan called for PKK disarmament. On **March 1**, the PKK declared a ceasefire. On **May 5–7**, at its 12th Congress, the PKK announced its **formal dissolution**, ceasing all activities in Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria — with Öcalan participating by phone. A parliamentary cross-party delegation visited Öcalan on İmralı Island on November 24, 2025.

Yet repression of Kurdish civil society continues: the **Kobane trial** (May 2024) convicted 24 Kurdish politicians with sentences of 9–42 years. **Selahattin Demirtaş** remains imprisoned despite an ECtHR ruling. More than 26 Kurdish music and cultural events were banned between 2019 and 2024. Public opinion remains resistant: **66%** oppose meetings between parliament and Öcalan; **80%** oppose his release.

### Religious orders absorbed the Gülen vacuum

After Fethullah Gülen's movement was purged post-2016 (Gülen died **October 20, 2024**), three Naqshbandi-affiliated orders filled the institutional vacuum. The **Menzil order**, based in Adıyaman province under leader Abdülbaki Erol, broke its tradition of political neutrality to publish an open endorsement of Erdoğan before the May 2023 elections. Its affiliates — Beşir Derneği, Semerkand Vakfı, TÜMSİAD — all aligned with the ruling alliance. The **İsmailağa community**, Turkey's most conservative Sufi order, experienced contested succession after two leaders died in 2022 and 2024. The **Süleymancılar** operate thousands of student dormitories nationwide, serving as recruitment and socialization centers that partially replaced the disbanded Gülenist education network.

### NGO funding restrictions and the shelved foreign agent law

Turkey has not yet passed a foreign agent law comparable to Russia's, but the trajectory is clear. **Law No. 7262** (December 2020), ostensibly about weapons proliferation, introduced annual government inspections of NGOs, allowed the Interior Ministry to appoint trustees to organizations whose board members face mere *charges* (not convictions), and requires government permission for projects receiving overseas funding. In March 2024, the government proposed **Article 339/A** imposing 3–7 year sentences for "crimes against state security" — directly compared to Russia's and Georgia's foreign agent laws. Intense opposition forced its withdrawal in November 2024. In May 2025, Turkey's Council of State annulled several provisions of a 2021 amendment that had empowered the Interior Ministry to classify associations into risk groups.

## Section 14: Turkey's alliance architecture balances everyone against everyone

Turkey's foreign policy operates on a principle of strategic promiscuity — maintaining enough leverage with every major power to extract concessions from all while committing fully to none. This architecture is most visible in its simultaneous pursuit of American F-35s, Russian gas infrastructure, British fighter jets, and BRICS membership.

### The US relationship orbits around weapons and sanctions

The US-Turkey relationship remains structured by three interlocking disputes. The **F-16 Block 70 deal** — originally $23 billion for 40 new aircraft and 79 modernization kits — was signed in June 2024 but Turkey trimmed it to ~$7 billion by performing domestic upgrades. First deliveries are projected for **late 2026** if price disputes with Lockheed Martin are resolved. **CAATSA sanctions**, imposed December 14, 2020, over Turkey's $2.5 billion S-400 purchase remain in force, obstructing an estimated **$20 billion** in spare parts procurement. Erdoğan proposed in December 2025 to **return the S-400 to Russia and seek a refund** — a remarkable reversal — though no deal materialized. Turkey's permanent expulsion from the F-35 program continues despite Trump's expressed "openness" at a September 25, 2025 White House meeting.

That same meeting produced a **nuclear cooperation MOU** for Turkey's Sinop plant, pivoting from Rosatom to US/South Korean partners — one of several signals of a westward energy rebalancing. Turkey approved Sweden's NATO accession in early 2024, effectively unlocking the F-16 sale. **Incirlik Air Base** remains operational under NATO, though a US proposal to place the S-400 under American supervision there was rejected.

### Russia provides gas and nuclear power but declining leverage

Russia remains Turkey's largest single energy supplier, though its dominance is eroding. **TurkStream**, with 31.5 bcm annual capacity, became the **only direct Russian gas pipeline to Europe** after Ukrainian transit ceased in January 2025 — yet Russia's share of Turkey's gas imports fell to **37%** in the first half of 2025, from over 60% two decades ago. The **Akkuyu Nuclear Power Plant** in Mersin province, a ~$20 billion four-reactor VVER-1200 project fully financed by Rosatom, is delayed to 2026–2027 after Western sanctions disrupted supply chains (Siemens unable to ship components). Turkey's Energy Minister first publicly acknowledged uranium fuel dependency on Russia in November 2025.

Bilateral trade approached **$60 billion** in 2023 but showed steady monthly contraction through 2025, with imports falling from $4.4 billion in January to $3.25 billion in September. Over **6 million Russian tourists** visited Turkey in 2024, sustaining an important economic link. In post-Assad Syria, Russia's military role has been effectively diminished while Turkey signed a military cooperation agreement with the new government in August 2025.

### The Eurofighter deal anchors a UK defense partnership

On **October 27, 2025**, Turkey and the UK signed a deal for 20 new-build Eurofighter Typhoon Tranche-4 jets worth **£8 billion ($10.7 billion)** — the UK's largest fighter export in a generation. Turkey will receive **sovereign control of mission data**, a rare concession. An MoU for a total of 40 Typhoons was signed in July 2025, with additional second-hand aircraft negotiated from Qatar (12 Tranche-3A, potentially arriving early 2026) and Oman (12). Turkey's indigenous **KAAN fifth-generation fighter** completed its first flight on February 21, 2024, with first Air Force delivery targeted for end of 2028. The indigenous **TF35000 engine** (35,000 lbs thrust) is scheduled for ground testing in 2026 with full integration by 2032. Export interest is substantial: Indonesia (48 aircraft, ~$10 billion), Saudi Arabia (100 aircraft), and co-production agreements with Egypt and Pakistan.

### Gulf normalization flows through investment and arms

Turkey-UAE relations represent the most successful regional reset, with framework agreements exceeding **$50 billion** in bilateral investment. A central bank currency swap of ~$4.9 billion was renewed in 2025, and UAE's Masdar entered a major Turkish solar project. Saudi Arabia deposited **$5 billion** into Turkey's central bank, expressed interest in 100 KAAN fighters, and tightened alignment with Ankara over shared Gaza positions. Qatar remains Turkey's deepest Gulf partner: Turkey operates **Camp Tariq bin Ziyad** with an estimated 3,000–5,000 troops, Qatar provided cumulative **$15 billion** in swap agreements, and Qatari investment leads GCC flows to Turkey at **$9.9 billion**.

### Azerbaijan and Central Asia form a Turkic architecture

The **Shusha Declaration** (June 15, 2021) upgraded Turkey-Azerbaijan relations to a formal military alliance, with provisions for joint action against third-party threats and defense industry integration. Bayraktar TB2 drones were decisive in Azerbaijan's 2020 Karabakh victory, destroying 89 T-72 tanks and 543 trucks. The **Organization of Turkic States** expanded at its Gabala summit (October 2025) to include an "OTS+" format for non-member partners, announced **first joint military exercises for 2026**, and proposed a Treaty on Strategic Partnership among all Turkic States. The Zangezur Corridor through Armenia remains contested but an Azerbaijan-Armenia Joint Declaration was signed in Washington on **August 8, 2025**.

### Israel relations have collapsed to their lowest point in history

Turkey's response to the Gaza conflict escalated systematically: from suspending exports of 54 product categories (April 2024), to a complete trade halt (May 2024), to severing all diplomatic relations (November 2024), to closing airspace and ports to Israeli vessels (August 2025), to issuing **arrest warrants for 37 Israeli officials** including Netanyahu in November 2025. Despite official severance, enforcement leaks: **456 commercial voyages** from Turkish ports to Israel were recorded January–September 2025, and Turkey remained Israel's **fifth-largest import source** in 2024 at $2.86 billion. A direct Turkey-Israel military confrontation over Syria is now a scenario diplomats actively discuss.

### BRICS rejected, NATO strained, strategic autonomy preserved

Turkey became the **first NATO member to formally apply for BRICS membership** at the October 2024 Kazan summit but was offered only partner-country status — which Ankara declined, comparing it to the EU's insulting "special partnership" offers. India blocked Turkish membership at the July 2025 Rio summit over Turkey's support for Pakistan. FM Fidan's framing is revealing: "NATO is a military alliance, but the area of economic alliances has not taken on a similarly structural form for us." Turkey meets NATO's 2% GDP defense spending target and fields the alliance's second-largest army, yet remains the only NATO member that has not sanctioned Russia.

### Africa as a new sphere of influence

Turkey's Africa strategy combines drone diplomacy, base construction, and trade expansion. **Eighteen African states** now operate Turkish combat drones, and Turkey is tied with Russia as the **third-largest arms supplier in West Africa**. The Mogadishu military base (**TURKSOM**), Turkey's largest overseas facility, has trained ~16,000 Somali soldiers. A February 2024 ten-year pact commits Turkey to defending Somali maritime sovereignty and conducting **drone strikes against al-Shabaab** (19 confirmed strikes since 2022). Trade with Africa has grown **eightfold** since 2003 to surpass $40 billion, and Turkey now maintains **44 embassies** on the continent (up from 12 in 2005).

## Section 15: Constitutional engineering drives Erdoğan's path to a fourth term

Turkey's constitutional order is undergoing simultaneous erosion from multiple directions: the executive's defiance of court rulings, the weaponization of the judiciary against opposition leaders, and a transactional negotiation linking Kurdish peace to presidential term limits. The Can Atalay crisis has established the precedent that Constitutional Court rulings can be ignored with impunity — what legal scholars call **"de-constitutionalization" (anayasasızlaştırma)**.

### Five coups shaped the institutional DNA

Turkey's political system carries the genetic imprint of five military interventions. The **1960 coup** established the Constitutional Court and the pattern of military guardianship over secularism. The **1980 coup** produced the still-operative 1982 Constitution with its strong executive bias and 10% electoral threshold (reduced to 7% in 2022). The **1997 "postmodern coup"** forced Islamist PM Erbakan from power without overt force, profoundly shaping Erdoğan's subsequent approach to neutralizing the military. The **2016 failed coup**, attributed to the Gülen movement, enabled the most sweeping purge in Turkish republican history: **300,000+ dismissals**, 163 generals detained (45% of total), 2,745 judges arrested on day one, 131 media outlets shuttered, and an estimated **$11–60 billion** in business assets seized. A senior government official admitted in February 2025 that many dismissed personnel were unconnected to the coup, blaming a flawed screening algorithm called "Fetometre."

### The constitutional arithmetic requires Kurdish votes

Erdoğan's current term expires in **2028**. Under Article 101, he cannot run again — unless the constitution is changed or early elections are called (Article 116 permits a third bid if parliament triggers early elections during a second term). The ruling People's Alliance holds approximately **327 seats** (AKP 272 + MHP 47 + YRP 4 + HÜDA PAR 4) — **33 seats short** of the 360 needed to send a constitutional amendment to referendum and 73 short of the 400 for direct adoption. The **DEM Party's 56–57 seats** would bring the coalition to approximately 383–384, comfortably above the referendum threshold. This arithmetic is the fundamental reason the Kurdish peace process exists.

On **May 27, 2025**, Erdoğan appointed 10 legal scholars to draft a new constitution. An AKP constitutional commission met in Ankara on **January 12, 2026**. Erdoğan has repeatedly denied personal motivations — having also said he would not run again in 2009, 2012, 2022, and 2024 before reversing each time. AKP spokesman **Ömer Çelik** stated openly: "As those who march with our president, extending his term is on our agenda."

### The İmamoğlu prosecution is the most aggressive judicial operation in modern Turkish history

Istanbul Mayor **Ekrem İmamoğlu**, Turkey's most popular opposition politician, faces an extraordinary multiplicity of simultaneous legal attacks. His **December 2022 conviction** for insulting election officials (2 years, 7 months + political ban) remains on appeal before the Court of Cassation. On **March 18, 2025**, Istanbul University cancelled his diploma, constitutionally barring him from the presidency. The next day he was arrested; on **March 23** he was sent to Silivri Prison, removed from office, and triggered Turkey's **largest protests in over a decade** and a reported **$40 billion** in market losses.

Additional charges have accumulated: a **July 2025 conviction** for insulting a public official (20 months, on appeal), an **October 2025 espionage investigation**, and on **February 4, 2026**, a formal espionage indictment carrying **15–20 years**. Most dramatically, on **March 9, 2026** — three days before this assessment — İmamoğlu went on trial with **400+ co-defendants** in a sweeping corruption case with potential aggregate sentencing exceeding **2,000 years**. Authorities built a new courtroom at Silivri prison complex for the trial. Human Rights Watch published a detailed report on March 3, 2026 documenting the systematic judicial persecution.

### Can Atalay established the precedent for defying constitutional rulings

Lawyer **Can Atalay**, convicted to 18 years in the Gezi trial and then elected to parliament for the Workers' Party (TİP) in May 2023, became the focal point of Turkey's deepest constitutional crisis. The Constitutional Court ruled **three times** (October 2023, again in 2024, and August 2024) that his imprisonment violated his parliamentary rights and ordered his release. Each time, the Court of Cassation **refused to comply**, filing a criminal complaint against the nine Constitutional Court justices who voted for release. Erdoğan sided with the lower court. In **January 2024**, parliament stripped Atalay of his seat in a riotous session; in August 2024, the Constitutional Court declared this null and void; in response, AKP's parliamentary majority rejected restoration, and a physical brawl broke out on the floor. Atalay remains imprisoned as of March 2026.

### Succession is an open contest between intelligence chiefs and family

The most consequential internal AKP dynamic is the rivalry between potential successors. A **February 2025 SONAR poll** placed Foreign Minister **Hakan Fidan** (57, former MIT intelligence chief) at **35%** support among ruling-camp voters, followed by former Interior Minister **Süleyman Soylu** at **32.5%** (tarnished by organized crime allegations but popular with nationalists). **Bilal Erdoğan** (the president's son) polled at **14.2%** and is reportedly undergoing "political grooming" per Bloomberg. Son-in-law **Selçuk Bayraktar** (Baykar CEO) drew **12.9%**. Late 2025 reporting exposed a media-influence rivalry between the Fidan and Bilal Erdoğan camps, with arrests of journalists caught between the factions.

## Section 16: Grievance narratives form an interconnected strategic framework

Three deeply embedded collective memories — Sèvres trauma, Lausanne contestation, and Blue Homeland aspiration — form an interconnected framework that shapes Turkish domestic politics and foreign policy. These narratives are not merely historical; they are actively instrumentalized by the current government and enjoy significant public resonance.

### Sèvres syndrome drives 62% of Turks to believe in Western dismemberment plots

The **Treaty of Sèvres** (1920), which would have partitioned the Ottoman Empire into a rump Anatolian state, was never implemented but left a permanent scar on Turkish political culture. The **A&G Research Center** found **62%** of Turks believe Western powers maintain active programs to dismember Turkey; **52%** believe EU reform demands amount to "capitulation." The 2016 failed coup dramatically intensified this narrative: Erdoğan declared it "the second War of Independence" and stated that had it succeeded, Turkey would have received "a treaty that would have made us long for Sèvres."

The syndrome manifests across policy domains: Kurdish self-determination is framed as Western-backed dismemberment, Armenian Genocide recognition as a tool for territorial claims, EU accession conditions as sovereignty violations. Academic analysis identifies it as "an obstacle to a peaceful Turkish-Armenian relationship" and the primary driver of Turkey's rejection of external criticism.

### Lausanne's centenary exposed a deep ideological fissure

The 100th anniversary of the Treaty of Lausanne on **July 24, 2023** became a battleground between two Turkish identities. Erdoğan's commemorative message called it "one of the turning points in our history" while carefully noting Turkey would pursue "new initiatives" — a diplomatic echo of his **September 2016 speech** in which he declared Lausanne "could not be regarded as a victory," arguing that Turkey's delegation "gave away islands to Greece." The opposition defended Lausanne as the republic's founding document. CHP leader Kılıçdaroğlu declared: "Lausanne is Turkey's deed."

Perhaps most remarkable was the revelation that **approximately 50% of Turks** believed the treaty would "expire" in 2023 and its alleged "secret articles" would be unveiled — a conspiracy theory traced to pro-Nazi author Cevat Rıfat Atilhan and Erdoğan's intellectual inspiration **Necip Fazıl Kısakürek**. Some **43% of university graduates** held this belief. In Lausanne, Switzerland, ~6,000 Kurds held the largest rally in the city's history, highlighting the treaty's consequences for the Kurdish people.

### Blue Homeland claims 462,000 square kilometers of maritime territory

The **Mavi Vatan** (Blue Homeland) doctrine, coined by Rear Admiral **Cem Gürdeniz** in 2006, claims approximately **462,000 km²** across the Black Sea, Aegean, and Eastern Mediterranean — rejecting UNCLOS (which Turkey has not ratified) and denying that Greek islands generate full maritime jurisdiction when near the Turkish mainland. The doctrine has achieved rare **cross-party consensus**: AKP, MHP, CHP, and İYİ Party leaders have all endorsed it. The **November 2019 Turkey-Libya maritime MoU** was its most concrete manifestation, creating a shared maritime border that disrupted the Greece-Cyprus-Egypt corridor.

Annual **Mavi Vatan naval exercises** continue, with the January 2025 iteration showcasing Turkey's shift toward maritime power projection. The Turkish Navy has **31 ships under construction** in domestic shipyards as part of an estimated $8 billion modernization program.

### Anti-Western sentiment is structural but nuanced

Pew Research data reveals that Turkish anti-Americanism, triggered by the 2003 Iraq invasion, has never recovered: US favorability stood at **~25%** in 2025, up from **18% in 2024** (one of only three countries where US favorability rose under Trump's second term). But the picture is more complex than simple anti-Westernism. EU favorability has risen to **46%** (up from 34% in 2019), NATO favorability increased **21 percentage points** since 2019, and support for EU membership reached **56%** in 2024 (up from 40% in 2017). A generational divide is stark: **42%** of Turks under 35 view the US favorably versus only **13%** of those over 50. Erdoğan's eastward pivot toward Russia and China contradicts the public's growing pro-European orientation.

A 2025 ASAL poll found **84%** view Israel as an enemy, **75%** view the United States with hostility, and **55%** see France as an adversary. The Friedrich Ebert Foundation's 2025 security survey found **47% of Turks blame the US** for the Russia-Ukraine war (versus 50% blaming Russia), and only **27%** support military aid to Ukraine — the lowest among NATO countries polled.

### Armenian Genocide recognition has expanded to 34 countries

As of 2025, **34 UN member states** plus the Holy See and European Parliament have formally recognized the Armenian Genocide. Biden's April 2021 statement made the US the highest-profile recognizer, but Trump's **April 24, 2025** Remembrance Day message dropped the word "genocide" — a regression. In August 2025, Netanyahu "personally recognized" the genocide (without formal Israeli government action) to antagonize Turkey, prompting Ankara to close airspace to Israeli aircraft. Turkey and Azerbaijan remain the only two countries that formally deny the genocide characterization.

### Neo-Ottomanism fills a legitimacy vacuum with spectacle

Ottoman nostalgia has become the AKP's primary cultural legitimation strategy as economic conditions deteriorate. The **Hagia Sophia reconversion** (July 2020, deliberately timed to the Lausanne anniversary) and Chora Church conversion served as symbolic acts of civilizational reclamation. Ottoman-themed TV series — **Diriliş: Ertuğrul** (150 episodes), **Kuruluş: Osman**, **Payitaht: Abdülhamid** — command audiences of approximately **650 million globally**, functioning as soft-power instruments particularly influential in Pakistan, Central Asia, and the Arab world.

As political scientist M. Hakan Yavuz argues, Erdoğan exhibits "near-obsessive reverence" for two Ottoman sultans: **Mehmed II** (the Conqueror) and **Abdulhamid II** (the last absolutist). The January 2026 analysis from E-International Relations captures the dynamic precisely: "As economic crises deepen, the government increasingly relies on the civilizational prestige of a reimagined Ottoman past to fill the legitimacy vacuum. **Prestige replaces prosperity; spectacle substitutes for stability.**"

### The TRNC election complicates Turkey's two-state push on Cyprus

The **October 2025 election** of **Tufan Erhürman** as TRNC president with **62.8%** of the vote — defeating hardline incumbent Ersin Tatar — represents a significant challenge to Turkey's two-state strategy. Erhürman, a constitutional law professor, campaigns for a **bi-zonal bi-communal federal solution** within UN parameters. His first meeting with Greek Cypriot President Christodoulides on November 20, 2025 generated cautious optimism. MHP leader Bahçeli responded by calling for Northern Cyprus to be absorbed by Turkey — revealing nationalist anxiety at the prospect of reunification talks resuming. Turkey maintains **35,000+ troops** in Northern Cyprus, and the Varosha/Maraş reopening has attracted nearly **5 million visitors** since October 2020.

## Section 17: Turkey's geopolitical positioning maximizes leverage from geography

Turkey has parlayed its geographic position — controlling the Black Sea straits, bordering Syria and Iraq, and straddling Europe and Asia — into outsized influence in multiple theaters simultaneously. Its most important role is as the venue and mediator for the only direct Russia-Ukraine negotiations since 2022, while simultaneously emerging as the dominant external power in post-Assad Syria.

### The Istanbul Process hosts the only Russia-Ukraine track

Turkey's **"Istanbul Process"** has produced the most sustained direct Russia-Ukraine diplomatic contact since the war's early months. **Round 1** (May 16, 2025) brought delegations led by Ukraine's Rustem Umerov and Russia's Vladimir Medinsky to agree on exchanging 1,000 prisoners per side. **Round 2** (June 2, 2025) expanded to prisoner exchanges and repatriation of approximately 6,000 soldiers' remains. Since early 2025, **512 fighters** have been exchanged and over **18,000 bodies** transferred. A trilateral meeting involving the US was scheduled for **March 11, 2026** — days before this assessment. FM Fidan has reaffirmed Turkey's readiness to host a leaders' summit.

Turkey's credibility as mediator rests on a carefully calibrated balancing act. It is **the only NATO member that has not sanctioned Russia**, yet it closed the Bosphorus and Dardanelles to belligerent warships under the Montreux Convention on **February 27, 2022** — preventing either side from reinforcing Black Sea naval assets. It sold Bayraktar TB2 drones to Ukraine (a Ukrainian production facility became operational in August 2025) while maintaining $60 billion in annual Russian trade. The Black Sea Grain Initiative (July 2022–July 2023) facilitated **33 million tonnes** of grain exports to 45 countries before Russia withdrew; FM Fidan proposed a new limited agreement in December 2025 after Russian forces struck a Turkish-owned vessel at Chornomorsk.

### Post-Assad Syria is Turkey's greatest strategic prize

The fall of Assad's regime on **December 8, 2024** validated Turkey's 13-year investment in the Syrian opposition. The new government, led by HTS chief **Ahmed al-Sharaa** (who dropped his former nom de guerre Abu Mohammed al-Julani), made his first official state visit to Ankara on **February 4, 2025**. Turkey's MİT Director İbrahim Kalın and FM Fidan visited Damascus immediately after the regime change. Trump reportedly told Erdoğan: "You've taken it over through surrogates."

Turkey's economic penetration is rapid: exports to Syria reached **$3 billion in 2025** (up from $2.6 billion for all of 2024, a 54% increase), and Turkey committed to supplying **900 MW of electricity** by 2026 through a new Kilis-Aleppo gas pipeline channeling Azerbaijani gas. Turkish and regional firms committed **$14 billion** for infrastructure development. The approximately **$400 billion reconstruction market** represents an enormous prize for Turkish construction conglomerates.

Over **578,000 Syrian refugees** returned from Turkey to Syria in the year following Assad's fall, reducing the registered population from nearly 3 million to approximately **2.3 million**. The SDF/SNA integration deal agreed March 10, 2025 has stalled, with Turkey warning of military action if SDF forces do not dissolve. The Turkey-Israel rivalry over Syria is now the most dangerous flashpoint: Israel conducted **988 air and artillery strikes** in the seven months post-Assad, and Turkey is reportedly installing radar systems on Syrian territory to track Israeli aircraft.

### The Middle Corridor grew 62% in 2024

The Trans-Caspian International Transport Route — running from China through Kazakhstan, across the Caspian Sea, through Azerbaijan, Georgia, and Turkey to Europe — has emerged as the most viable alternative to Russia's Northern Corridor (which lost 34% of shipping volume in 2022). Cargo volumes reached **4.5 million tonnes** in 2024, a **62% increase** year-on-year. China-Europe container traffic via the corridor exceeded **27,000 TEU** — a **25-fold increase** over 2023. Azerbaijan Railways plans to triple block trains to 1,000 in 2025. The EBRD estimates **€18.5 billion** in needed infrastructure investment, with transit costs of $2,500–$3,250 per container and a target of under 15 days transit time.

### Defense exports hit $7.4 billion, Baykar leads global drone market

Turkey's defense industry achieved record exports of **$7.4 billion** in the first 11 months of 2025, a 30% increase over the prior year, with products reaching **180 countries**. At IDEF 2025 (Istanbul, July), **$5.85 billion** in export deals were signed. Five Turkish companies now rank in the global Top 100 defense firms. **Baykar** alone exported **$2.2 billion** in 2025 — world's largest armed drone exporter for three consecutive years — with agreements covering **37 countries**. The TB2 completed 1 million flight hours globally by December 2024. A Baykar-Leonardo joint venture (LBA Systems) formed in mid-2025 signals NATO-standard integration, and Baykar's acquisition of Italy's Piaggio Aerospace secured a European production base. The unmanned fighter jet **Kızılelma** is scheduled to enter military inventory in 2026.

### Greece arms at $27 billion while Turkey deploys F-16s to Cyprus

Turkey-Greece relations follow a pattern of diplomatic thaw punctuated by military provocations. Erdoğan's December 2023 Athens visit (first in six years) and subsequent high-level meetings generated a "positive agenda." Greece implemented visa facilitation for Turkish visitors to Aegean islands. Yet incidents persist: in July 2024, Turkish warships pressured cable-laying vessels near Karpathos; in September 2025, armed Turkish F-16s entered Athens' flight information region for the first time in over two years; in November 2025, Greek coast guard fired warning shots at Turkish fishing boats escorted by the Turkish coast guard. Greece announced a **$27 billion, decade-long defense modernization** driven largely by Turkish belligerence, while Turkey deployed **six F-16s and an air defense system** to northern Cyprus in early 2026. The 1995 *casus belli* declaration against Greek extension of territorial waters to 12 nautical miles remains in effect.

## Conclusion: five tensions that will define Turkey's trajectory

This assessment identifies five structural tensions that will shape Turkey through 2028 and beyond. **First**, the constitutional clock: the DEM Party's 57 seats are the mathematical key to Erdoğan's fourth term, making the Kurdish peace process simultaneously Turkey's most hopeful development and its most cynical transaction. **Second**, institutional collapse: the Can Atalay precedent has established that constitutional rulings carry no enforcement power, while the İmamoğlu prosecution has demonstrated that any political threat can be neutralized through judicial architecture regardless of popular mandate. **Third**, the alliance paradox: Turkey's strategic autonomy delivers remarkable diplomatic returns — hosting Ukraine-Russia talks while arming Ukraine, buying Russian gas while pursuing NATO fighters — but risks structural isolation if forced to choose. **Fourth**, the generational divide: Turks under 35 are significantly more pro-Western (42% vs. 13% US favorability among over-50s), more urbanized, and more connected to global norms — a demographic time bomb under the current political settlement. **Fifth**, the legitimacy substitution: as economic pain persists (inflation at ~31%, broad unemployment at 32%), the government increasingly relies on neo-Ottoman spectacle, defense-industry nationalism, and foreign-policy adventurism to replace the prosperity that initially built AKP's dominance. Whether prestige can indefinitely substitute for prosperity is the central question of Turkish politics.
