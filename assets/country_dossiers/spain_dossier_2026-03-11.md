## Section 0: Key actors and institutions primer

Spain's institutional architecture is a parliamentary monarchy erected atop a deeply layered state tradition — Roman legal codes, Habsburg composite governance, Bourbon centralism, Francoist authoritarian residue, and a post-1978 democratic framework bound by EU treaty obligations. The system's formal design distributes power across a constitutional monarch, a bicameral legislature, an executive presidency, an independent judiciary, and seventeen autonomous communities. In practice, power concentrates in the Presidency of the Government, is constrained by coalition arithmetic, and is contested by a judiciary whose independence has been structurally compromised by partisan appointment mechanisms.

### The Crown and the executive

**King Felipe VI** (since June 2014) holds a formally ceremonial role: he proposes the prime ministerial candidate after investiture consultations, signs laws, and serves as commander-in-chief. His actual political function extends beyond protocol at moments of systemic crisis — his October 2017 televised address denouncing Catalonia's unilateral independence declaration was decisive in rallying unionist sentiment. The monarchy's legitimacy remains a partisan fault line; approval ratings recovered from the scandals of his father Juan Carlos I (in self-imposed exile in Abu Dhabi since 2020), but the left periodically calls for a republican referendum. The institution is regime-specific to the Transition settlement, not historically inevitable. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

**Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez** (PSOE, in office since June 2018, third term from November 2023) wields extensive executive powers: he proposes and dismisses ministers, sets government policy, and holds the prerogative to dissolve the Cortes. The constructive vote of no confidence — requiring an alternative candidate — makes him extremely difficult to unseat. His minority coalition (PSOE + Sumar, ~152 seats of 350) depends on external support from seven parties, making every significant vote an ad hoc negotiation. Spain has not passed a new General State Budget since 2023, operating on rolled-over *prorrogados* — testament to the government's fragile arithmetic. In March 2026, Sánchez denied the US use of Rota and Morón bases for strikes against Iran, provoking direct confrontation with President Trump, who threatened trade retaliation. This episode illustrates how PM powers in foreign and defense policy create immediate international friction under conditions of American unpredictability. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### The legislature and electoral system

The **Cortes Generales** comprise the Congress of Deputies (**350 seats**, decisive chamber) and the Senate (266 seats, subordinate). Congress seats are distributed across 52 constituencies using the D'Hondt proportional method with closed party lists and a 3% per-constituency threshold. Each province receives a minimum of two seats; the remaining 248 are allocated by population. This system structurally advantages large parties and **overrepresents rural provinces** — Madrid and Barcelona are underrepresented relative to population, with significant consequences for the weight of *España vaciada* constituencies. The Senate can delay or amend legislation but Congress overrides by absolute majority; PP holds a Senate majority and used it to veto the Amnesty Law in 2024, only to be overridden (177–172). [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### The judiciary: politicization as structural feature

The **Constitutional Tribunal** (12 members, 9-year terms) has become acutely politicized. Its current progressive majority (6–4 or 6–5 depending on the case) resulted from the government's contested 2022–23 renewal. On **26 June 2025**, it upheld the Amnesty Law as constitutional in a 6–4 vote — a ruling fiercely contested by PP and the conservative judiciary. The Supreme Court had already ruled in July 2024 that the amnesty does not cover Puigdemont's embezzlement charges, referring questions to the CJEU. The Tribunal thus functions as a de facto political arbiter whose composition directly reflects which parties controlled its appointments.

The **CGPJ** (General Council of the Judiciary) — governing judicial appointments, promotions, and discipline — endured a **5.5-year renewal blockade** (December 2018 to July 2024), the longest in democratic history. PP exploited the three-fifths parliamentary majority requirement to maintain ideological control, leaving 20 Supreme Court vacancies (25% of staff) unfilled. After European Commission mediation, new members assumed office on 25 July 2024. The fundamental tension — parliamentary election of judicial governors creating de facto politicization — remains unresolved. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Political parties: the current constellation

The July 2023 general election produced the following Congress configuration: **PP** 137 seats (led by Alberto Núñez Feijóo), **PSOE** 122, **Vox** 33 (Santiago Abascal, down 19 seats), **Sumar** 31 (Yolanda Díaz's left coalition), **ERC** 7, **Junts** 7 (Puigdemont's party, became kingmaker), **EH Bildu** 6, **PNV** 5, **BNG** 1, plus minor parties. **Ciudadanos** is defunct, having collapsed entirely in 2023. The Sánchez investiture required the **Amnesty Law** as Junts' price of support. However, Junts withdrew parliamentary support in late 2025 over immigration policy disputes, leaving the government without a reliable majority. Díaz announced in February 2026 she will not be a candidate in 2027. PP leads all polls as of early 2026; Vox has consolidated as Spain's third force, capitalizing on anti-immigration sentiment. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Security apparatus

The **Armed Forces** (FFAA) comprise approximately **116,400 active personnel**, well below the 140,000 legal ceiling. Defense spending reached **$22.27 billion in 2024** — just **1.28% of GDP, the lowest proportion in NATO**. Spain committed to 2% by 2029 but resisted the alliance's 3.5%/5% targets. Approximately 4,000 military and Guardia Civil personnel are deployed across 15 countries. The **Naval Station Rota** (Cádiz) hosts four US Navy Aegis destroyers — critical for NATO ballistic missile defense — and **Morón Air Base** serves as a key US logistics hub. The March 2026 denial of US base access for Iran strikes represents the most consequential test of the bilateral defense relationship in decades.

The **CNI** (Centro Nacional de Inteligencia, successor to CESID since 2002) was embroiled in the **Pegasus scandal** (2022), when surveillance of PM Sánchez's phone and Catalan independence leaders was revealed. The criminal investigation was **shelved for a second time in January 2026** due to Israel's complete failure to cooperate — the judge termed this a "manifest breach of international obligations." The **Guardia Civil** (~80,000, gendarmerie-type, rural areas and borders) and **Policía Nacional** (~65,000, civilian, urban) divide territorial and functional jurisdiction under the Interior Ministry. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Economic actors and autonomous communities

Four IBEX 35 companies exceed **€100 billion market capitalization**: Inditex, Santander, Iberdrola, and BBVA. **Telefónica** became a geopolitical flashpoint when Saudi Telecom Company (STC) acquired a 9.9% stake in 2023, prompting **SEPI** (state industrial holding) to acquire 10% in response and engineer a CEO replacement — the most significant state re-entry into a privatized company since the 1997 sale. SEPI's combined holdings now represent **4.1% of IBEX 35 market capitalization**, the highest in 27 years.

**Catalonia** is now governed by **Salvador Illa** (PSC-PSOE, since August 2024), ending over a decade of pro-independence regional government. The **Basque Country** is led by **Imanol Pradales** (PNV, since June 2024), governing in coalition with PSE-EE. The Basque **Concierto Económico** — under which the three foral provinces collect virtually all taxes and pay an annual *cupo* to Madrid — grants fiscal sovereignty unmatched in Europe short of full nation-states. This asymmetry is a permanent source of inter-territorial tension.

Among external actors, the **EU** is the most structurally consequential: the ECB directly shapes monetary conditions, the Commission mediates institutional crises, and NextGenerationEU funds (~€163 billion) constitute Spain's primary fiscal lifeline. **Morocco** leverages migration control as diplomatic currency; Spain's 2022 endorsement of Morocco's Western Sahara Autonomy Plan was extracted under border pressure. **NATO** provides the alliance framework, but Spain's lowest-in-NATO defense spending and the March 2026 base-access crisis illustrate the structural friction produced by American retrenchment and unpredictability. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

## Section 1: Formation trauma and founding mythology

Modern Spain's identity rests on a succession of layered traumas, each incompletely processed and politically contested. The most consequential is the **Transición** (1975–1982) — the negotiated transition from dictatorship to democracy — which functions as the central founding mythology of the post-1978 regime. This mythology is now under sustained challenge from both left and periphery, creating an ongoing legitimacy contest that structures contemporary politics.

### From Reconquista to imperial collapse

The **Reconquista narrative** — framing the Christian campaigns against Muslim-held Iberia (711–1492) as a unified, continuous national reclamation — is Spain's most deeply embedded historical myth. The term itself is a **19th-century invention**, first entering the Real Academia Española dictionary in 1936, the year Franco launched his coup. Serious medievalists describe the nationalist reading of a unified Christian campaign to reclaim a pre-existing "Spain" from Islam as a fundamental misreading of medieval reality. Under Francoism, the narrative served as the conceptual touchstone of National-Catholic historiography. Today, **Vox has made the Reconquista its central political symbol**: in 2019, Abascal launched his campaign at Covadonga — the legendary site of the first Christian victory in 722 — declaring "the Reconquista has started in the south." The left and progressive historians critique this as an exclusionary, Catholic-supremacist construction instrumentalized for anti-Muslim and anti-immigrant mobilization.

The year **1492** constitutes Spain's foundational triple event: the fall of Granada (January 2), the Alhambra Decree expelling Jews who refused conversion (March 31, affecting an estimated 40,000–200,000), and Columbus's departure (August 3). Together, these constructed an identity of **Catholic exclusivism fused with global imperial ambition** — an equation Franco would revive as *nacionalcatolicismo*.

The **1898 "Disaster"** — Spain's loss of Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Philippines, and Guam to the United States — shattered pretensions of world-power status. The **Generation of '98** (Unamuno, Machado, Baroja, Azorín) emerged as the defining intellectual response, interrogating Spanish identity and calling for Europeanization. The economic historian Leandro Prados de la Escosura argues the direct economic impact was modest, but the indirect consequence was a drift toward autarchy via intense defensive nationalism. The crisis also catalyzed peripheral nationalisms: Catalan nationalism gained momentum through the *Renaixença*, and Basque nationalism emerged under Sabino Arana. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Civil War, dictatorship, and the Transition as founding myth

The **Second Republic** (1931–1939) attempted radical modernization — agrarian reform, church-state separation, Catalan autonomy — but deep polarization produced the **Civil War** (1936–1939, ~500,000 dead). The Republic received limited Soviet support and the International Brigades (~35,000 volunteers from 53 countries); the Nationalists received massive German (Condor Legion, Guernica bombing) and Italian (~75,000 troops) intervention. Britain and France maintained non-intervention. Franco's dictatorship (1939–1975) passed through an autarky phase (international isolation, severe repression, perhaps 20,000 post-war executions), then a **technocratic opening** from 1959 driven by Opus Dei economists who engineered the "Spanish Miracle" — GDP growth second only to Japan's.

The **Transición** is the regime's creation story. Franco died on 20 November 1975. King Juan Carlos I moved toward democracy, appointing Adolfo Suárez as PM in July 1976. The sequence: the Political Reform Law (November 1976, the Francoist Cortes voting itself out of existence), legalization of the Communist Party (April 1977), first elections (June 1977), the Moncloa Pacts (October 1977), the **1978 Constitution** (approved by 87.9% referendum, with 32.9% abstention), and the **23-F coup attempt** (23 February 1981) — when Lieutenant Colonel Tejero stormed Congress and Juan Carlos's 1:14 AM televised address ordering military loyalty to democracy became the single most iconic moment of modern Spanish history. PSOE's Felipe González won by landslide in October 1982, consolidating democracy.

This narrative has been increasingly contested since the 2000s. The left, particularly Podemos, uses the pejorative *régimen del '78* to frame the Constitution as a pact that preserved Francoist state structures. As González himself admitted: **"the state apparatus was retained, in its entirety, from the dictatorship."** Peripheral nationalists note the PNV's abstention from the constitutional referendum and view the 2017 Catalan crisis as proof of the Constitution's rigidity. The "Culture of the Transition" (CT) describes the elite consensus around territorial unity, monarchy, Constitution, amnesty/impunity, market economy, and Atlanticism — a consensus now eroding. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### The Pact of Forgetting and its unraveling

The **Pacto del Olvido** was an informal but binding agreement to avoid confrontation with the Francoist legacy during the Transition. Codified in the **1977 Amnesty Law**, it freed political prisoners while ensuring no prosecutions for human rights violations. An estimated **114,000 civilians "disappeared"** in Francoist killings. No truth commissions, no trials, no systematic exhumations followed. The **Valle de los Caídos** — a monumental mausoleum built by Republican forced labor, housing 33,000+ victims of both sides — continued to hold Franco's remains alongside José Antonio Primo de Rivera.

Challenges to the pact have escalated: the **2007 Historical Memory Law** (Zapatero) formally condemned repressions but delegated exhumation costs to families; the Rajoy government defunded it entirely. **Franco's exhumation** from the Valle de los Caídos on 24 October 2019 was a pivotal symbolic moment. The **2022 Democratic Memory Law** (Sánchez) made the state assume responsibility for exhumations, created an official Register of Victims, abolished 33 Franco-era noble titles, and mandated teaching about the dictatorship in schools. PP and Vox opposed both laws. The UN has repeatedly urged Spain to repeal the 1977 Amnesty Law, citing international prohibitions on amnesty for crimes against humanity. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

## Section 2: Geographic determinism and persistent strategic problems

Spain's geography produces a distinctive strategic profile: **semi-isolation from continental Europe**, **dual Mediterranean-Atlantic maritime orientation**, **direct African exposure**, and **severe internal resource asymmetries**. These features are not background conditions — they are active determinants of policy options, alliance value, and vulnerability.

### Peninsula, barrier, and the meseta's centralizing logic

The **Pyrenees** (430 km, Bay of Biscay to Mediterranean) create a formidable natural barrier. Even today, transport connections across them are severely limited: the sole high-speed rail connection to France is the Perpignan-Figueres line (completed 2013, including the 8.3 km Perthus Tunnel), and gauge incompatibility (Spain's 1,668mm Iberian gauge vs the 1,435mm European standard) impedes rail freight. **France is Spain's only land gateway to continental Europe**, giving Paris outsized influence over Spain's energy and trade connectivity.

The **Meseta Central** — a vast high-altitude plateau (600–700m) — made Madrid the natural seat of centralized power. The radial administrative and transport model (roads, rail, highways emanating from Madrid like spokes) reinforces center-periphery tension: coastal peripheries (Catalonia, Basque Country, Valencia, Andalusia) possess their own economic dynamism and outward-facing orientations that clash with Madrid's hub logic.

Spain commands **three distinct coastlines** — Mediterranean, Atlantic, and Cantabrian — giving it rare dual maritime orientation. The Mediterranean coast drives tourism, agriculture, and trade with Europe and North Africa; the Atlantic coast anchors connections to the Americas and West Africa; the Cantabrian coast connects to northern European maritime routes. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Gibraltar, Ceuta, Melilla, and the Canary Islands

The **Strait of Gibraltar** (14.2 km at narrowest) carries approximately **100,000 vessel transits annually** — over 300 ships per day — carrying more than 10% of global maritime trade. Spain holds **Ceuta** (since 1668) and **Melilla** (since 1497) on the North African coast — the EU's only land borders on the African continent, both claimed by Morocco. The **Port of Algeciras** handled 104.8 million tonnes of cargo in 2023 and 4.7 million TEUs of containers in 2024. Competition from Morocco's rapidly expanding **Tanger Med** port (10.24 million TEUs in 2024, +18.8%) is reshaping the balance across the Strait. The **US Naval Station at Rota** anchors Western naval presence at the Strait's Atlantic entrance, providing missile defense and rapid power-projection capability.

The **Canary Islands** (~100 km off Africa, ~1,300 km from mainland Spain) extend Spain's and the EU's territorial presence into the mid-Atlantic. The archipelago (population 2.27 million, GDP ~€64 billion) is a critical node in African migration to Europe — the Western African route saw over 46,843 irregular arrivals in 2024 alone. Spain's total **EEZ of 1,039,233 km²** (30th globally, approximately double its land area) is heavily driven by the Canary Islands component. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Water: Spain's accelerating strategic vulnerability

Spain faces a **profound and worsening water crisis**. Average annual rainfall exceeds 2,000mm in Galicia but falls below 200mm in Almería and Murcia. According to the Ministry for Ecological Transition, **74% of Spanish territory is at risk of desertification**. In 2024, rainfall hit its **lowest level in 150 years**. Catalonia endured a 36-month drought that left Barcelona-area reservoirs at 18% capacity. Agriculture consumes approximately **80% of total water**. Spain's installed desalination capacity (~5 million m³/day) is substantial but only 16–20% is typically utilized due to high energy costs. Climate models project **20–40% reductions in river flows** in key basins by mid-century. The political conflict between transfer and desalination strategies — the Aznar government's proposed Ebro transfer (2001) versus the Zapatero AGUA program (2004) — remains unresolved and will intensify. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Resource geography and renewable potential

Spain produces virtually no domestic oil or gas, creating structural energy import dependency. However, it possesses **exceptional renewable energy potential**: solar irradiation across the southern two-thirds (1,800–2,000+ kWh/m²/year) is among Europe's highest, and wind resources are substantial. By end-2025, installed solar PV capacity reached approximately **50 GW** and wind approximately **33 GW**. Spain also holds deposits of critical minerals — notably the **Cáceres lithium deposit** in Extremadura — increasingly relevant to EU strategic autonomy goals. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

## Section 3: Imperial legacy and institutional inheritance

Spain occupies a unique position in the international system: it is not a post-colonial state but a **post-imperial one** — the former colonizer, not the colonized. This distinction shapes its diplomatic self-conception, institutional DNA, and the particular tensions of its territorial structure.

### Layered institutional geology

Spain's institutional architecture resembles geological strata. **Roman civil law** — codified statutes over precedent, inquisitorial court procedure — was transmitted through the Visigothic *Liber Iudiciorum* (654 AD) and remains the legal foundation. Eight centuries of **Al-Andalus** left durable institutional imprints: sophisticated irrigation systems (*acequias*), urban planning models, agricultural innovations, and the politically contested *Convivencia* narrative of interfaith intellectual coexistence.

The critical structural tension, however, derives from the **Habsburg vs. Bourbon administrative traditions**. Under the Habsburgs (1516–1700), Spain operated as a **composite monarchy** — historian J.H. Elliott's term for a system where constituent kingdoms (Castile, Aragon, Catalonia, Valencia, Naples, the Netherlands, American viceroyalties) shared a monarch but retained their own laws, *fueros*, parliaments, and fiscal systems. García Cárcel calls this "horizontal Spain." The Bourbon **Nueva Planta Decrees** (1707–1716), imposed after the War of the Spanish Succession, dismantled Aragonese autonomous institutions, imposed Castilian administrative models, and created "vertical Spain" — centralized around a Castilian axis. **Crucially, the Basque provinces and Navarre retained their fueros for having supported Philip V** — an 18th-century asymmetry with direct modern consequences in the form of the Concierto Económico. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### The fueros as living institutional inheritance

The Basque **Concierto Económico** and Navarrese **Convenio** are not modern political concessions but **direct descendants of medieval privilege systems** that survived centralization, Carlist Wars, Francoist punishment (Franco revoked Guipúzcoa and Vizcaya's conciertos in 1937 for Republican loyalty, while Álava and Navarre retained theirs for backing him), and democratic restoration (the 1978 Constitution's Additional Provision 1 explicitly "protects and respects the historic foral rights"). Today, the three Basque *Diputaciones Forales* collect virtually all taxes and transfer a negotiated *cupo* (€1,682 million in 2023) to Madrid. Per capita public spending in foral communities runs approximately **60% above the common-regime average**. This is not an anomaly but a persistence — institutional inheritance operating across centuries. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Napoleonic rupture and the "Two Spains"

Napoleon's 1808 invasion and the subsequent **Constitution of Cádiz (1812, *La Pepa*)** — among the world's first liberal constitutions — inaugurated the **"Two Spains" division** between liberal and absolutist, progressive and traditionalist. Ferdinand VII's abolition of the Constitution upon his return in 1814 began a pattern of constitutional instability (six constitutions between 1812 and 1876) that would persist through the 20th century. The poet Antonio Machado captured it: *"Españolito que vienes al mundo, te guarde Dios: una de las dos Españas ha de helarte el corazón"* ("Little Spaniard coming into the world, God keep you: one of the two Spains will freeze your heart").

### Francoist institutional residue

Despite democratization, Francoist institutional DNA persists. The military remained politically significant through the Transition (23-F proved this); full civilian supremacy took years to consolidate. Centralist administrative patterns directly trace to both Bourbon precedent and Francoist enforcement. The **Opus Dei technocrats** who engineered the 1959 Stabilization Plan and subsequent development plans believed economic modernization would obviate political reform — a philosophy José Casanova termed "the Opus Dei ethic" — and their institutional imprint persisted in economic policy circles, banking, and education through institutions like the University of Navarre. **National Catholicism** fused Catholic identity with Spanish nationalism; despite rapid secularization, the Church's institutional presence persists through the 1979 Concordat agreements, a vast education network, and the tax-checkbox system generating over **€320 million annually**. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### EU accession as transformative overlay

Spain's **1986 EEC accession** was the most transformative institutional event since the Constitution. It served as democratic consolidation (ending isolation dating to the 1945 Potsdam Declaration), economic transformation (per capita income rose from 68% of EU average to near-parity; per capita GDP from $3,207 in 1986 to ~$35,589 in 2024), and institutional modernization (alignment with the *acquis communautaire*). Spanish officials filled prominent EU positions: Javier Solana as first CFSP High Representative, Josep Borrell as European Parliament president and later EU foreign affairs chief. Spain's self-conception as a "good European" — a regenerationist impulse traceable to the Generation of '98 — is now the dominant frame through which it navigates international order. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

1. The Habsburg-Bourbon administrative tension (composite monarchy vs. centralization) maps directly onto modern center-periphery conflict: the Nueva Planta Decrees (1707–16) created the institutional template for centralism that Catalonia and the Crown of Aragon's successor communities still resist. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]
2. The Basque Concierto Económico is a living medieval institutional inheritance — not a modern political concession — which makes it nearly impossible to revoke and creates permanent fiscal asymmetry within the State of Autonomies. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]
3. EU accession (1986) functions as Spain's second founding: it restructured governance, economy, and identity so thoroughly that withdrawal or serious EU weakening would constitute a regime-level crisis. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

[MODERATE CONFIDENCE]
5. ## Section 4: Ethnic, sectarian, and linguistic cleavage structure

Spain's cleavage structure is fundamentally **center-periphery (territorial)** and **left-right (ideological)** — not ethnic or sectarian. This distinction matters analytically because Spain lacks the confessional divisions or racial-ethnic stratification that define cleavages elsewhere. Instead, the dominant fault lines are the tension between state centralism and peripheral nationalist demands, and the left-right axis concerning economy and society. These two cleavages **cross-cut each other**, producing a complex four-quadrant political space that is structurally determinative of government formation.

### Linguistic diversity as political architecture

The Constitution (Article 3) establishes Castilian as the official state language with co-official status for regional languages in their respective communities. **Catalan** (~17% of Spain's population across Catalonia, Balearics, and parts of Valencia) carries the highest political salience: the policy of *immersió lingüística* has made Catalan the primary vehicular language in public schools since the early 1980s, provoking judicial challenges (Spanish courts ordered at least 25% instruction in Spanish, which the Catalan government resisted). **Basque** (Euskara, ~2%, a pre-Indo-European language isolate) has undergone significant revitalization through *ikastolak* schools. **Galician** (~6%, closely related to Portuguese) carries lower political salience. Language policy is a permanent source of center-periphery friction. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### The Catalan question: from crisis to managed containment

The **October 2017 crisis** — unilateral referendum, independence declaration, Article 155 intervention, imprisonment of nine leaders (9–13 year sentences), Puigdemont's flight to Belgium — represented the most severe constitutional crisis since 23-F. The subsequent trajectory has been one of managed de-escalation: **pardons** (June 2021), the **Amnesty Law** (passed May 2024, upheld by Constitutional Court June 2025 at 6–4), and the **May 2024 Catalan election** that was transformative. The PSC (Socialists) under Salvador Illa won 42 of 135 seats; pro-independence parties collectively **dropped to 61 seats and 43.2% of the vote**, losing their combined majority for the first time in over a decade. Illa became President of the Generalitat on 8 August 2024, ending over a decade of separatist-led government. The Supreme Court, however, refuses to apply the amnesty to Puigdemont on embezzlement grounds; he remains in exile. Analysts characterize the *procés* as politically concluded, though **latent independence sentiment (~40% of the Catalan electorate) endures** as a mobilizable reserve. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Basque nationalism: the graduated model

ETA (founded 1959, ~850 killed) declared definitive cessation in October 2011 and formally dissolved in May 2018. The post-ETA political landscape features a stable dynamic between **PNV** (moderate, governing nationalism) and **EH Bildu** (progressive, independence-leaning, founded 2012). In the April 2024 Basque elections, both tied at 27 seats, but PNV retained government by renewing its coalition with PSE-EE (39 of 75 seats). EH Bildu's extraordinary growth — it now holds the most mayoralties in the Basque Country — reflects its appeal to younger voters with no memory of ETA violence. Crucially, the **Concierto Económico removes the core fiscal grievance that fueled Catalan separatism**, and PNV's gradualist strategy (trading parliamentary support for incremental autonomy) avoids confrontation. There is no Basque equivalent of the *procés*. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### The cross-cutting cleavage trap

The left-right × center-periphery intersection is **structurally determinative of government formation**. PSOE/Sánchez's coalition depends on peripheral nationalist parties (Junts 7, ERC 7, EH Bildu 6, PNV 5, BNG 1) for parliamentary majorities. Each party extracts concessions — amnesty, singular financing, fiscal renegotiation — as the price of support. PP's centralist positioning makes it structurally unable to court these parties, constraining its path to power absent an outright majority. This parliamentary arithmetic means Spain's center-periphery cleavage is inseparable from the mechanics of national government formation — a structural feature, not a temporary alignment. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Immigration as emerging cleavage

Immigration has become Spain's most significant **emerging** cleavage. As of January 2026, the **foreign-born population reached 20.3% of the total** (~49.6 million) — up from 1.6% in 1998. Major communities: Moroccans (969,000 foreign nationals), Colombians (677,000, fastest-growing), Romanians (609,000). Foreigners are heavily concentrated in domestic service (43.5% of sector), agriculture (39.6%), and hospitality (22.4%). An October 2024 survey showed **57% of Spaniards believe there is "too much" immigration**. Vox has been the primary political vehicle for anti-immigration sentiment, though this has not yet translated into sustained electoral gains. The **Canary Islands received a record 46,843 irregular migrants** by sea in 2024. Agricultural labor exploitation in Almería's greenhouses and Huelva's strawberry fields remains a persistent flashpoint. The Catholic Church, meanwhile, continues a trajectory of institutional decline: only ~14% attend mass weekly, ~37% identify as non-believers or atheists, and parish priests' average age is 65.5. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

## Section 5: Demographic tides and generational dynamics

Spain faces one of the developed world's most acute demographic crises. The combination of **near-record-low fertility, rapid population aging, and baby-boomer retirement** creates a fiscal-demographic vise that only sustained immigration and further pension reform can alleviate. The political challenge — constructing consensus for immigration integration, pension adjustment, and a fundamentally changing national composition — will define Spanish governance for decades.

### The fertility collapse and immigration-dependent growth

Spain's **Total Fertility Rate fell to 1.10 children per woman in 2024** — among the very lowest in the EU and worldwide, half the 2.1 replacement level. This collapse from 3.0 in 1964 to 1.10 in six decades is one of the most dramatic fertility declines recorded. The total population reached **49.1 million as of January 2025** and surpassed 49.5 million by January 2026, but this growth is **entirely immigration-driven**. Natural population change has been negative since 2017: in 2024, 318,005 births versus 439,146 deaths produced a **negative natural balance of ~121,000**. Foreign-born mothers accounted for **33.3% of all births** in 2024.

INE projections (2024–2074) are stark: Spain's population would gain ~5 million in 15 years and ~6 million by 2074, but **only through immigration**. The native-born share would decline from 81.9% to **61.0%** within 50 years. Births will remain below deaths throughout the entire projection period. TFR is projected to rise only marginally to 1.24 by 2038. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### The aging structure

Spain's **median age is approximately 46.8 years**, among the EU's highest. The population over 65 reached **20.7%** as of January 2025. The old-age dependency ratio (population 65+ per 100 working-age) stands at 32 and is projected to reach **51.4 by 2050** — essentially 1.2 working-age persons for every person over 65, compared to the current 2.6. Life expectancy projections reach 86.0 years for men and 90.0 for women by 2073. The percentage over 65 would peak at **30.5% around 2055**. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Pension sustainability: the fiscal cliff

Spain's pay-as-you-go pension system faces escalating pressure as baby boomers began retiring in 2023. Current pension spending is approximately **12.7–13.0% of GDP**. The Sánchez government's two-phase reform (2021–2023) repealed the 2013 sustainability factor, returned to CPI-linked indexation, and introduced the Intergenerational Equity Mechanism (an extra contribution rising to 1.2 percentage points by 2029). AIReF's March 2025 projections are sobering: pension spending will rise by **3.4 percentage points of GDP by 2050** (from 12.7% to 16.1–16.3%), peaking around 2049. Without further measures, public debt could reach **129% of GDP by 2050 and 186% by 2070**, with deficits reaching 7% of GDP. The OECD confirmed a "sizeable funding gap persists." Total aging-related public spending is projected to increase by **5.3 percentage points of GDP by 2050** (from 44.7% to 51.0%). [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Generational cohorts and the youth unemployment scar

Four analytically distinct generational cohorts shape Spanish politics. The **Franco-era generation** (born pre-1955, now >70) carries memories of dictatorship and Transition; it is shrinking rapidly. The **Transition generation** (born 1955–1975) benefited from democratization, EU accession, and the economic boom — this is the baby-boom cohort whose retirement will strain the pension system maximally. The **crisis generation** (born 1975–1995) came of age during or after the 2008 crash, experiencing youth unemployment that peaked at **55.5% in 2013**; they are characterized by precarious employment, delayed independence, and deferred family formation. **Gen Z** (born after 1995) faces the housing affordability crisis as its defining material concern.

Youth unemployment has declined to approximately **23.5–25.5%** by early 2026, and the 2021 labor reform produced a **142% increase in permanent contracts** for young workers. But the structural scar persists: youth inactivity (neither working nor seeking work) reached 2.8 million in Q3 2025, the highest in 23 years. The working-age youth population (16–24) is **19.1% smaller** than 30 years ago. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Immigration as demographic lifeline

All credible projections agree that sustained high immigration (**300,000+ net annually**) is essential to maintain the welfare state. The foreign-born population surged from 542,314 (1.4%) in 1996 to 9.8 million (19.8%) by October 2025. Net migration in 2023 was 642,296. The Sánchez government planned to legalize ~900,000 undocumented migrants by 2027. From 2005 to 2024, ~2.7 million foreigners obtained Spanish citizenship through naturalization.

The **"España vaciada"** (Emptied Spain) phenomenon represents the territorial dimension of demographic crisis: Soria has population density below 9 inhabitants/km², and 86% of municipalities with fewer than 1,000 inhabitants have experienced population decline in the last decade. The phenomenon spawned political parties (Teruel Existe, Soria ¡Ya!) but these lost national representation in 2023 amid increased polarization. Research shows depopulated municipalities lean PP; those on the brink of disappearance lean Vox. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

## Section 6: Economic structure, financial architecture, and dependency patterns

Spain's economy — the **Eurozone's fourth-largest at €1,686 billion nominal GDP (2025)** — has outperformed European peers since the pandemic, growing at **2.8% in 2025** (roughly double the Eurozone average). This outperformance masks structural vulnerabilities: tourism dependency, labor market dualism, Eurozone fiscal constraints, persistent informal economy, and corporate exposure to Latin American volatility. The economy is navigating the transition from crisis recovery to structural consolidation under conditions of US commercial unpredictability.

### Services dominance and the tourism trap

Services constitute approximately **68.5% of GVA**, with tourism the most distinctive structural feature. Tourism directly contributed **€200.7 billion (12.6% of GDP)** in 2024, with the broader WTTC methodology (including indirect effects) estimating **15.6% of GDP**. Spain received **96.8 million international tourists in 2025** (record, second globally after France), spending €134.7 billion. Tourism employs approximately **3.0–3.2 million workers** (~14% of total employment). The COVID shock demonstrated catastrophic vulnerability: arrivals plummeted from 83.5 million (2019) to 18.9 million (2020), contributing to Spain's 11.2% GDP contraction — worse than most EU peers. Overtourism has become politically salient, with protests in Barcelona, Mallorca, and the Canary Islands. Manufacturing contributes ~11.5% of GDP; Spain is the EU's **second-largest vehicle producer** (after Germany), with Stellantis, SEAT/Cupra-VW, and Ford plants central to the EV transition challenge. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### The IBEX-35 nexus and state-capital relations

Spain's economic landscape is dominated by a concentrated corporate elite. **Santander** posted record attributable profit of **€14.1 billion** in 2025 (+12%); **BBVA** earned €10.5 billion in recurrent profit. Both banks' Latin American operations constitute a modern economic echo of the historical empire — Santander is massive in Brazil, Mexico, and Chile; BBVA México is one of Mexico's largest banks. This creates a dual-edged sword: growth in dominant market positions alongside currency volatility and political risk exposure.

The state has re-entered the corporate arena. SEPI's acquisition of a **10% Telefónica stake** (responding to Saudi STC's 9.9% purchase) marks the most significant state intervention in a privatized company since the 1990s. The **revolving door** between senior government positions and IBEX 35 boards is well-documented. The Sánchez government's relationship with large corporations has been strained by windfall taxes on banking and energy (2022–2023), the proposed 37.5-hour work week, and progressive labor reforms. CEOE president Garamendi has called government proposals "populism"; Repsol CEO Imaz publicly threatened to "manage alternatives to Spain." However, the IBEX 35's **30% surge in 2025** and triple credit-rating upgrades suggest fundamentals remain sound. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Eurozone constraint and fiscal trajectory

Spain adopted the euro in 1999 as a founding member. The 2008–2014 crisis demonstrated the binding constraint: unable to devalue, Spain endured **internal devaluation** — wage cuts, austerity, unemployment above 26% (2013). The banking crisis centered on politically connected *cajas* (savings banks); the **Bankia bailout** consumed €22.4 billion in public funds. Spain received **€41.3 billion** in EU bank recapitalization (technically a financial-sector, not sovereign, bailout). SAREB ("bad bank," created 2012) absorbed ~€50 billion in toxic assets and had negative net worth of €10.5 billion by end-2020.

The fiscal trajectory has improved markedly. **Debt-to-GDP fell to 101.8%** in 2024 (from ~120% COVID peak), forecast to drop below 100% in 2026. All three major rating agencies upgraded Spain simultaneously in September 2025 — **S&P to A+, Moody's to A3 (two-notch jump), Fitch to A** — the highest ratings since 2012. In a historic first, **investors now demand more compensation to lend to France than to Spain**. The Bono-Bund spread compressed to ~54 bps. Budget deficit was 3.2% of GDP in 2024, projected to decline to 2.5% in 2025. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### NextGenerationEU: the fiscal lifeline

Spain's total NGEU allocation is approximately **€163 billion** (€79.8 billion grants + €83.2 billion loans) — second-largest after Italy. By August 2025, €71 billion had been disbursed (44% of total). The Bank of Spain estimates **45% of companies with NGEU-financed projects would not have undertaken them without the program**, and GDP impact is estimated at 2.6% above the counterfactual in 2024. Key investments target renewable energy, digital transition (Kit Digital for SME digitalization), EV transition (PERTE-VEC gigafactory program), railway infrastructure, and housing renovation. However, the program ends August 2026 with **€36.5 billion in remaining grant execution** needed — significant acceleration required. Impact on productivity has been "less than expected" according to Real Instituto Elcano. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Trade and dependency patterns

The EU accounts for **62% of Spanish exports**. Spain maintained a trade surplus with the EU for 14 consecutive years (€19.8 billion in 2025). Key export surpluses: food/beverages/tobacco (>€18 billion), vehicles. Key import deficits: mineral fuels/oil (-$31.8 billion). The energy trade deficit was ~€30.4 billion in 2024, declining due to renewables growth. The current account maintained a surplus of 2.9% of GDP. The **informal economy** is estimated at 15.8–21.5% of GDP depending on methodology — among the largest in Western Europe.

The 2021 **labor reform** has been transformative: the temporary employment rate plunged from an average of 29.7% (2014–2019) to **12.7%** by end-2024, approaching the EU average. Unemployment fell to **9.93%** in Q4 2025 — first time below 10% since 2008 and the lowest in 18 years. The minimum wage (SMI) has risen **61% since 2018** (from €735.90 to €1,184/month in 2025, €1,221 in 2026). [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

## Section 7: Infrastructural inheritance and resource pathways

Spain's infrastructure reflects both the radial centralizing logic of the Meseta and the transformative impact of EU funding since 1986. The country possesses Europe's longest high-speed rail network, its largest LNG regasification capacity, and a rapidly expanding renewable energy base — but these assets are structurally bottlenecked by the France-Spain interconnection deficit, which leaves the Iberian Peninsula functioning as an **energy island** within Europe.

### Transport: the radial inheritance and its correction

The **AVE high-speed rail network** (~3,900 km, Europe's longest, second globally after China) embodies Madrid-centric radial design: all major lines emanate from the capital. Key corridors include Madrid-Seville (471 km, 1992, Spain's first), Madrid-Barcelona (621 km, 2008, ~2.5 hours), and Madrid-Valencia. The network carried a record **39 million passengers in 2024**, and construction costs averaged **€17.7 million per km** — significantly below the European average of €45.5 million. Spain received almost half of all EU funds earmarked for high-speed rail between 2000 and 2017. However, ridership density remains low by international standards (~12,000 passengers per km versus 61,400 for France, 158,121 for Japan), reflecting criticism that the network was overbuilt in low-population areas for political rather than economic reasons.

The **Mediterranean Corridor** rail project represents the most strategically significant correction to the radial inheritance — an EU TEN-T priority providing standard-gauge freight along Spain's eastern coast from the French border to Algeciras. After decades of campaigning by business groups under the "Quiero Corredor" banner, progress has accelerated: **624 km completed (36% of the Spanish segment)**, 83% under construction as of November 2025. The La Sagrera intermodal station in Barcelona (€1 billion investment) is expected to open by end-2026.

Spanish ports handled approximately **557.7 million tonnes** in 2024. **Valencia** (5.5 million TEUs, Spain's #1 container port, EU's 4th), **Algeciras** (4.7 million TEUs, EU's 5th, premier transshipment hub), and **Barcelona** (3.9 million TEUs, surging 31.9% in 2024 due to Red Sea disruptions) rank among Europe's top ten. **AENA**, majority state-owned, is the world's largest airport operator by passenger volume (369 million passengers across 46 airports in 2024, record profits of €1.93 billion). A **€13 billion airport investment programme** (2027–2036) will expand Madrid-Barajas to 90 million passengers and Barcelona-El Prat to 72 million. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Energy: LNG fortress, renewable surge, nuclear phase-out

Spain maintains **Europe's largest LNG regasification capacity** — approximately **60 bcm/year** across six operational terminals (Barcelona, Cartagena, Huelva, Bilbao, Mugardos, Sagunto), representing roughly 27% of all European capacity. Average utilization was only 29% in H1 2025, reflecting massive overcapacity that nonetheless provides security-of-supply insurance. During the 2022 European energy crisis, this infrastructure proved invaluable: Spain exported gas to France at record levels. The **Medgaz pipeline** (757 km from Algeria to Almería, 10.5 bcm/year capacity) is now Spain's sole pipeline to Algeria after the Maghreb-Europe pipeline through Morocco ceased operations in October 2021. Russian LNG still accounted for **21% of supply** — a politically sensitive dependency.

Spain's **renewable energy transformation** is among Europe's most dramatic. By end-2025: solar PV ~50.2 GW installed (8.85 GW added in 2025 alone), wind ~33.3 GW, total renewable capacity ~93 GW (**68.6% of total installed capacity**). Renewables generated **64% of Spain's electricity** in 2024. On 16 April 2025, Spain achieved a historic milestone: **100% of peninsular electricity demand met by renewables for an entire weekday**. However, grid saturation is the binding constraint: **83.4% of grid connection nodes are saturated**; of 40 GW of access requested in 2025, only 4.5 GW was approved. Seven nuclear reactors (7.1 GWe) generate ~20% of electricity; the planned phase-out (2027–2035) remains politically contested after Congress approved a PP proposal to reverse it in February 2025. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### The energy island problem

The **France-Spain electricity interconnection** — just **2.8 GW**, representing 2–3% of installed capacity — is the most severe energy bottleneck in Western Europe. It was congested 68% of the time in 2024. This isolation was catastrophically exposed by the **unprecedented blackout of 28 April 2025**, which cut power across Spain, Portugal, and parts of southern France. The **Bay of Biscay submarine interconnection** (under construction, expected 2027–2028) will nearly double capacity to 5 GW. Two additional Pyrenean crossings are planned (Navarra-Landes by 2029, Aragón-Marsillon by 2030), which would raise total capacity to approximately 8 GW — still below the EU's 15% target.

The **H2Med project** — replacing the abandoned MidCat gas pipeline — will create Europe's first dedicated hydrogen corridor. The BarMar segment (~450 km submarine pipeline from Barcelona to Marseille) and CelZa segment (Portugal to Spain) secured EU Project of Common Interest status. The BarMar company was formally established in July 2025 as a joint venture between Enagás, NaTran, and Teréga. Commercial operation is targeted for **2032**, aiming to transport **2 million tonnes of green hydrogen per year** — approximately 10% of Europe's projected hydrogen consumption. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

### Digital infrastructure and water systems

Spain has emerged as a major European submarine cable hub, with landing points at Bilbao, Barcelona, Valencia, and the Canary Islands. Madrid is a leading Southern European data center market, driven by renewable energy supply and strategic positioning as a gateway to Latin America and Africa. AENA is constructing standard-gauge rail access to Madrid-Barajas Terminal 4 with NGEU funding.

Spain's water infrastructure network — approximately **1,200 reservoirs with ~56,000 hm³ combined capacity**, extensive inter-basin transfers (the politically contentious Tagus-Segura system carries up to 600 hm³/year), and desalination plants (~5 million m³/day capacity) — faces accelerating climate stress. The 2023–2024 Catalan drought emergency forced Barcelona's desalination plants to maximum capacity, and new plants with 2029 commissioning targets are planned.

EU structural and cohesion funds since 1986 financed the bulk of Spain's highway network, high-speed rail, water infrastructure, and port modernization. Under NextGenerationEU, Spain channels 37% for green transition and 33% for digitalization. The Bank of Spain estimates **45% of NGEU-funded projects would not have proceeded without the program**. [HIGH CONFIDENCE]

## SECTION 8: ENVIRONMENTAL & CLIMATIC STRUCTURE

### Spain faces the most severe water crisis in modern European history

**Spain's multi-year drought (2022–2025) exposed structural vulnerabilities in a country where 74% of territory faces desertification risk and agriculture consumes 80% of available water.** The crisis triggered political confrontation over inter-basin transfers, accelerated a massive desalination build-out, and culminated in the deadliest flood disaster in Spain's modern history — the October 2024 Valencia DANA — which killed over 220 people and caused an estimated €16–50 billion in damage. Climate projections suggest the Iberian Peninsula will be Europe's hardest-hit region, with precipitation declining **20–40%** and semi-arid conditions becoming the country's dominant climate type by 2050.

Spain's water infrastructure was designed for a wetter country. Average water availability has already decreased **12% since 1980**, and projections indicate a further **14–40% decline by 2050**. The drought that began in late 2022 was the longest since AEMET records began in 1961, with Catalonia experiencing its worst three consecutive dry years since 1914. The Sau Reservoir supplying Barcelona dropped to **1% of capacity** in March 2024. By mid-2025, southern basins remained critically low — Guadalquivir at **25.6%**, Guadiana at **34.7%**, Segura at **35.8%** — while northern basins like Duero (74%) and Miño-Sil (78.5%) fared better. Heavy rains in March 2025 broke the Catalan drought but did little for the south.

The claim that 2024 was Spain's lowest-rainfall year in 150 years requires qualification. National average precipitation actually rose to **663 mm in 2024** from 527 mm in 2023, though the Canary Islands recorded their driest year since 1961 (only 139 liters). The statistic likely refers to specific regions or hydrological-year measurements rather than the national calendar-year average. What is unambiguous is that **20% of mainland Spain is already desertified** and arid climates have doubled since the mid-twentieth century, expanding at roughly **1,500 km² per year**.

### The Tagus-Segura transfer is dying — and taking southeast Spain's economy with it

The Tagus-Segura inter-basin transfer, operational since 1979 via a 286-km canal, is Spain's most politically explosive water infrastructure. Designed to move up to 600 hm³/year from central Spain to the arid southeast, actual transfers averaged 328 hm³/year. A **May 2025 Supreme Court ruling** declared that deferring ecological flow enforcement in the Tagus violated environmental law, particularly in Natura 2000 protected areas. Castilla-La Mancha's president Emiliano García-Page declared the transfer "history in its current form," while Murcia, Valencia, and Andalusia — where the transfer sustains **€2.4 billion in agricultural GDP** — fiercely resisted. Climate studies project **70–79% reduction** in transferable flows, making transfers physically impossible by 2070 and threatening annual economic losses of €380–425 million in the Segura basin.

Spain compensates partly through desalination. It is **Europe's largest desalinated water producer** and the world's fourth or fifth largest, operating **765 plants** with a combined daily output of approximately 5 million m³. The Torrevieja plant (Alicante), Spain's largest, is being expanded from 80 to 120 hm³/year. Barcelona's El Prat plant, Europe's largest for human consumption, went from supplying **3% of drinking water in 2021 to 33% in 2023**. New plants near Barcelona (Tordera and Foix) will add 220,000 m³/day by 2028–2029, backed by €467 million in investment.

### Climate projections paint an alarming picture for the peninsula

Average temperatures rose **1.5°C between 1965 and 2015**. Heatwave frequency has doubled since 2010. June 2025 was the hottest June on record, with temperatures exceeding 46°C in Andalusia. The August 2025 heatwave was the most intense ever measured — 16 days with a **+4.6°C anomaly** above the norm. Projections under high-emission scenarios indicate **20–40% annual precipitation reduction** by century's end, reaching **80% in summer**, with temperatures rising 2.5–5°C in mountain areas.

Sea levels around Spain have risen **9 cm since 1993**. By 2100, projections range from +58 cm (Alicante) to +81 cm (Santa Cruz de Tenerife). Cádiz faces the prospect of being cut off from the mainland. Barcelona's beaches lose an estimated **6–10 meters of sand per year**. The Ebro Delta, Doñana wetlands, and Llobregat Delta face existential threats.

The 2025 wildfire season was catastrophic — the worst in over 30 years, with approximately **400,000 hectares** burned by late August, six times the 2006–2024 average. Ourense province alone lost 150,000 hectares — one-seventh of its total surface. Record CO₂ emissions from fires reached **17.68 million tonnes**, exceeding any previous full-year total since 2003. At least 8 people died, 36,000 were evacuated, and 37 individuals were detained for suspected arson.

### Agriculture's 80% water share collides with climate reality

Agriculture consumes approximately **80–85% of Spain's total water** while contributing only **2.3% of GDP** — a disproportion at the heart of the crisis. Nearly 4 million hectares are under irrigation, among the highest in Europe. Spain's position as the world's largest olive oil producer (45–50% of global production) is directly threatened. The 2022/23 harvest collapsed to **663,000 tonnes** — half the historical average — sending extra-virgin prices to record peaks. The 2024/25 harvest recovered to approximately **1.4 million tonnes**, but farmers warn that two more drought years could "kill olive tree plantations in the south."

Almería's "sea of plastic" — **31,600–40,000+ hectares** of greenhouses producing one-third of Europe's winter vegetables — requires 800–1,000 mm of water annually in a region receiving only 200 mm of rain. Over 80% of agricultural water comes from groundwater now suffering salinization and nitrate contamination.

### Transboundary water disputes with Portugal intensify

Spain and Portugal share five major river basins governed by the 1998 Albufeira Convention. The convention's institutional weaknesses are severe: the UNECE found no integrated basin management plans, no functioning secretariat, no public participation mechanisms, and a website unupdated since 2020. In September 2024, environment ministers signed new agreements at the convention's 25th anniversary, including Spain agreeing to pay Portugal **€2 million annually** for water abstracted from the Alqueva reservoir. Portuguese civic movements have demanded cessation of what they describe as twenty years of illegal pumping by Spanish farmers. Water pollution compounds tensions — Tagus waters flowing to Portugal are "highly contaminated," and in 2023, **161 villages** in Salamanca and Zamora lost water supply due to pesticide contamination.

### The October 2024 Valencia DANA exposed institutional failure

On October 29, 2024, a DANA (cut-off low-pressure system) dropped **500+ mm in 8 hours** on parts of Valencia. Turís recorded **772 mm in 24 hours** — the second-highest daily total in Spain's history. Between **223 and 237 people** were killed, most in Valencia's Horta Sud area, particularly Paiporta (~40 deaths). Nearly **1 million people** were impacted and **15,000 displaced**.

Institutional failures compounded the disaster. The PP-led Valencian government of **Carlos Mazón** had disbanded the Valencian Emergencies Unit established after 2019 floods, dismissing its €9 million budget as a "superfluous expense." Critical delays occurred between AEMET's meteorological warnings and civil protection alerts to citizens. Political consequences were severe: crowds threw mud at **King Felipe VI and Queen Letizia** during their visit to Paiporta. The Sánchez government approved **€10.6 billion** in fiscal aid, and Labour Minister **Yolanda Díaz** introduced "paid climate leave" of up to four days during weather emergencies. A Nature Communications study confirmed human-caused climate change intensified the flooding.

### Emissions decline as renewables surge, but transport lags

Spain's total GHG emissions fell to **279.9 MtCO₂-eq in 2024**, a net reduction of **39.3% from 2005** — above the EU average of 30.5%. The electricity sector drove most gains, cutting emissions 57% between 2008 and 2020 as **renewables reached 56% of net electricity generation in 2024**, making Spain the EU leader in solar share at 21%. Including nuclear, **80% of power generation was emission-free**. Transport (32.5% of emissions) remains the stubborn laggard — EV adoption stands at only **11.4%** of new car sales, below the EU average.

The EU Just Transition Fund allocated **€869 million** to Spain for coal-region adjustment. Coal employment collapsed from **45,000 in 1990** to fewer than **300 by 2023**, with only one mine remaining in Asturias. Spain is on track to phase out coal power ahead of its 2030 target. Specific transition projects include AI innovation hubs in former mining areas and offshore renewable energy development in A Coruña.

## SECTION 9: ILLICIT NETWORKS & SHADOW GOVERNANCE

### Spain has reclaimed its position as Europe's primary cocaine gateway

**In 2023, Spanish authorities seized a historic 142 tonnes of cocaine — surpassing Belgium (121 tonnes) and the Netherlands (60 tonnes) — confirming Spain's status as Europe's principal drug entry point.** Organized crime generates an estimated €139 billion in illicit proceeds annually across the EU, with authorities recovering only 2.1%. Spain ranks **fifth out of 44 European countries** on the 2023 Global Organized Crime Index, behind Serbia, Italy, Ukraine, and Russia. The country's Transparency International CPI score has dropped from 65 (2012) to **55 (2025)**, reflecting not only drug trafficking but endemic corruption scandals that have engulfed both major parties.

### Cocaine flows through Algeciras and Galicia on an industrial scale

Algeciras, Spain's busiest port and Europe's seventh-largest, is the epicenter. In October 2024, authorities made Spain's **largest-ever single cocaine seizure — 13 tonnes** concealed in bananas from Ecuador. In August 2023, Operation Nano netted 9.5 tonnes involving **30 different criminal organizations**. Increased security at Antwerp and Rotterdam has redirected trafficking through Spanish ports. Between January 2019 and June 2024, Spanish seaports accounted for at least **388 tonnes** of seized cocaine.

Galicia's role has evolved from the ostentatious 1980s-era clans — Sito Miñanco, Laureano Oubiña, the Charlín family — to more discreet networks offering maritime logistics services. The infrastructure remains potent: in December 2023, Operation Nazare dismantled a Galician clan seizing 2+ tonnes; in August 2023, **Manuel Charlín** (85 years old, the Netflix *Cocaine Coast* figure) was arrested off the Azores with 2.4 tonnes; in January 2025, a speedboat carrying 1+ tonne was intercepted at Vilanova de Arousa. Between 2023 and 2025, **21 cocaine laboratories** were dismantled across Spain, nine in Galicia, including Europe's largest lab in Pontevedra capable of producing 200 kg per day. Colombian chemists, Mexican cartel financing (Sinaloa, CJNG), and Albanian distribution networks operate through Spanish territory.

Cannabis and hashish flow from Morocco through the Strait of Gibraltar. The Campo de Gibraltar area recorded **150,802 kg of hashish** and **44,206 kg of cocaine** seized in 2024 across 12,068 operations (up 43% from 2023). In February 2024, two Guardia Civil officers were killed at Barbate port when a narco-launch deliberately rammed their patrol boat. Over **20 deaths** from drug trafficking in the Strait have occurred since 2016. La Línea de la Concepción's unemployment exceeds **30%**, creating fertile ground for recruitment. The area's Special Security Plan, launched in 2018, allocated **€200 million** for 2024–2025.

### Corruption is structural in Spain's political economy, not merely peripheral

Both major parties have been consumed by corruption scandals spanning decades. The **Gürtel case** revealed that the PP maintained parallel bookkeeping ("Caja B") **from 1982 to 2009**, funded by illegal construction-company donations. Party treasurer **Luis Bárcenas** was sentenced to 33 years and fined €44 million, having held **€48 million in Swiss bank accounts**. Mastermind Francisco Correa received 51 years. The scandal led to PM Rajoy's ouster via Spain's first-ever successful no-confidence vote in June 2018.

The **ERE case** — Spain's largest corruption case by monetary scope — found that PSOE Andalusian governments diverted **€680 million** in public unemployment funds to party-aligned individuals between 2001 and 2010. Former presidents **José Antonio Griñán** (6 years prison) and **Manuel Chaves** (9-year political ban) were among 21 convicted officials. Spain's Constitutional Court later overturned several convictions on procedural grounds in 2024, generating fierce political controversy.

The **Koldo case** (ongoing since February 2024) is the most significant active scandal, centering on fraudulent COVID-19 mask contracts worth **€53 million**. Former Transport Minister **José Luis Ábalos** was indicted in October 2024; PSOE organization secretary **Santos Cerdán** resigned after leaked recordings. Businessman **Víctor de Aldama** testified he paid bribes to multiple officials, including Ábalos (€400,000). October 2025 police reports revealed Ábalos received cash in PSOE-branded envelopes, with codewords: €500 notes called "txistorras," €200 notes "suns," €100 notes "lettuces." Connected threads include the Begoña Gómez influence-peddling investigation (PM Sánchez's wife), the prosecution of Attorney General **Álvaro García Ortiz** for leaking confidential information, and scrutiny of the €1.1 billion Globalia/Air Europa bailout.

### Money laundering thrives through real estate on the Costa del Sol

Spain's property market — particularly Marbella, Barcelona, and the Balearics — remains highly vulnerable to money laundering. Russian organized crime has been entrenched since the 1990s. Operation Troika (2008), Judge Garzón's massive raid, collapsed in 2018 when charges against 17 accused couldn't be proven. In July 2024, **Dmitry Artyakov** (son of a senior Rostec executive) was arrested in Girona with €200,000+ in cash. In 2025, police raided Mallorca villas linked to sanctioned Russian defense figure **Nikolai Kolesov**. Operation White Lion (2024) dismantled what authorities called "the largest underground bank serving organized crime in Europe," with 80+ arrests and €100+ million in frozen assets.

**SEPBLAC**, Spain's financial intelligence unit, receives generally favorable FATF assessments — Spain is compliant or largely compliant on **38 of 40 FATF Recommendations**. However, Spain still lacks a national anti-corruption strategy, a dedicated independent anti-corruption authority, and robust conflicts-of-interest legislation. The revolving door between politics and business is systemic, particularly in energy, telecoms, construction, and banking — research by ESADE found that two-thirds of Spain's politically connected firms employ former public servants on their boards. The **King Juan Carlos I scandal** — involving an alleged $100 million Saudi "gift," €65 million transferred to a former lover, and subsequent exile to Abu Dhabi since August 2020 — epitomizes elite impunity, as prosecutors shelved all investigations citing statute of limitations and constitutional immunity.

## SECTION 10: INFORMATION ECOSYSTEM & MEDIA STRUCTURE

### Spain's media system exemplifies the Polarized Pluralist model — and is becoming more fragmented

**Spain's media landscape features high political parallelism, increasing ownership concentration by international financial actors, declining public trust (only 33% of Spaniards trust news), and a digital transformation that has empowered both investigative journalism and disinformation.** The country's media system closely fits the Hallin & Mancini Mediterranean/Polarized Pluralist model: outlets have clear but often unacknowledged ideological alignments, state intervention shapes public broadcasting governance, professional self-regulation is weak, and journalists routinely cross into politics. Spain ranks **23rd globally** on the 2025 RSF Press Freedom Index (score: 77.35), up from 30th in 2024, with its weakest sub-indicator being economic freedom (rank 34).

The print/digital landscape divides sharply along ideological lines. **El País** (PRISA Group; majority shareholder Joseph Oughourlian of Amber Capital at ~29.6%, Vivendi SE at ~11.9%) remains Spain's largest newspaper with **400,000+ digital subscribers** and a center-left alignment historically linked to the PSOE. **El Mundo** (Unidad Editorial/RCS MediaGroup, Italian-owned) occupies center-right territory with 163,000 digital subscribers. **ABC** (Vocento) is conservative and monarchist. **La Razón** (Grupo Planeta) aligns closely with PP; its editor **Francisco Marhuenda** previously served as chief of staff in a PP government — emblematic of Spain's political parallelism. **La Vanguardia** (Grupo Godó, Barcelona) offers a centrist Catalan perspective with 145,000 digital subscribers and is one of only two major outlets edited outside Madrid.

Digital-native outlets have reshaped the ecosystem. **elDiario.es**, founded in 2012 by Ignacio Escolar with roughly 12 staff, now employs 92 journalists across all 17 autonomous communities, reaches **17 million+ monthly unique visitors**, and sustains itself through **106,000+ paying members** averaging €71.50/year. Revenue hit €15.5 million in 2024 with €1.53 million profit — a rare success story for membership-funded journalism. On the right, **OKDiario** (founded 2015 by Eduardo Inda) has been rated Spain's worst-valued news outlet by the University of Valencia and carries a "Low" factual reporting rating from Media Bias/Fact Check. **El Español** (founded by former El Mundo editor Pedro J. Ramírez) and **El Confidencial** compete as center-right digital outlets with strong readership.

### Television audiences fragment as Atresmedia dominates and RTVE politicization deepens

**Antena 3** (Atresmedia/Grupo Planeta) led television ratings for a fourth consecutive year in 2025 with **12.8% share**, and is the most trusted outlet in Spain (64% trust per Pew) — a European anomaly where a private broadcaster beats the public one. **Telecinco** (Mediaset España/MFE-MediaForEurope, Berlusconi family heirs) hit a historic low of **9.5%**. RTVE's La 1 recovered to **11.0%**, its best since 2012, partly due to the acquisition of David Broncano's late-night show for €12 million/year. Combined, Atresmedia (26.1%) and Mediaset (24.5%) hold roughly half the television market, with RTVE at 16.5%.

RTVE's governance remains deeply contested. A Royal Decree-Law in October 2024 expanded the board from 10 to 15 members and lowered the appointment threshold from a two-thirds majority to a simple absolute majority — a change press freedom organizations including the International Press Institute condemned as enabling partisan control. **José Pablo López** was appointed president in December 2024. The State Media Monitor 2025 found that **8 of 15 regional public broadcasters** are now classified as "State Controlled," up from just 1 in 2022, with most takeovers by PP-led regional administrations. RTVE has operated without commercial advertising since 2010, relying on state budget subsidies and levies on private broadcasters, leaving it perpetually vulnerable to political pressure.

### Social media reshapes political discourse as disinformation surges

**WhatsApp** dominates with **91%+ penetration** among internet users, serving as a primary news distribution channel. **TikTok** was the "big winner of 2024" with 66% growth in views, but also a critical disinformation vector — Maldita.es identified **129 hoax videos** with 10,000+ views during the Valencia floods alone. **X/Twitter** is declining but remains important for polarized political discourse, with the most viral flood disinformation coming from monetized (paid-badge) accounts. **Telegram** has become a significant political communication channel; far-right MEP **Alvise Pérez** built his following through it. In February 2026, Telegram founder Pavel Durov sent a mass message to all Spanish users attacking government plans for social media regulation.

The October 2024 Valencia DANA was a watershed disinformation event. **Maldita.es identified 112+ distinct disinformation narratives**, including HAARP weather-manipulation conspiracies, false claims that the Sánchez government had demolished dams, accusations that Morocco orchestrated the floods, and fake emergency numbers used for scams. Russian amplification was confirmed, with initial distribution traced to Pravda channels. Platform responses were inadequate: no visible action on 75% of YouTube disinformation and 70% on X. Spain's fact-checking ecosystem — led by **Maldita.es** (IFCN-accredited), **Newtral** (founded by journalist Ana Pastor), **IBERIFIER**, and **EFE Verifica** — was overwhelmed by the volume.

### Regional media ecosystems in Catalonia and the Basque Country operate as distinct information spheres

**TV3** (Corporació Catalana de Mitjans Audiovisuals) has been the most-watched channel in Catalonia for **16 consecutive years** with ~14.2% share, ahead of all national channels. Its 3Cat digital platform has 1.6 million registered users. Catalan-language digital outlets — **VilaWeb**, **Ara**, **El Nacional**, **NacióDigital** — create a distinct information sphere largely invisible to Spanish-language audiences. Catalonia is one of only three regions with an independent audiovisual authority (CAC) and the only one with a quantitative limit on media ownership concentration (50% cap).

In the Basque Country, **EITB** (Euskal Irrati Telebista) operates ETB 1 (Basque-language) and ETB 2 (Spanish), reaching 300,000+ daily radio listeners. **Berria** serves as the daily of record for the Basque-language ecosystem. Both regional systems capture higher audience loyalty than national channels within their territories.

## SECTION 11: INTERNATIONAL INSTITUTIONAL COMMITMENTS

### Spain punches below its weight in NATO but leveraged its EU presidency to shape the Union's agenda

**Spain — the EU's fourth-largest member by population with 61 MEPs and approximately 10.5% of EU population — combines significant institutional weight with chronic underinvestment in defense, a tension that reached crisis point in March 2026 when it denied US forces access to jointly operated military bases during strikes on Iran.** Spain belatedly reached the NATO 2% GDP target in 2025 after a 43% year-over-year spending increase, only to become the sole NATO member refusing the new 5% GDP target adopted at The Hague Summit. Its July–December 2023 EU Council presidency achieved landmark results including the opening of Ukraine/Moldova accession talks, the Migration and Asylum Pact, the AI Act agreement, and Bulgaria/Romania's Schengen integration.

Within the EU, Spain supports extending qualified majority voting to foreign and security policy, as a member of the "Group of Friends on QMV." It joined the Atlantic Group (with Portugal, Ireland, Belgium, Netherlands, Denmark) conditioning eastward enlargement on institutional and budgetary reform. Spain's public debt at **~101.8% of GDP** in 2024 exceeds the 60% SGP reference value, though it has benefited from the reformed EU fiscal framework that replaced rigid numerical targets with country-specific adjustment paths. Spain's Recovery and Resilience Plan totals **€165.5 billion** (€80.9 billion in grants, €83.7 billion in loans), with 40% allocated to green transition.

### NATO membership has been politically fraught from day one

Spain joined NATO on May 30, 1982 under PM Calvo-Sotelo. The PSOE under Felipe González had campaigned "No to NATO" but reversed course in power, holding a referendum on March 12, 1986 that narrowly carried **52.5% to 39.8%** (turnout: 59.4%) under conditions: no integration into NATO's military command structure, no nuclear weapons on Spanish soil, and gradual reduction of US military presence. Spain remained outside the integrated command until 1997 under PM Aznar.

Defense spending languished for over a decade — 2022 spending matched 2008 levels in real terms. At the 2024 Washington Summit, Spain spent only **~1.28% of GDP**. PM Sánchez announced in April 2025 an Industrial and Technological Plan for Security and Defence adding ~€10.5 billion, and by end-2025 NATO confirmed Spain had reached **2% of GDP** (approximately €33.1 billion). However, at the June 2025 Hague Summit, when NATO adopted a **5% GDP target by 2035** (3.5% core defense, 1.5% broader security), Spain was the only member to refuse. Sánchez called the commitment "unreasonable and counterproductive" in a letter to Secretary General Rutte. In October 2025, Trump suggested NATO should consider Spain's expulsion.

### The March 2026 base access denial marks the worst US-Spain crisis in decades

When the US and Israel commenced missile strikes against Iran on February 28, 2026, Sánchez refused to allow US forces to use Rota and Morón for operations. On March 2, Foreign Minister **José Manuel Albares** formalized the denial. Trump responded on March 3 by threatening to "cut off all trade with Spain" and making what many interpreted as a sovereignty threat: "We could use their base if we want. We could just fly in and use it. Nobody's going to tell us not to use it." The White House claimed on March 4 that Spain had agreed to cooperate; Spain "categorically" denied this. Defense Minister **Margarita Robles** stated: "No single country should act as a guardian of the world." US aircraft repositioned from Morón to Ramstein. As of early March 2026, the crisis remained unresolved.

**Rota Naval Station** hosts four (expanding to six) US Navy Aegis BMD-capable Arleigh Burke-class destroyers — core to NATO's ballistic missile defense against Iran and Russia. The fifth destroyer (USS Oscar Austin) arrived in fall 2024; the sixth is due in 2026. **Morón Air Base** hosts the SPMAGTF-CR-AF with capacity for up to **3,500 Marines and 35 aircraft**. Both bases operate under Spanish sovereignty via the 1988 Agreement on Defense Cooperation, which requires mutual consultation for expansions — a legal framework Spain invoked to deny access.

### Spain's foreign policy balances Western alignment with Global South engagement

Spain recognized Palestinian statehood on **May 28, 2024**, alongside Ireland and Norway, based on pre-1967 borders with East Jerusalem as capital. Israel recalled its ambassador. In March 2022, Sánchez reversed Spain's decades-long Western Sahara position, recognizing Morocco's autonomy plan as "the most serious, realistic and credible basis" for resolution — ending a diplomatic crisis triggered by Morocco's weaponization of migration (10,000+ migrants allowed into Ceuta in May 2021).

Spain participates in **44 PESCO projects** — third-most of any EU member after France (52) and Italy (51). The Ibero-American system, with **SEGIB** headquartered in Madrid and Spain as its principal architect, provides unique European access to Latin America. The Union for the Mediterranean secretariat in Barcelona reflects Spain's central Mediterranean role. Spain has served on the UN Security Council five times, most recently **2015–2016**, and has accepted compulsory ICJ jurisdiction with standard reservations.

## SECTION 12: MILITARY & SECURITY SECTOR DNA

### Spain's military is thoroughly depoliticized but chronically under-resourced

**Spain's Armed Forces — approximately 120,000–122,000 active personnel across Army, Navy, and Air and Space Force — underwent complete depoliticization after the failed 23-F coup of 1981 and present zero coup risk today.** The professional military has struggled with chronic recruitment shortfalls since conscription was abolished in 2001, maintains a reserve force of only **3,000–5,000** (experts recommend 20,000–50,000), and deployed approximately **3,000–4,000 personnel** across 16 missions on four continents as of 2025. Defense Minister **Margarita Robles** oversees a force led by JEMAD Admiral General **Teodoro E. López Calderón**.

The post-Franco transition fundamentally reshaped civil-military relations. The 1978 Constitution placed armed forces under civilian authority. The February 23, 1981 coup attempt — when Lieutenant Colonel **Antonio Tejero** stormed the Congress of Deputies and King Juan Carlos I ordered troops to their barracks on television — became the defining moment of Spain's democratic consolidation. NATO integration from 1982 onward served as a powerful civilianizing force, exposing Spanish officers to allied democratic norms. The March 2026 base crisis demonstrated that military leadership follows government directives without hesitation, even on politically fraught decisions.

### Major procurement programs face mixed fortunes

The **F-110 Bonifaz-class frigate program** represents Spain's largest current naval procurement: €4.325 billion for five frigates, the first ships globally to carry the AN/SPY-7(V)2 radar integrated with the Aegis combat system. The lead ship F111 Bonifaz was launched September 2025 (over 70% complete), with three frigates now under simultaneous construction at Navantia's Ferrol yard. Deliveries are scheduled one per year from 2028 through 2032.

The **S-80 Isaac Peral-class submarine program** became synonymous with procurement dysfunction — original delivery dates of 2011–2018 slipped by over a decade after excessive weight issues required General Dynamics Electric Boat assistance and hull lengthening from 71m to 80.8m. Costs escalated from the original €1.756 billion to approximately **€4 billion** (€977 million per unit). S-81 Isaac Peral was finally commissioned November 2023 and made its first NATO deployment in October 2025. S-82 Narciso Monturiol was launched October 2025, with S-83 and S-84 expected in 2028 and 2029.

Spain operates Eurofighter Typhoons across multiple wings, with **Project Halcón** acquiring 20 additional aircraft with AESA radar for €906 million. The **Future Combat Air System (FCAS)** — a trilateral program with France (Dassault) and Germany (Airbus) estimated at ~€100 billion — faces existential Franco-German industrial disputes. In October 2025, German Defense Minister Pistorius publicly threatened to end the program. Spain, through **Indra** (sensors, >€600 million share) and **Airbus Spain** (low-observable technologies), has committed to FCAS and ruled out the F-35. By December 2025, France and Spain reaffirmed commitment, but Germany remained silent.

In December 2025, the government approved **31 Special Modernization Programs (PEMs)**, including SDR radios (€1.421 billion via Indra), 23 NH-90 helicopters (€1.3 billion), Santiago II electronic warfare systems (€373 million), MH-60R naval helicopters, Pizarro IFV modernization (€263 million), C-295 transport aircraft (€1.209 billion), and 30 Hürjet trainers from Turkish Aerospace Industries.

### The defense industry anchors Spain's strategic autonomy ambitions

**Navantia**, the state-owned shipbuilder, operates yards at Ferrol (frigates), San Fernando/Puerto Real (corvettes), and Cartagena (submarines). Its **Alsarawat program** — five Avante 2200 corvettes for Saudi Arabia at €1.8 billion — was the largest export contract in Spanish shipyard history. Navantia's Hobart-class program for Australia and Fridtjof Nansen-class frigates for Norway established its international reputation. **Airbus Spain** (Getafe) is critical to A400M and Eurofighter production. **Indra Sistemas**, Spain's leading defense electronics company, was the largest beneficiary of the 2025 PEMs. **GDELS-Santa Bárbara Sistemas** (General Dynamics subsidiary) produces Leopard 2E tanks and Pizarro IFVs. Spain was the world's **seventh-largest arms exporter** in the 2015–2019 SIPRI period and Saudi Arabia's second-largest supplier (10% of Saudi imports) in 2020–2024.

### Overseas deployments span four continents with UNIFIL as the flagship

Spain leads the Multinational Brigade East in **UNIFIL Lebanon** with **~660–700 troops** at the "Miguel de Cervantes" base — the third-largest European contributor. In the NATO eastern flank, approximately **600 troops** serve in Latvia's Forward Land Forces, with 128+ personnel and 8 Eurofighters conducting Baltic Air Policing from Šiauliai. A **Patriot battery** deployed to Turkey under NATO Active Fence provided data enabling US forces to intercept an Iranian ballistic missile during the March 2026 crisis — demonstrating that Spain didn't completely disengage from allied operations even while denying base access. Additional deployments include approximately 400 troops in Iraq, support to EU operations in Somalia (EUNAVFOR Atalanta, commanded from Rota), and training missions in Mali and the Central African Republic.

### Intelligence and law enforcement face accountability deficits

The **CNI** (Centro Nacional de Inteligencia), directed by **Esperanza Casteleiro** since May 2022, operates under a 1968 Official Secrets Act that classifies all CNI information as secret — widely criticized as inadequate. The **Pegasus scandal** revealed two strands: CNI legally surveilled at least 18 of 63+ targeted Catalan/Basque independence figures (2017–2020), while PM Sánchez's own phone and those of Ministers Robles and Grande-Marlaska were compromised by an apparent foreign state actor. On **January 23, 2026**, Audiencia Nacional Judge **José Luis Calama** shelved the investigation for the second time, citing Israel's refusal to cooperate on five requests for judicial assistance sent between May 2022 and February 2025. Separately, a Barcelona court in March 2025 ordered the first-ever indictment of NSO Group executives.

The **Guardia Civil** (~77,000–80,000 personnel) operates as a national gendarmerie with military status under the Interior Ministry (Defense Ministry in wartime), covering rural areas, highways, borders, and coasts. The **Policía Nacional** (~87,000 officers) handles provincial capitals and urban areas. Autonomous police forces — the **Mossos d'Esquadra** (~18,000, Catalonia), **Ertzaintza** (~8,000, Basque Country), and **Policía Foral** (Navarre) — assume full policing duties in their territories. Spain has not joined the German-led **European Sky Shield Initiative**, aligning with France and Italy in concerns about over-reliance on non-European defense systems, though it recently signed a **$1.7 billion Patriot system** deal with the United States.

## Conclusion

These five dimensions of Spain's structural profile reveal a country defined by acute tensions between ambition and capacity. Environmentally, Spain faces existential water and climate threats that its institutional framework — fragmented between 17 autonomous communities — struggles to address. Its illicit economy is not peripheral but intertwined with political party financing, real estate markets, and port infrastructure at a scale that structural reforms have failed to contain. The media ecosystem's sharp polarization and declining trust (33%) undermines democratic deliberation precisely when complex policy trade-offs — on water, defense, immigration — require informed publics. Internationally, Spain leverages EU institutional weight and Latin American soft power but chronically underinvests in hard security, producing the NATO spending crisis and the March 2026 base confrontation that together represent the most serious test of the transatlantic relationship from a Southern European ally. The military itself is thoroughly depoliticized and professionally competent but structurally undersized, with a reserve force of barely 3,000–5,000 in a continent rearming for potential great-power competition. The common thread is a gap between Spain's fourth-largest-EU-economy status and the institutional, fiscal, and political capital required to address converging environmental, security, and governance challenges simultaneously.

# Spain: Structural Country Dossier (Sections 13–20)

**Middle Powers Monitor — March 2026**

Spain enters 2026 as a paradox: Europe's fastest-growing major economy, led by a minority government that cannot pass a budget, embroiled in the most severe diplomatic rupture with the United States since the Iraq War. Pedro Sánchez's refusal to permit US strikes on Iran from Rota and Morón bases in March 2026 crystallized Spain's distinctive multi-vector positioning — a NATO member willing to defy Washington on international law grounds, shielded by EU collective trade policy, and motivated by deeply embedded anti-war collective memory. Beneath this headline crisis, Spain's civil society is undergoing a housing-driven mobilization cycle comparable to 15-M, its far right is surging to historic highs, and the constitutional settlement that has governed since 1978 faces accumulating stresses it was not designed to absorb.

## SECTION 13: Civil society and dissent infrastructure

### Labor unions wield outsized influence despite low membership density

Spain's two major trade union confederations — **CCOO (Comisiones Obreras)** and **UGT (Unión General de Trabajadores)** — operate in a paradox of low density but extraordinary structural power. Combined membership reached **2.07 million by 2024** (up 12.5% from 2015 but still below the 2010 peak), producing a union density of approximately **16.2%**. Yet collective bargaining coverage reaches **92% of employees**, among the highest in the EU, thanks to the "audience model" that grants legal representativeness based on works council elections rather than membership.

CCOO, historically linked to the Communist Party (PCE/IU), claims **1.09 million members** under General Secretary **Unai Sordo**. UGT, historically affiliated with PSOE, is led by **Pepe Álvarez**. Both unions' landmark achievement was the **February 2022 labor reform**, negotiated through tripartite social dialogue with the employers' association CEOE and Labor Minister Yolanda Díaz. Passed by a single vote in Parliament (one PP deputy accidentally voted in favor), the reform abolished unjustified fixed-term contracts (Spain had ~25% temporary employment), restored sectoral collective agreement primacy, and introduced short-time work schemes. The minimum wage trajectory illustrates their policy leverage: from €950/month in 2021 to **€1,221/month in February 2026** (signed by Sánchez, Sordo, and Álvarez), benefiting ~2.5 million workers, 57% of them women.

The Basque Country operates an entirely separate union ecosystem. **ELA (Eusko Langileen Alkartasuna)**, with over **105,000 members**, dominates Basque workplace representation and refuses to participate in Spanish-state social dialogue. **LAB**, with ~50,000 members, is aligned with EH Bildu's social base. Together they organized six general strikes between 2009 and 2013 plus a 2020 Basque general strike — making it Europe's most strike-active territory. The anarcho-syndicalist **CGT** remains a distant third nationally but is active in feminist mobilizations.

### The Catholic Church's accelerating institutional decline

The institutional footprint remains vast — roughly **23,000 parishes**, over 5,800 publicly funded private schools, and extensive healthcare and social service networks — but every metric of vitality is deteriorating. Catholic self-identification has fallen from ~70% (2011) to **55–56%** (2025 CIS data), with only 18.8% describing themselves as practicing. Among 18–29-year-olds, Catholic identification plunged from 60% (2002) to **32%** (2024). Catholic marriages collapsed from near-universality in the 1970s to just **18%** in 2023. Over 40% of the population now identifies as atheist, agnostic, or indifferent.

The vocational crisis is existential: average priest age is ~65, new ordinations have fallen to record lows (~168 annually), and **over 6,000 parishes lack a full-time priest**. Latin American immigration is the primary source of Church vitality, with Spain's foreign-born population growing from 1.6 million (2000) to 9.4 million (2025).

Financially, the Church benefits from the *asignación tributaria* system (taxpayers can allocate 0.7% of tax liability to the Church) and extensive property tax exemptions. The **inmatriculaciones controversy** — an estimated 40,000–100,000 heritage properties registered to Church ownership through bishops' notarial declarations under a 1998 Aznar-era law — remains a live political issue. A March 2026 book documented the scope of this "patrimony grab." The provision was repealed in 2015 after a European Court of Human Rights ruling, but registered properties have not been returned.

The Church has lost every major culture-war battle of the Sánchez era: euthanasia legalization (2021), Trans Law (2023), expanded abortion access (2023), and LGBTQ rights expansion. **Opus Dei**, which operates the prestigious University of Navarra and historically placed members in government and finance, has faced renewed scrutiny following a 2024 exposé alleging labor exploitation and financial crimes.

### Housing has become Spain's defining mobilization issue

Housing has overtaken unemployment as Spain's top public concern (CIS data). Rents have **doubled over the past decade**; home purchase prices rose 47% between 2015 and 2023 and another 8% in 2024. The Bank of Spain reports **40% of renters spend more than 40% of income on housing**. Public and social housing constitutes under **2% of total stock** — against an OECD average of 7% and a Dutch benchmark of 35% — despite 3.8 million vacant homes nationwide.

The mobilization cycle began with anti-tourism protests in 2024: **50,000 marched across the Canary Islands** in April demanding temporary tourism limits; 12,000–50,000 protested in Palma de Mallorca in July; and in Barcelona, protesters wielded water guns against tourists while chanting "Barcelona is not for sale." The movement broadened into a coordinated Southern European alliance (**SET — Sud d'Europa contra la Turistització**), staging simultaneous protests across 16 cities in June 2025.

The watershed came on **April 5, 2025**, when **hundreds of thousands marched in 40+ cities** — an estimated 150,000+ in Madrid and 100,000+ in Barcelona — under the slogan "Let's End the Housing Business." The **Sindicato de Inquilinas e Inquilinos** (Madrid Tenants' Union, founded 2017) and the **Sindicat de Llogateres** (Catalan Tenants' Union, ~4,000 members) organized the actions, demanding 50% rent reductions, expropriation of vacant properties held by investment funds, and calling for a rent strike on the ~500,000 contracts expiring in 2025. This represents the most significant street mobilization cycle since 15-M.

### The feminist movement is powerful but fractured

Spain's 8M (International Women's Day) marches rank among the world's largest feminist mobilizations. The **2018 mobilization** — a historic feminist general strike catalyzed by the La Manada gang-rape case — drew hundreds of thousands to the streets of Madrid alone and reshaped the policy landscape. Legislative achievements include the Gender Violence Law (2004, continuously expanded), the "Only Yes Means Yes" consent law (2022), Europe's first menstrual leave (2023), and removal of the abortion reflection period (2023).

However, the movement is now visibly split. The **Comisión 8M** (supporting trans rights and gender self-determination) and the **Movimiento Feminista de Madrid** (opposing the Trans Law as "erasure of women") have organized **separate marches since 2023**. The "Only Yes Means Yes" law produced an unintended crisis: the merger of offense categories without transitional provisions led to **721 sex offenders receiving reduced sentences and 74 being released**. PSOE voted with PP to reinstate higher minimums in March 2023, fracturing the coalition — Podemos accused PSOE of betraying feminism.

### The think tank ecosystem reflects Spain's political cleavages

The **Real Instituto Elcano** (founded 2001, directed by Charles Powell) is the establishment foreign policy voice — ranked top 50 globally, funded by IBEX 35 companies and the state, center-right in orientation. **CIDOB** in Barcelona offers a more internationally focused, less ideologically aligned perspective on Mediterranean and global affairs. **Fundación FAES**, chaired by former PM José María Aznar, serves as PP's ideological laboratory, advocating liberal-conservative democracy and Western alignment. **Fundación Alternativas**, linked to PSOE figures including Felipe González and Javier Solana, provides center-left policy analysis.

Spain has approximately 53 think tanks, with business models split among non-profit (66%) and university-based (13%) models. The ecosystem has relatively low policy influence compared to Anglo-Saxon counterparts, with no strong revolving-door culture between think tanks and government. Consulting firms (Llorente y Cuenca, Acento) increasingly perform think-tank-like functions.

### From 15-M to tenant unions: the protest-to-party pipeline

The **15-M/Indignados movement** (May 15, 2011 — mass occupation of Puerta del Sol and plazas nationwide) generated Spain's most consequential social movement infrastructure of the 21st century. Its institutional legacy includes: **Podemos** (founded January 2014 by Pablo Iglesias, entered coalition government 2020); **Barcelona en Comú** (founded June 2014 by PAH co-founder Ada Colau, who served as Barcelona mayor 2015–2023, increasing social housing stock from 7,600 to 11,000+ units); and **Ahora Madrid** (Manuela Carmena as Madrid mayor 2015–2019). The current junior coalition partner **Sumar** is the latest descendant.

The **PAH (Plataforma de Afectados por la Hipoteca)**, founded in 2009 during the mortgage crisis, achieved 90% public approval through nonviolent eviction-blocking. Its energy has now migrated from anti-eviction (mortgage crisis) to anti-rent-inflation, with tenant unions as the organizational heirs. The April 2025 housing protests suggest a potential new mass mobilization cycle.

### Catalan and Basque civil society are normalizing

The **Assemblea Nacional Catalana (ANC)** claims ~100,000 members and **Òmnium Cultural** claims 190,000+, though both have experienced declining mobilization capacity post-2017. The annual Diada (September 11) demonstrations have shrunk significantly. The 2024 amnesty law covering ~400 people (including former political prisoners Oriol Junqueras, Jordi Cuixart, and Jordi Sànchez) and the election of PSC's Salvador Illa as Catalan president in August 2024 have shifted the independence movement from confrontational to institutional mode.

In the Basque Country, **EH Bildu** — the coalition inheriting ETA's former political base, now led by Arnaldo Otegi — operates as a conventional progressive party. It won the most votes in the Basque Country and Navarre combined in 2023 municipal elections. The broader *abertzale* movement has pivoted to socioeconomic issues: housing, pensions, labor rights, and Basque language promotion, supported by an extensive ecosystem of *ikastolas*, cultural associations, and Basque-language media.

### The Gag Law remains Spain's primary civic space constraint

The **Ley Mordaza** (Organic Law 4/2015 on Citizen Security), passed by PP with an absolute majority, has generated **~300,000 sanctions** in its first decade. Provisions include fines up to €30,000 for unauthorized use of police images, penalties for "lack of respect" to officers, and enabled summary deportations. Spain sentenced the **highest number of artists to prison** in 2017 according to FreeMuse — ahead of China and Iran. Despite repeated PSOE promises of repeal, reform efforts have stalled. A 2023 bill failed when ERC and Bildu rejected it as insufficient; a 2024 partial reform modified image-sharing provisions but left the law substantially intact. CIVICUS rates Spain's civic space as "open" overall, but the Gag Law remains the primary constraint flagged by the UN, Amnesty International, and the Committee to Protect Journalists.

## SECTION 14: Alliance genealogy and patron-client history

### From Franco's satellite to NATO member: Spain's Western integration

Spain's alliance architecture was built on a Cold War bargain that has never been fully superseded. The **1953 Pact of Madrid** — structured as three "executive agreements" to avoid US Senate ratification (politically impossible given opposition to Franco) — permitted American construction of air and naval bases in exchange for economic and military aid totaling over **$2 billion between 1954 and 1988**. Spain became what analysts called "a strategic satellite rather than a formal ally," ending its post-1945 international isolation and enabling UN admission in December 1955.

The democratic transition reshaped the relationship's terms without altering its geography. NATO membership came on **May 30, 1982**, partly motivated by strengthening civilian control after the failed February 1981 coup. The deeply contested **March 12, 1986 referendum** — approved by just **52.5%** on 59% turnout — attached three conditions that still echo: non-integration into NATO's military command structure, prohibition on nuclear weapons, and gradual reduction of US military presence. Spain only joined the integrated military structure in 1996, the same year **Javier Solana** became NATO Secretary General. EEC accession on January 1, 1986 complemented Western integration.

### The March 2026 base crisis: 73 years of partnership tested

The Rota Naval Station (Cádiz) and Morón Air Base (Seville), governed by the **1988 Agreement on Defense Cooperation**, host four US Arleigh Burke-class destroyers for ballistic missile defense and a Marine crisis response force for Africa. These bases remain under Spanish sovereignty with American joint use — a legal distinction that proved operationally decisive in March 2026.

When the US and Israel launched **"Operation Epic Fury"** against Iran on February 28, 2026, Foreign Minister **José Manuel Albares** declared Spain would **not authorize Rota and Morón for offensive strikes**, insisting operations must comply with the bilateral agreement and UN Charter. PM Sánchez condemned the strikes as "unjustified" and "dangerous." The US military **relocated at least 15 aircraft** — including KC-135 refueling tankers — to Ramstein, Germany.

On March 3, President Trump, meeting German Chancellor Merz in the Oval Office, escalated: *"Spain has been terrible. We're going to cut off all trade with Spain."* He told Treasury Secretary Bessent to "cut off all dealings with Spain" and stated: *"We could use their base if we want. We could just fly in and use it."* Spain responded on March 4 with Sánchez's televised address — **"No to war"** — deliberately echoing the 2003 Iraq War slogan. When the White House claimed Spain had "agreed to cooperate," Spain **"categorically" denied** this. Defense Minister **Margarita Robles** stated: "No single country should act as guardian of the world."

The crisis was amplified by pre-existing friction. Spain spent **1.28% of GDP on defense in 2024** — NATO's lowest. At the June 2025 Hague summit, Spain obtained a unique exemption from the 5% target, capping at 2.1%. Trump had previously suggested NATO consider Spain's expulsion. Spain also reportedly declined to purchase F-35 fighters, Huawei 5G contract renewals drew US intelligence committee scrutiny, and Spain had banned Israeli-bound weapons vessels from its ports.

Spain's economic exposure proved manageable: exports to the US total ~€16 billion (~1% of GDP), and EU collective trade policy makes a unilateral embargo effectively impossible. The European Council expressed "full solidarity" with Spain. The Ibex 35 actually rose during the crisis. The US House voted **219–212** on March 5 to halt Trump's Iran attacks.

### France: partner and competitor in defense and energy

The **FCAS (Future Combat Air System)** is Europe's most ambitious defense program — a Franco-German-Spanish trilateral effort estimated at **€100+ billion** to develop a sixth-generation combat system. Spain joined in June 2019, committing **€2.5 billion** through initial phases. **Indra** leads the sensors pillar and coordinates Spain's industrial participation; Airbus Spain handles low-observable technologies. The funding split is France 42%, Germany 34%, Spain 24%.

The program was "teetering on the brink of cancellation" as of early 2026 due to Dassault-Airbus disputes over workshare and intellectual property. Germany confirmed further postponement of Phase 2 in January 2026. Spain is the most vulnerable partner: it lacks the industrial capacity for an independent sixth-generation fighter program, making FCAS collapse an existential risk for its defense modernization trajectory.

Energy interconnection politics have historically constrained Franco-Spanish cooperation. The MidCat pipeline project — designed to increase gas flow from the Iberian Peninsula to continental Europe — was shelved despite renewed urgency after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, largely due to French resistance rooted in nuclear energy priorities.

### Morocco: Spain's most transactional bilateral relationship

The **March 2022 Western Sahara reversal** — Sánchez's letter to King Mohammed VI endorsing Morocco's autonomy plan as "the most serious, realistic and credible" initiative — represented an enormous departure from Spain's decades-long support for a UN-supervised self-determination referendum. The reversal was precipitated by Morocco's deliberate weaponization of migration: in **May 2021, 8,000–12,000 migrants** crossed into Ceuta after Morocco relaxed border controls, retaliating for Spain's hospitalization of Polisario Front leader Brahim Ghali.

The fallout with Algeria — Spain's former top gas supplier — was severe. Algeria recalled its ambassador, shut the Maghreb-Europe pipeline, and its gas share of Spanish supply plummeted from ~60% (2018) to **23%** (February 2022). The US replaced Algeria as Spain's leading gas provider at ~33%. The joint declaration with Morocco contained **no mention of Ceuta and Melilla**, where Morocco maintains sovereignty claims. A June 2022 mass crossing attempt at Melilla resulted in 37 deaths. Spain's phones were also infected with Pegasus spyware in May 2021, with Morocco the "plausible" culprit according to the European Parliament.

### Latin America: economic presence, diplomatic retreat

Spain maintains the deepest European footprint in Latin America through IBEX 35 multinationals — **Santander, BBVA, Telefónica, Iberdrola, Repsol** — and the institutional architecture of the Ibero-American Summit system and **SEGIB**. Spain revitalized the EU-Latin America relationship during its 2023 EU Council Presidency, hosting the first EU-CELAC summit in years. However, the **Milei-Sánchez dispute** in May 2024 — when Argentina's president insulted Sánchez's wife, prompting ambassador withdrawal — exposed what Bloomberg called Spain's "dwindling Latin American influence."

### Gibraltar: a 300-year dispute approaches resolution

The **December 12, 2025 legal text** for a post-Brexit Gibraltar treaty represents a historic breakthrough: removal of all border checks, a customs union between Gibraltar and the EU, **demolition of the 1.2km border fence**, and dual security checks at Gibraltar's airport and port. UK sovereignty is explicitly protected, and the RAF base remains under British control. Gibraltar's VAT-free regime ends, replaced by a transaction tax rising to 17% by 2029. Ratification requires UK, Gibraltar, European Parliament, and all 27 EU national parliament approvals, with provisional application planned for **April 10, 2026**.

### Multi-vector positioning: from Palestine to Iran

Spain's **May 28, 2024 recognition of Palestinian statehood** — coordinated with Ireland and Norway — was supported by **78% of Spaniards** (Elcano poll). Israel recalled its ambassador. Spain subsequently joined South Africa's ICJ genocide case against Israel in October 2024, committed €75 million in Palestinian aid, and suspended arms exports to Israel.

The 2023 EU Council Presidency — Spain's fifth — yielded substantive achievements: opening EU accession negotiations with Ukraine and Moldova, the Migration and Asylum Pact, the AI Act agreement, Schengen extension to Bulgaria and Romania, and the EU-CELAC summit. Sánchez visited Kyiv on Day 1 of the presidency.

Spain's positioning synthesizes: EU/NATO member but maintaining independent positions on the Middle East, pragmatic engagement with China (Sánchez met Xi Jinping three times in two years, making Spain "one of the EU's most China-friendly governments" per CEPA), Latin American bridge-building, and willingness to defy Washington. The March 2026 Iran crisis is the fullest expression of this multi-vector approach.

## SECTION 15: Constitutional crises and regime transition patterns

### A country defined by cyclical regime rupture

Spain has produced **nine constitutions since 1800, four civil wars, 13 military coups, and two Bourbon restorations**. The pattern: Bourbon Restoration (1874–1931) ended when Alfonso XIII fled after municipal elections became a de facto monarchy referendum. The Second Republic (1931–1939) delivered universal suffrage, agrarian reform, and regional autonomy before collapsing into Civil War. Franco's 36-year dictatorship ended with his death on November 20, 1975.

The democratic transition (1975–1982) — the *ruptura pactada* (negotiated rupture) — achieved democratic change through legal continuity rather than revolutionary break. A seven-member drafting committee spanning the political spectrum met over 100 sessions. Key compromises: republicans accepted the Bourbon monarchy in exchange for democratic safeguards; centralists and federalists split the difference with the autonomous communities model; and the **1977 Amnesty Law** prioritized stability over punitive justice. The failed coup of **February 23, 1981** (Lt. Col. Tejero's seizure of Congress) tested and ultimately strengthened the settlement — Juan Carlos's televised intervention proved decisive.

### The 1978 Constitution: deliberately rigid by design

The Constitution establishes a **parliamentary monarchy** with the king as ceremonial head of state (Article 56), a strong executive (the PM dominates through investiture and dissolution powers), and 17 autonomous communities plus Ceuta and Melilla. The electoral system — proportional representation via the D'Hondt method across 52 constituencies — structurally favors large national parties and strong regional ones, with small provinces effectively requiring 10–15%+ for a seat.

Amendment rigidity is the system's defining feature. **Article 167** (ordinary amendments) requires three-fifths supermajority in both chambers. **Article 168** (fundamental changes affecting the Crown, core rights, or foundational principles) demands two-thirds approval, immediate dissolution, new elections, two-thirds approval by the new legislature, *and* a mandatory referendum — making change to Spain's foundational architecture nearly impossible. In practice, any amendment requires PP-PSOE agreement.

Only **three amendments** have passed in 47 years: the 1992 Maastricht Treaty adjustment (extending municipal voting to EU citizens), the controversial 2011 balanced-budget rule (rushed through in under a month during the Eurozone crisis), and a 2024 update to disability terminology in Article 49.

### Democratic erosion: institutional stress accumulates

The **CGPJ (General Council of the Judiciary) blockade** was the most visible institutional crisis. PP blocked renewal of the judiciary's governing body from December 2018 until **July 25, 2024** — a five-and-a-half-year deadlock driven by PP's refusal to negotiate while PSOE governed with Catalan and Basque nationalist support. The UN Special Rapporteur warned in January 2024 of an "unprecedented crisis." Resolution came through European Commission mediation, with **Isabel Perelló** becoming the first female CGPJ and Supreme Court president.

The **2024 Amnesty Law** (Organic Law 1/2024, effective June 11, 2024) amnestied ~350–400 people linked to the Catalan independence process. Passed 177–172 with PSOE, Sumar, ERC, Junts, and Bildu support, it was challenged as unconstitutional by PP and Vox. The Constitutional Court upheld the law on **June 26, 2025** in a **6–4 decision**, though the Supreme Court refused to apply amnesty to Puigdemont on embezzlement charges, maintaining his arrest warrant. This judicial defiance of legislative amnesty remains unresolved.

Sánchez's heavy reliance on **Royal Decree-Laws** (Article 86 emergency legislation) — 25+ in his first 8 months of minority government alone — draws persistent executive-overreach criticism. Governing without a passed budget since 2022 compounds the constitutional strain.

### The electoral horizon: PP-Vox convergence accelerates

Current polling (March 2026 aggregates) shows **PP at 30.2%** (~122 seats), **PSOE at 27.7%** (~112 seats), and **Vox at 18.3%** (~73 seats). A PP-Vox coalition would command approximately **195 seats** — well above the 176-seat majority. The left-of-PSOE space has fragmented: Sumar polls at 7% and Podemos at 3.9%, a combined ~11% down from 12.3% at the 2023 election.

Vox's surge is the story of 2025–2026. The party has broken through its previous ~15% ceiling, polling at 18–20% nationally and exceeding 20% in Castile and León for the first time in any region. Recent regional elections confirm the pattern: in Extremadura (December 2025) and Aragon (February 2026), Vox doubled seats while PSOE collapsed in traditional strongholds. A quarter of Spaniards under 24 could vote for Vox, rising to **36% among young men**. The party left the ECR in July 2024 to join Orbán's Patriots for Europe alongside Le Pen and the Austrian FPÖ.

PP under **Alberto Núñez Feijóo** — a four-term Galician president elected PP leader in April 2022 — projects technocratic reliability but faces a structural dilemma: PP-Vox coalitions governed five regions after 2023, but all collapsed in July 2024 when Abascal withdrew over migrant redistribution. National coalition would require absorbing Vox's radical demands on immigration, territorial restructuring, and gender policy rollback.

Within PSOE, the post-Sánchez succession is quietly underway: **María Jesús Montero** (Finance Minister, appointed PSOE-Andalusia leader February 2025), **Salvador Illa** (Catalan president, turned PSC into Catalonia's dominant force), and **Teresa Ribera** (now EU Competition Commissioner). The **Koldo corruption case** — centering on kickback schemes involving former Transport Minister Ábalos's aide, which sent PSOE Organization Secretary Santos Cerdán to prison in June 2025 — has severely damaged the party's institutional credibility.

### The monarchy: stable but not invulnerable

Felipe VI's approval ratings (44% approve, 21% disapprove, average **6.1/10** — higher than any politician) and Princess Leonor's exceptional popularity (8.1–9.5 rating) provide a buffer. Republican sentiment holds steady at **32–40%**, with a technical tie among under-30s. Juan Carlos I's exile to Abu Dhabi (August 2020) over financial scandals — alleged Saudi contract kickbacks and offshore accounts — damaged the institution, though Felipe's response (stopping his father's allowance, renouncing inheritance) partially contained the fallout.

The **constructive vote of no confidence** (Article 113) — requiring presentation of an alternative PM candidate and an absolute majority — is the system's crucial stabilizer. Only one has ever succeeded: Sánchez's **June 1, 2018** motion against Rajoy, passed 180–169 following the Gürtel corruption verdict. The mechanism explains why Sánchez survived Junts's October 2025 withdrawal — PP cannot assemble 176 votes for Feijóo without total opposition unity, which is structurally impossible.

PP controls the vast majority of autonomous communities — with absolute majorities in **Andalusia** (Juanma Moreno), **Madrid** (Isabel Díaz Ayuso), and **Galicia** (Alfonso Rueda) — constituting a powerful counterweight to the central government. PSOE retains **Catalonia** (Illa), **Castilla-La Mancha** (Emiliano García-Page, notable for his independence from Sánchez), and **Asturias**. The PNV governs the Basque Country.

## SECTION 16: Collective memory and grievance narratives

### The Civil War still organizes Spanish politics

The 1936–1939 Civil War — which killed over 500,000 — and Franco's 36-year dictatorship remain the foundational trauma. The **Pacto del Olvido** (Pact of Forgetting), formalized through the 1977 Amnesty Law, enabled Spain's peaceful transition but left an estimated **114,226 enforced disappearances** unaddressed. Of approximately **2,382–3,500 mass graves**, only ~850 have been exhumed, recovering ~11,000–17,000 victims over two decades.

Two legislative interventions have attempted redress. The **2007 Historical Memory Law** (Zapatero) recognized victims and provided exhumation funding, but the Rajoy government (2011–2018) cut all state funding without repealing it. The **2022 Democratic Memory Law** (Sánchez) was substantially more ambitious: the state assumed direct responsibility for exhumations, created a Register of Victims with DNA testing, voided tens of thousands of Francoist political convictions, mandated Civil War teaching in secondary schools, and established a sanctions regime. It also created the **"Grandchildren's Law"** granting citizenship to descendants of exiles — generating **876,321 applications** by July 2025, with 40% from Argentina alone.

**Franco's exhumation from the Valle de los Caídos** on **October 24, 2019** was a signature Sánchez achievement. The site — renamed **Valle de Cuelgamuros** — contains remains of ~33,800 Republican victims interred without family consent alongside Nationalist dead. In November 2025, the winning design for the site's transformation was announced: demolition of the monumental staircase, creation of an open vestibule and interpretation center, with construction estimated to begin in 2027. Visitor numbers, while recovering to 254,157 in 2025, remain 33% below the 2018 peak.

PP-Vox regional governments have pushed back: Aragon, Castile and León, and Valencia proposed "Harmony Laws" to replace the Democratic Memory Law, prompting condemnation from three UN experts in April 2024.

### The "Two Spains" persist in new form

Antonio Machado's verse — "One of those two Spains will freeze your heart" — articulates a cleavage predating him (satirist Larra wrote in 1836: "Here lies half of Spain. It died of the other half"). The core oppositions — Catholic/secular, centralist/peripheralist, European/traditionalist — culminated in civil war and persist through left-right polarization, urban-rural divides (*España vacía*), and center-periphery tensions. The media ecosystem maps onto this: **El País** (progressive/center-left) against **El Mundo, ABC, La Razón, and OK Diario** (conservative), with **elDiario.es** and **Público** on the further left.

### Vox weaponizes the Reconquista

Vox has deliberately appropriated the Reconquista narrative — the centuries-long Christian reconquest of Iberia from Muslim rule (711–1492) — as a political framework. Santiago Abascal launched his April 2019 campaign at **Covadonga**, the mythical birthplace of the Reconquista, and regularly rallies alongside monuments to Pelayo, El Cid, and Blas de Lezo. At a February 2025 Madrid rally with Viktor Orbán, Abascal vowed to "reconquer Europe." At a September 2025 Patriots for Europe event, he called for a new Reconquista against an "Islamist invasion" and "woke ideology."

Historians note the term "Reconquista" did not exist in the Middle Ages — it emerged with 19th-century nationalism. The Battle of Covadonga (722 CE) was largely invented by King Alfonso the Great some 150 years later. Yet the myth's political potency is undeniable: it frames anti-immigration politics as civilizational defense and connects Vox to Spain's foundational national mythology.

### Catalan grievance: structural decline after 2017

The independence movement has entered structural decline. The CEO (Catalan polling center) found in February–March 2025 that **38% support independence** — the lowest ever recorded — while **54% oppose** it (the highest ever). In the 2024 Catalan election, pro-independence parties won only 61 of 135 seats and 43.2% of the vote, down from 74 seats and 48.1% in 2021. Salvador Illa became the first unionist leader in 14 years and restored the Spanish flag to the Generalitat palace.

The movement is fractured among **ERC** (pragmatic social democrats, reduced to 20 seats), **JxCat** (Puigdemont's center-right maximalists, 35 seats), and the collapsed **CUP** (anti-capitalist). The fiscal grievance — Catalonia contributing ~20% of Spain's GDP while claiming net fiscal transfers to Madrid — remains potent but has been partially addressed through the controversial "singular financing" deal negotiated with ERC for Illa's investiture.

### Basque memory: violence processed, justice incomplete

ETA's violence — **over 850 killed, 2,600 wounded** over nearly 60 years — ended with the October 2011 ceasefire and May 2018 formal dissolution. Key turning points include the 1973 assassination of PM Carrero Blanco, the 1987 Hipercor bombing (21 killed), and the 1997 kidnapping and murder of town councilor **Miguel Ángel Blanco**, which turned millions against ETA. The abertzale left has completed its transition to conventional democratic politics through EH Bildu.

No truth and reconciliation commission has been established, partly because it would require investigating **state crimes** — the GAL (Grupos Antiterroristas de Liberación) death squads killed 27–28 people under Felipe González's government (1983–1987), and the prisoner dispersal policy remains contentious. Victims' associations, particularly the **AVT** (aligned with PP positions), maintain significant political influence.

### Anti-Americanism: from Iraq to Iran

Spain's anti-American sentiment has deep roots — the 1953 bases agreement associated the US with propping up Franco — but the Iraq War was the crystallizing event. **91% of Spaniards** opposed the war, the highest in any country polled. PM Aznar's alignment with Bush at the Azores Summit (March 2003) defied overwhelming public opinion. The **March 11, 2004 Madrid train bombings** (191 killed) — linked to Spain's Iraq involvement — and Aznar's defeated attempt to blame ETA, produced PSOE's election victory three days later and immediate troop withdrawal.

The template is being explicitly replayed in March 2026. Sánchez invoked 2003 directly: "In 2003, a few irresponsible leaders dragged us into an illegal war." The slogan **"No a la guerra"** has returned. Spain consistently ranks among the most anti-American countries in Western Europe in Pew surveys, with sentiment directed primarily at the US government rather than the American people.

### The Black Legend shapes diplomatic reflexes

The *Leyenda Negra* — 16th-century Protestant propaganda portraying the Spanish Empire as uniquely cruel — was systematized by Julián Juderías in 1914, published in the acute vulnerability following the 1898 Disaster. María Elvira Roca Barea's *Imperiofobia y Leyenda Negra* (2016) became a bestseller (17+ editions) arguing Spain's colonial legacy has been unfairly maligned. Mexico's 2019 demand for a colonial-era apology provoked intense backlash. Spain's sensitivity to external criticism of its colonial record shapes diplomatic reflexes, including its framing of *Hispanidad* as a cultural bridge rather than imperial project.

### Memory narratives constrain and enable foreign policy

These collective memories produce specific foreign policy outcomes. Civil War trauma and Iraq War backlash generate deep public resistance to military adventurism — enabling Sánchez's "No to war" stance. The 1898 Disaster drives enthusiastic EU engagement as compensation for perceived decline. The Catalan crisis makes Spain sensitive to self-determination issues internationally — Spain is one of **five EU members** not recognizing Kosovo. And Spain's historical pro-Arab diplomacy, combined with anti-war sentiment and progressive positioning, enabled the May 2024 Palestinian statehood recognition and October 2024 intervention in the ICJ genocide case against Israel.

## SECTION 17–20: Cross-cutting political dynamics

### Spain is Europe's economic outperformer — for now

Spain recorded GDP growth of **3.2–3.5% in 2024** and **2.8–2.9% in 2025** — the strongest among major eurozone economies. Unemployment fell below **10% for the first time since 2008** (9.9% in Q4 2025), with employment at a record 22.46 million. Inflation moderated to 2.4% in January 2026. Public debt is projected to fall below 100% of GDP in 2026.

Key drivers include **record tourism** (94–97 million visitors generating €135 billion), strong high-value services growth, and NextGenerationEU fund absorption — Spain's allocation totals **€163 billion** (€79.8 billion in grants plus €83.2 billion in loans, second only to Italy). As of early 2025, €48.3 billion had been received. Renewables supplied over 50% of electricity in 2024, though the **April 28, 2025 Iberian Peninsula blackout** — caused by voltage control failures and weak France-Spain interconnection (3.4% vs. the EU 10–15% target), not renewables — exposed grid fragility.

Growth forecasts for 2026 range from 1.9% (Goldman Sachs) to 2.4% (BBVA), with the Iran crisis and potential US trade disruption as downside risks. Spain's trade exposure to the US is relatively limited at ~4.4% of GDP versus 10.1% for the EU average.

### The DANA floods reshaped Valencian politics

The **October 29, 2024 DANA flooding** — 771.8 liters/m² in 24 hours at Turís — killed **224–237 people** and displaced 15,000, with over €3 billion in property damage. Valencian President Carlos Mazón (PP) was at a six-hour lunch during the disaster and delayed emergency alerts; he was branded a "murderer" by constituents and **resigned November 3, 2025**. The central government deployed a €10.6 billion aid package. Paradoxically, Vox has been the main political beneficiary in Valencia, surpassing 16% in regional polls while spreading disinformation blaming Sánchez. Two weeks after the disaster, the PP-Vox majority in Valencia approved reducing coastal construction setbacks from 500m to 200m.

### Immigration: declining numbers, rising political salience

Irregular arrivals dropped **42.6% in 2025** to 36,775 — Canary Islands arrivals fell 62% — following intensified bilateral cooperation with Morocco and Mauritania and €240 million in EU funding for Mauritanian coastal surveillance. In early 2026, the government approved a decree to **regularize ~500,000 undocumented migrants** (those present 5+ months who applied for protection before December 2025). Total foreign-born population exceeds 7 million out of 49.4 million. Despite declining numbers, immigration has risen to a top public concern per CIS data, fueling Vox's electoral surge. An estimated 10,457 people died attempting to reach Spain in 2024.

### The government operates without a budget or a stable majority

Since Junts's withdrawal in **October 2025**, Sánchez governs with 148 coalition seats (PSOE 121 + Sumar 31) — 28 short of a majority — negotiating each key vote ad hoc. No budget has been passed since 2022. The flagship **37.5-hour work week** was voted down in early 2026 by PP, Vox, and Junts. The Koldo corruption scandal, the probe into Sánchez's wife, and investigations into his brother and the Attorney General create a permanent atmosphere of institutional siege.

Yet Sánchez has stated he will not call early elections and intends to run in 2027. The constructive no-confidence mechanism protects him: PP cannot assemble 176 votes for Feijóo. The March 2026 Iran crisis has temporarily boosted him domestically — positioning him as a leader standing up to Trump resonates powerfully with Spain's anti-war majority. A cabinet reshuffle is expected in April 2026, with Montero departing to lead PSOE's Andalusian campaign and Díaz reportedly stepping back from front-line politics.

### What no constitutional reform can address

No major constitutional reform is advancing, nor is one realistic. PP controls the Senate with an absolute majority, making any amendment requiring super-majorities impossible without bipartisan agreement that current polarization precludes. The territorial model (Title VIII), Senate reform, and an EU integration clause remain debated in academic circles but politically inert. Spain's Constitution has been amended three times in 47 years — each requiring extraordinary political alignment that current conditions cannot produce. The accumulated stresses — CGPJ blockades, amnesty controversies, executive decree reliance, regional power asymmetries — are being managed through political improvisation rather than institutional redesign. Whether this improvisation can sustain a constitutional settlement designed for a very different political era remains Spain's central structural question.

## Conclusion

Spain's position in early 2026 illustrates a distinctive middle-power pattern: leveraging institutional buffers (EU collective trade policy, constructive no-confidence rules, constitutional rigidity) to sustain political agency disproportionate to its material weight. The March 2026 base access crisis is not an aberration but the logical culmination of deeply embedded collective memories — Civil War trauma, Iraq War backlash, and anti-American sentiment — interacting with Spain's multi-vector positioning within but partially independent of NATO. Domestically, the paradox of economic outperformance coinciding with housing crisis-driven mass mobilization and far-right surge suggests the governing PSOE formula — progressive legislation without a parliamentary majority, sustained through ad hoc alliances with peripheral nationalists — is approaching exhaustion. The structural question is whether Spain's next political cycle, likely featuring a PP-Vox governing coalition, can manage the tensions between Atlantic alliance obligations, European integration commitments, and a public deeply resistant to military entanglement — all within a constitutional framework that has proven nearly impossible to reform.
