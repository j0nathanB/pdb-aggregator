THE MIDDLE POWERS MONITOR
Week of February 10, 2026
THIS WEEK
This week, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney moved to reshape the country's defence posture and trade architecture simultaneously, releasing Canada's first defence industrial strategy while installing Janice Charette as chief trade negotiator to Washington ahead of the critical July 1 CUSMA review deadline. Carney also held bilateral talks with U.K. Prime Minister Keir Starmer on trade and defence financing, signaling a deliberate diversification of partnerships beyond North America. A Supreme Court ruling annulling a parliamentary election result narrowed Carney's majority, tightening his domestic room to maneuver at a moment of acute external pressure from the Trump administration.

North America

Carney's defence industrial strategy, unveiled Tuesday in Montreal, targets awarding 70 percent of federal defence contracts to Canadian firms, a sharp pivot toward domestic manufacturing capacity and reduced dependence on foreign suppliers. The appointment of Charette—a veteran civil servant—as chief U.S. trade negotiator underscores Ottawa's urgency to stabilize the bilateral relationship ahead of the CUSMA joint review, even as an unresolved bridge dispute and broader friction with the Trump administration persist. The week's agenda was jolted by one of Canada's worst mass shootings in Tumbler Ridge, British Columbia, which killed nine and injured more than 25; Carney joined opposition leaders at a candlelight vigil Friday. Meanwhile, opposition parties pressed Carney to send humanitarian aid to Cuba or publicly challenge Washington's tightened fuel restrictions on the island, adding another friction point to the already strained Canada-U.S. dynamic. The Starmer talks on trade and defence financing point to a longer-term hedging strategy, but the narrowing of Carney's parliamentary majority after the Terrebonne ruling constrains his government's capacity to push bold legislation on any of these fronts.
