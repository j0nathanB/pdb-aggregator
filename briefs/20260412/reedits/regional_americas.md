Canada's Liberal government has gained a majority through defections from other parties while the Conservative opposition faces revolt from within — up to 40 MPs are questioning their leadership. In Brazil, the 2026 presidential race has become a dead heat between Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro two years early, prompting a cabinet reshuffle and frantic party repositioning. In Chile, the governing coalition has split between Chile Vamos and Partido Republicano after José Antonio Kast, the president, saw his approval rating fall to 36% and a cabinet minister was physically attacked.

Mexico is the exception. While its neighbours wrestle with leadership crises and broken coalitions, Mexico is pursuing big economic projects: a $540 million development programme in Tlaxcala and a reversal of its fracking policy to cut dependence on imported natural gas. The contrast is sharp — where other political systems are cracking, Mexico's seem able to think beyond the next crisis.

That all three crises erupted at once points to broader regional strains, but each follows its own path. Canada's problems are about leadership, Brazil's about an election campaign that started two years too early, Chile's about Mr Kast's inability to hold his coalition together. Yet all three share a common thread: challenges to the authority of the executive.

More notable is what has not happened. Despite the political chaos at home, none of these countries has turned inward or pulled back from international commitments. Their foreign policy machines seem insulated from the domestic political storms, a sign of institutional strength that the headlines about government crises and falling approval ratings do not capture.

Regional cooperation was largely absent this week, despite migration and trade integration challenges that usually prompt joint action across the Americas.
