Leaders across the Americas are taking bigger risks as the cautious politics that kept them in power stop working.

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, Brazil's president, faces his first serious threat in over two decades. Polling shows him tied with Flávio Bolsonaro for 2026 — a shift for someone who has been Brazil's dominant political figure for over 20 years. His response shows how cornered he is. When a Petrobras gas auction drove prices up 118% in March, Mr Lula publicly called it "stupidity, nonsense, banditry." The company promptly reversed the pricing, citing "exceptional market context" — a euphemism for presidential pressure. Direct intervention in market pricing was unthinkable for a president who built his reputation on respecting institutions. That he risks it shows his weakness.

Mark Carney, Canada's prime minister, is converting weakness into strength. Conservative floor-crossings have brought his Liberals within one seat of majority control, which Monday's by-elections should deliver. The shift from minority to majority government would end two years of relying on the Bloc Québécois and free Mr Carney to act until his mandate expires in 2028. But the move comes at a cost: 40 Conservative MPs fear losing their seats under Pierre Poilievre's leadership, threatening his position despite 87% support from party members. Mr Carney's gambit could break the opposition as effectively as winning elections.

The same pattern appears elsewhere. José Antonio Kast, Chile's president, is pushing ahead with a 43-measure economic package even as his approval rating sinks to 36% and opposition activists throw water at his ministers. In Mexico, Claudia Sheinbaum, the president, has reversed her predecessor's ban on fracking despite Andrés Manuel López Obrador's strict independence doctrine. Both moves abandon negotiation for direct action that carries political risks.

These are not small adjustments but fundamental breaks that will define the region's politics. As the old arrangements prove inadequate, leaders are choosing unilateral action over gradual change. The bet is that bold moves will create new realities before opposition can organise. Whether they succeed or fail, the cautious bargaining that characterised the past decade appears to be ending.

Countries kept up external engagement despite intense domestic political stress. With such internal pressure in three of four countries, you might expect regional cooperation or inward-looking policies. Instead, the institutional separation between domestic politics and foreign policy remains intact.
