Three of the four biggest countries in the Americas are cracking under political pressure, each in different ways. In Canada, Mark Carney's Liberals won majority control as five opposition MPs switched sides, but up to 40 Conservative MPs now question Pierre Poilievre's leadership privately. In Brazil, polls show Flávio Bolsonaro tied with Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva at 46% to 45% for 2026, forcing Mr Lula into emergency cabinet changes as his approval crashed to 29% positive against 40% negative. In Chile, José Antonio Kast's approval fell to 36% for the third straight month while students threw water at his science minister and his own coalition partners turned on each other—the Independent Democratic Union told the Republican Party to stop campaigning and start governing.

Each government is trying a different escape route. Mr Carney will use his new majority to pass policy without the Bloc Québécois as kingmaker. Mr Lula is reshuffling 40% of his cabinet by April 2026 because election law bars office holders from campaigning. Mr Kast announced a 43-measure economic package on television even as his Chile Vamos alliance threatens to collapse, trying to rebuild support through job creation promises.

The crisis has moved beyond normal politics. In Brazil, Mr Lula publicly said Supreme Court justice Alexandre de Moraes should step aside from corruption cases. Other justices called his comments unfair. When Petrobras held a gas auction that drove prices up 118%, Mr Lula called it "stupidity, nonsense, banditry" and the company promptly reversed the pricing under what it called "regulatory concerns"—presidential pressure.

Mexico is different. While her neighbours manage political fires, Claudia Sheinbaum is executing long-term plans. She broke from her predecessor's energy independence doctrine by lifting Mexico's fracking ban, admitting that PEMEX lacks the technology and may need private partners to cut reliance on American natural gas imports. She opened the first Economic Development Pole in Tlaxcala with $540 million in investment and over 5,000 jobs, while the Senate confirmed Roberto Velasco as foreign minister with 81 votes.

Regional cooperation frameworks did not appear this week despite severe domestic political stress in three countries. Countries under such stress usually pull back from international engagement, yet these maintained external partnerships—Canada courting Finland, Chile deepening ties with Argentina, Mexico planning its first European trip in eight years to Spain.
