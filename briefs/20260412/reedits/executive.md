The machinery of global cooperation is breaking down, but unevenly. When energy shocks hit this week, every region chose national solutions despite decades of integration. Australia used LNG exports for bilateral fuel deals. Japan quadrupled US imports while drawing down strategic reserves. Indonesia sent its president to Moscow for Russian energy deals. Three completely different solutions to the same problem, without coordinating. The pattern held across all regions — shared shocks now trigger separate responses however close countries' ties on paper.

Yet European coordination worked when Hungary's government fell. The EU coordinated congratulations through official channels while Eastern Europe delivered policy wins: Ukraine gained an integration breakthrough, Poland's position strengthened, and Czech and Latvian officials had prepared their responses. Economic crises fragment European cooperation, but political transitions enable it. The difference shows EU institutions work for alignment but lack the tools for economic crisis management. When Hungarian obstruction disappeared, the coordination capacity that had been blocked for years suddenly functioned across both Western and Eastern Europe.

Alliances are splitting by function under pressure. Pakistan activated its Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement with Saudi Arabia for the first time, deploying fighter jets and troops to King Abdulaziz Air Base during Iranian attacks. The military commitment held firm even as diplomatic relationships frayed elsewhere. Western European NATO allies maintain security cooperation while publicly rejecting American diplomatic positions: Britain shelved its Chagos handover after US pressure, Norway criticised Hormuz blockade threats, and Sweden disagreed with American NATO strategy. The operational alliance survives political breakdown.

International cooperation is not collapsing but dividing by function. Military commitments prove reliable while diplomatic coordination fragments. European political coordination thrives while economic coordination fails. National responses dominate crisis management while institutional cooperation continues in calmer waters. Each type of challenge now gets its own rules, creating a patchwork system where different institutions work for different problems. The question is whether this selectivity is adaptation or the prelude to broader institutional breakdown when multiple crises hit simultaneously.
