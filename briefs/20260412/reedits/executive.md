International cooperation is fragmenting by function, not by region. When the Middle East energy crisis hit this week, every country went national despite having institutions designed for coordination. But the same European institutions that failed on energy worked well for political alignment, and military alliances kept operating even as diplomatic relationships cracked. The international system is not breaking down — it is learning to work selectively. Some functions still connect, others have been abandoned.

Every country now goes national when economic shocks hit, no matter how integrated they claim to be. Australia used liquefied natural gas (LNG) exports for fuel deals rather than coordinating through regional bodies. Japan quadrupled energy imports from the United States while using strategic reserves. Indonesia sent its president to Moscow for direct energy talks. Europe, despite shared institutions, responded separately. The same external shock, the same absence of coordination, whether the affected countries were closely integrated or barely connected. Crisis economics has gone national everywhere.

European coordination can work — just not for economics. When Hungary's democratic transition accelerated this week, European institutions responded with the precision that was missing from the energy response. Western Europe coordinated congratulations through EU channels. Eastern Europe gave policy support: Ukraine won an EU integration deal, Poland strengthened its regional position, and the Czech Republic and Latvia had responses prepared in advance. The coordination was seamless because Hungarian obstruction was finally removed and the issue was political alignment rather than sharing costs.

Military alliances show the same selective performance. Pakistan used its Strategic Mutual Defense Agreement with Saudi Arabia for the first time, deploying fighter jets and troops to King Abdulaziz Air Base during Iranian attacks. The cooperation worked as designed. But European NATO allies maintained security cooperation while rejecting American diplomatic demands: Britain shelved the Chagos Islands handover after American pressure, Norway criticised American threats to blockade Hormuz, and Sweden disagreed with American NATO strategy. The same alliance that enabled military coordination could not produce diplomatic alignment.
