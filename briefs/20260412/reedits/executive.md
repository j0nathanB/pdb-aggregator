International cooperation is not collapsing — it is splitting by task. Military operations still work. Political deals can be struck. But handling economic crises has failed everywhere. When energy disruptions hit the Middle East this week, every region turned to national responses despite having ways to work together. Australia used its LNG exports for fuel deals. Japan quadrupled imports from America and tapped strategic reserves. Indonesia sent its president to Moscow for Russian energy. Each response made sense alone, but none were joined up, even in regions that claim to work as one.

European countries share energy infrastructure and crisis systems built over decades, yet each acted alone. The Americas have no regional energy setup, but the result was the same — national responses with no teamwork. Asia-Pacific countries used three different approaches to the same shock. Ways to work together exist in Europe and supposedly exist globally through the G20, but when tested they did not work. Countries now handle crises nationally no matter how integrated their region claims to be.

But other cooperation works well. Europe showed this week it can act together when politics allow. Hungary's shift to democracy brought congratulations through EU channels and policy work across Western and Eastern Europe. Ukraine gained an EU breakthrough. Poland's position grew stronger. The Czech Republic and Latvia prepared responses in advance. Countries worked across regions and got things done, unlike the chaos of handling economic crises. The difference is not ability — it is the type of problem. European institutions work for political deals but fail for economic crises.

Military alliances work the same way, staying effective while diplomatic ties fracture. Pakistan activated its Strategic Mutual Defense Agreement with Saudi Arabia for the first time, sending fighter jets and troops to King Abdulaziz Air Base during Iranian attacks. The military commitment worked even as wider diplomatic ties broke down. NATO shows the same split: European allies keep security cooperation while rejecting American diplomatic plans. Britain shelved the Chagos handover after American pressure. Norway criticised American threats over Hormuz blockades. Sweden disagreed with American NATO strategy. The alliance holds for military purposes but fragments for political ones.

What we see is not collapse but change — cooperation that works for some tasks but not others. Military operations work because they have chains of command and requirements that override political disagreements. Political deals can be struck when obstacles are cleared and interests match. But handling economic crises has failed across all regions and systems, suggesting the machinery built for global economic shocks no longer works. The system is adapting by dropping what fails and keeping what succeeds.
