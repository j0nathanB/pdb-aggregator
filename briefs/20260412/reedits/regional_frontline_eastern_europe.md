Viktor Orbán's electoral defeat sent four countries scrambling to adjust their positions around Hungary's new government. Ukraine lost its biggest obstacle to EU membership, while Donald Tusk, Poland's prime minister, celebrated the end of his most troublesome conservative ally. Latvia rushed to congratulate the opposition victory, and Andrej Babiš, the Czech prime minister, found himself weakened after backing the losing side. The speed of these responses revealed how much Budapest's obstruction had shaped regional diplomacy.

As countries adjusted to Hungary's shift, cracks opened across the region's democratic systems. In Poland, Constitutional Tribunal judges conducted oath ceremonies without the president — an unprecedented snub that highlighted the country's constitutional deadlock. Alar Karis, Estonia's president, broke with his own foreign ministry over Russia policy, while Jan Lipavský, the Czech foreign minister, refused to let his president attend NATO summits. Finland's governing coalition crashed to a term-low 37.9% approval rating amid challenges to its authority.

External security threats are straining internal democratic norms. Presidents clash with foreign ministers, judges defy executives, and coalition partners struggle to hold together. What links these fractures is not their specific causes — each country faces different constitutional battles — but their timing and force.

The week showed how quickly diplomatic calculations shift when a key player changes sides. Hungary's turn toward Europe removes a major brake on Ukrainian membership while weakening the networks that had connected Budapest, Warsaw and Prague. Diplomatic lines clarify, but the domestic foundations of these relationships look fragile.

NATO and EU countries failed to coordinate on defence spending this week despite sharing threats. Countries are pursuing separate plans rather than joint responses, suggesting either they lack the machinery to work together or prefer to cut their own deals.
