Three NATO allies disagreed with Washington this week while their security cooperation continued unchanged. Britain shelved its planned handover of the Chagos Islands after Donald Trump withdrew support, then Trump compared Britain to Neville Chamberlain when London criticised American energy policy. Norway called Trump's threats to blockade the Strait of Hormuz "problematic" and backed international alternatives instead. Ulf Kristersson, Sweden's prime minister, disagreed with Trump's NATO approach even as Stockholm deepened defence integration with Washington.

Each disagreement had different causes, but their timing points to a tension European allies have yet to resolve: how to maintain security ties while resisting Washington's go-it-alone policies. The friction runs between security obligations and political independence. Rather than choosing between alignment and autonomy, these countries are running parallel tracks — NATO partners in the security realm, independent actors in political disputes.

When Hungary's Viktor Orbán lost his election, Germany, Spain, Norway, and Sweden quickly celebrated with similar language about democratic breakthrough and reduced Russian influence. Sweden's foreign ministry went furthest, stating explicitly that the Hungarian opposition victory "fundamentally improves" Stockholm's position in the EU by removing its main opponent.

EU members had struggled to coordinate policy while one member blocked it, but swiftly welcomed his defeat. NATO allies, meanwhile, are finding ways to disagree with Washington without abandoning the alliance. Both patterns suggest European countries are becoming more selective about when and how they align with larger powers.

Economic coordination among EU members all facing costs from the Iran war did not emerge this week. Despite all facing higher energy prices from Middle East conflict, European countries are not coordinating economic responses, suggesting either different effects or a preference for national over joint action.
