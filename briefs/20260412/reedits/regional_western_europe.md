Viktor Orbán lost Hungary's election, and European capitals did not hide their relief. Sweden's government called him their primary antagonist. Germany congratulated his opponent, Magyar. Spain welcomed his defeat. Norway hailed the result as a blow against Russian influence. The shared response shows how much Orbán had frustrated his EU partners — and how quickly they can coordinate when they remove an obstacle.

But even as Europe found unity over Hungary, alliances with Washington soured through public rows that exposed deeper tensions. Britain shelved the Chagos handover after Trump withdrew support and compared the prime minister to Chamberlain over energy policy. Norway called Trump's Hormuz blockade threats "problematic" and backed other options. Sweden's prime minister disagreed publicly with Trump's NATO approach while keeping up defence integration. European allies are pushing back against American heavy-handedness while keeping their security ties intact.

Yet beneath the diplomatic win, coalition governments across the region faced trouble at home through corruption trials, energy policy failures, and parliamentary defeats. The strain suggests weakness under the surface unity, as governments struggle to stay legitimate while managing outside pressures. European leaders celebrate removing one problem while wrestling with plenty of their own.

EU members failed to coordinate their economic response to Iran war costs this week. Despite shared exposure to energy price shocks from Middle East conflict, European countries went their own way rather than acting together, suggesting either different impact levels or a preference for going it alone.
