Viktor Orbán's defeat unleashed European coordination this week. Germany, Spain, Norway and Sweden welcomed his fall with similar language about democratic victory and reduced Russian influence. Ulf Kristersson, Sweden's prime minister, called it a "historic victory" that opens "a new era." Espen Barth Eide, Norway's foreign minister, praised it as "a great victory for democracy." Sweden was most direct: officials said Orbán's departure "fundamentally improves" their EU environment by removing their "main roadblock." The chorus showed Europe can coordinate when a blocking partner vanishes.

Yet even as European capitals agreed on Hungary, they fractured under American pressure. Donald Trump forced Britain to abandon its plan to give the Chagos Islands to Mauritius, showing Washington can make London reverse international commitments. Mr Eide called Trump's threatened Hormuz blockade "surprising and problematic," comparing American military policy to Iranian actions. Mr Kristersson said he "completely disagrees" with Trump's NATO approach, adding "that's not how NATO works." Each ally managed its own bilateral crisis while security cooperation continued — British and Norwegian forces spent a month tracking Russian submarines, Swedish jets monitored another submarine in the Kattegat.

Coalition governments cracked under multiple pressures. In Germany, Katherina Reiche, the economics minister, attacked her Social Democratic partners, calling their energy proposals "expensive, ineffective and probably unconstitutional," forcing Friedrich Merz, the chancellor, to demand ministers keep disputes private. Norway's government lost another budget fight when opposition parties overruled it on fuel tax cuts, prompting the Green Party deputy leader to call the Centre Party's defection a "dagger in the back." Spain faced corruption testimony about cash payments at Socialist Party headquarters, while Sweden's ruling coalition split when Ebba Busch, the Christian Democrat leader, questioned whether Jimmie Åkesson, the Sweden Democrats leader, "works so much" compared to other politicians.

The pattern reveals Europe's pick-and-choose approach to unity. When member states want to coordinate — celebrating a democratic victory that removes an obstructionist — they agree within hours. When they need to coordinate — managing American pressure or supporting struggling coalition partners — each government handles its own crisis. Marine Le Pen dined with 15 major business leaders including Bernard Arnault as the far right pushes into the elite, while Emmanuel Macron's government faced union threats over May Day work plans. Giorgia Meloni summoned Israel's ambassador after Israeli forces fired on Italian peacekeepers, telling him "Italian soldiers are not to be touched." Europe coordinates when it chooses to, not when it has to.

European governments facing Iran war costs did not coordinate economic policy this week. Rachel Reeves promised support for British companies struggling with energy prices, while other European governments absorbed similar shocks alone, suggesting either different impacts or a preference for national over collective solutions.
