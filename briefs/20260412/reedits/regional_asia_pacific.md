Three big powers this week showed how Asia-Pacific countries respond to global crises: they go it alone. Australia struck a gas-for-petrol deal with Singapore and will expand it to Malaysia and Brunei next week. Australia supplies 32% of Singapore's gas and will get guaranteed fuel imports covering 55% of its domestic needs in return. Japan secured oil supplies through early 2027 by quadrupling American imports and building eight months of strategic reserves. Prabowo Subianto, Indonesia's president, flew to Moscow for energy talks with Mr Putin, defending the trip as needed to "secure oil" despite the diplomatic risks. None of these governments consulted the others; each built its own energy insurance policy.

Taiwan faces the sharpest tension as competing powers pull in opposite directions. Cheng Li-wun, the KMT chairwoman, became the first opposition leader to meet Xi Jinping in a decade, prompting China to announce ten new policies and regular party channels. Even as Ms Cheng called Taiwan "a symbol of peace jointly safeguarded by Chinese people on both sides of the strait," Lai Ching-te, Taiwan's president, hosted three separate US congressional delegations and promised defence spending will hit 5% of GDP by 2030. Beijing punctuated the diplomacy with 17 aircraft and seven naval vessels detected during the Cheng-Xi summit, 15 planes crossing the median line. The island's TSMC reported record revenue of NT$1.13 trillion as American and Chinese pressure intensified.

Constitutional changes are spreading as governments prepare for a more dangerous world. Sanae Takaichi, Japan's prime minister, set a one-year deadline for proposing amendments to the pacifist constitution, backed by the LDP's 316-seat supermajority in the Diet — the most concrete plan for changing Article 9 in postwar history. Lee Jae-myung, South Korea's president, won backing from 187 lawmakers for constitutional amendments that would tighten martial law requirements, though he needs opposition support for the two-thirds majority required. Both leaders are rewriting the basic rules while their countries face external pressure.

Middle powers this week showed how they navigate between great powers: by keeping their options open. Even as Mr Prabowo courted Mr Putin in Moscow, Indonesia kept its UN peacekeeping troops in Lebanon after three soldiers died — one to Israeli tank fire, two to Hezbollah bombs. Mr Lee sparked a row with Israel over Palestinian rights while winning rare praise from Kim Jong Un for apologising over drone incursions. Japan downgraded China in its diplomatic bluebook while Ms Takaichi reached out to Iran's president about Strait of Hormuz security. Each country is building multiple relationships rather than choosing sides.

Regional institutions did not respond to the Middle East crisis this week. ASEAN, the Quad, and other groups stayed silent as member countries built their own energy deals and diplomatic strategies. The absence suggests these institutions either lack crisis management tools or countries prefer going it alone when threats are urgent.
