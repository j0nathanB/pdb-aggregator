Three major Asia-Pacific powers moved to secure energy supplies this week as the Middle East oil shock hit global markets. Australia deployed its liquefied natural gas exports as diplomatic leverage across Southeast Asia, making fuel deals with multiple countries. Japan locked in secure oil supplies through early 2027 by spreading its sources, including a fourfold increase in American imports. Indonesia sent Prabowo Subianto, the president, to Moscow for energy talks with Vladimir Putin.

Each country built its own solution using different tools and different partners. Australia leveraged existing gas relationships. Japan activated procurement networks. Indonesia pursued Russian cooperation through presidential diplomacy. None coordinated with the others, despite facing the same external shock. The region's energy politics are now mature enough that countries can adapt independently rather than scramble for collective action.

Taiwan faces a separate but sharper dilemma. Cheng Li-wun, chairwoman of the Kuomintang (KMT), met Xi Jinping in Beijing this week — the first such meeting in a decade — and secured new communication channels and ten Chinese policy measures. Even as those talks concluded, Lai Ching-te, the president, was hosting multiple US congressional delegations in Taipei for defence talks. The two diplomatic tracks run in parallel but point in opposite directions.

Regional institutions did not appear in any country's crisis response this week. ASEAN, the Quad, and other multilateral frameworks offered no collective energy security mechanisms as the Middle East shock rippled through Asia-Pacific markets.
