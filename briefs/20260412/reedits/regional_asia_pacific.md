Three major regional powers responded separately to the Middle East oil crisis this week, using different tools without coordination. Australia used diplomacy to secure fuel supplies across Southeast Asia, trading its LNG exports for supply agreements. Japan extended its oil supplies through early 2027, shifting sources with a fourfold increase in American imports. Prabowo Subianto, Indonesia's president, took a different path, travelling to Moscow for energy talks with Vladimir Putin.

Each country tackled the same crisis through its own methods — diplomacy, procurement, presidential visits — instead of using regional frameworks like ASEAN or the Quad. Countries can handle crises well alone, but the responses expose the limits of working together under pressure.

Taiwan faces the region's sharpest tension, caught between China and America. Cheng Li-wun, chairwoman of the opposition KMT, met Xi Jinping in Beijing for the first time in a decade, opening new communication channels as China announced ten policy measures. Even as these talks unfolded, Lai Ching-te, the president, hosted multiple US congressional delegations on defence cooperation in Taipei, opening separate channels that pull in opposite directions.

Neither ASEAN nor the Quad appeared this week, suggesting these frameworks cannot handle operational crises or countries prefer to act alone when under pressure.
