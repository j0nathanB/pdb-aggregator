Pakistan pulled in two directions this week: it hosted 21 hours of US-Iran talks while sending fighter jets and troops to Saudi Arabia under their alliance. This was Pakistan's first military deployment under the agreement, not just token support. Saudi Arabia did the same, taking Pakistani military support at King Abdulaziz Air Base while its foreign minister held his first call with Iran since the war began. The kingdom activated its alliances while keeping talks open with the power those alliances target. The approach spreads beyond these two countries. The UAE received S. Jaishankar, India's external affairs minister, for his first Gulf visit since the war started, while the crown prince led a delegation to China. This surge of contacts during the crisis shows regional consensus that Iranian pressure requires both talks and force, not one or the other. Iranian attacks on UAE infrastructure have reached 2,819 projectiles, with the first casualties at the Habshan gas facility. Yet even as the UAE hardens its language—the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC) now calls Iranian strikes "economic terrorism"—it keeps financial ties open to support its diplomacy.

Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) coordination mechanisms did not activate this week despite Iranian attacks on multiple member states. The absence of GCC coordination suggests either institutional weakness during crisis or deliberate preference for bilateral responses over regional action.
