Pakistan spent 21 hours mediating between the United States and Iran this week, then sent fighter jets to defend Iran's rival Saudi Arabia. JD Vance, the US vice-president, met Iranian officials in Islamabad with Field Marshal Asim Munir mediating, but the talks collapsed over what Iran called "excessive demands." While those negotiations failed, Pakistani aircraft and troops deployed to King Abdulaziz Air Base under the Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement Pakistan signed with Saudi Arabia in September — the first time either country has activated the pact. The timing was no accident: Iran has killed 12 people and fired 2,819 projectiles at Gulf states in two months, forcing regional powers into difficult choices.

Saudi Arabia plays both sides. Faisal bin Farhan, the foreign minister, took his first call with Iran's foreign minister Abbas Araghchi since the war began, discussing de-escalation even as Pakistani jets landed at his country's air base. Saudi air defences have intercepted 894 Iranian attacks since March, including 799 drones and 86 ballistic missiles, but the kingdom still talks to Tehran. When Iranian strikes damaged a pumping station on the East-West Pipeline, Saudi Arabia restored full capacity to 7 million barrels per day within days, showing both vulnerability and resilience.

The UAE took a harder line after Iran killed its first worker. Debris from intercepted missiles struck the Habshan gas facility this week, killing one person and prompting Sultan Al Jaber, chief executive of Abu Dhabi National Oil Company, to call Iranian behaviour "economic terrorism." Yet even as Mr Al Jaber declared the Strait of Hormuz "has never been Iran's to close," the UAE kept its economy moving: its state investment company Mubadala reported assets under management jumped 17% to $385 billion, and Crown Prince Khaled bin Mohamed led a delegation to Beijing to deepen relations with China. Mohammed bin Zayed, the president, met S. Jaishankar, India's external affairs minister, to discuss protection for 3.5 million Indian citizens in the Emirates.

Each country talks to everyone — Pakistan mediating while arming Saudi Arabia, Saudi Arabia calling Iran while activating defence pacts, the UAE hosting Indian and Chinese delegations while denouncing Iranian "terrorism." All agree the Iran crisis needs both guns and talks, with each making different bets rather than choosing sides.

The Gulf Cooperation Council did not coordinate responses during Iranian attacks on multiple member states. The absence of visible GCC coordination suggests either weak institutions during crisis or a deliberate preference for bilateral responses over group action.
