Pakistan sent fighter jets and troops to Saudi Arabia this week under their defence pact—the first military deployment under the agreement—even as Saudi Arabia held its first foreign minister call with Iran since the war began. Regional powers are deploying forces to counter Iran while talking to Iran.

The same pattern runs across the region. Pakistan hosted US-Iran talks while backing Saudi Arabia militarily. The UAE hosted S. Jaishankar, India's external affairs minister, for his first Gulf visit since the war started, while the UAE Crown Prince led a delegation to China. Each represents crisis diplomacy—urgent talks when tensions peak.

Regional powers agree the Iran crisis requires diplomacy and force at the same time. Rather than choosing sides or cutting off talks, these countries are keeping everyone talking. The timing suggests coordination, with each country playing its part in the regional balance.

The trick is keeping different relationships separate. Pakistan can deploy troops to Saudi Arabia while playing middleman with Iran. Saudi Arabia can accept Pakistani military support while calling Iran's foreign minister. The UAE can host Indian officials—close American partners—while building ties with China. Each relationship operates on its own track.

What did not happen matters too. Despite Iranian attacks on Gulf states, the GCC did not coordinate a response. This suggests either the organisation is weak or these countries chose to act alone rather than together.

GCC coordination mechanisms did not activate despite Iranian attacks on multiple member states. The absence suggests either institutional weakness during the crisis or a deliberate preference for bilateral responses over multilateral frameworks.
