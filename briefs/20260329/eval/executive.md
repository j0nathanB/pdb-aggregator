## Executive Brief (post-editorial chain)

This week revealed which coordination systems still work and which have collapsed. Countries can coordinate to resist American leadership, but they cannot coordinate to solve shared problems. When energy prices spiked from the Middle East war, every region scrambled for its own solution — Estonia cut growth forecasts, the Czech Republic stepped into fuel markets, Korea announced a 26.2 trillion won emergency budget, Japan opened strategic reserves, Australia triggered crisis protocols. Same shock, same timing, completely different responses. The machinery for global economic coordination has broken down.

But European political coordination works when the goal is saying no to Washington. Five countries rejected American demands for war with Iran, and they did it in lockstep. Germany's president declared such a war illegal, Britain adopted a defensive-only stance despite American pressure, Italy denied the United States base access for Iran operations, Spain began 'No to War' campaigns, and Norway criticised NATO leadership. This was the most coordinated European pushback since the 2003 Iraq War. The timing was too precise, the methods too similar. Europeans have learned to coordinate resistance even if they cannot coordinate crisis response.

The most troubling coordination is happening where no one wants it. Far-right parties broke through at the same time across three major European democracies — France's National Rally maintains front-runner status for 2027, Germany's Alternative for Germany (AfD) overtook the governing parties in national polls for the first time, Sweden's Sweden Democrats secured guaranteed cabinet seats after 2026. These gains came across different electoral systems and political cycles, suggesting that the same forces are hitting democratic institutions everywhere. The pattern looks deep rather than temporary — the kind of stress that reshapes political systems rather than just changing governments.

Crisis reveals what works and what does not. The world's economic institutions cannot coordinate responses to shared shocks, leaving countries to improvise national solutions to international problems. Regional political institutions can coordinate, but for resistance rather than problem-solving. And anti-establishment forces coordinate across borders without trying, spreading through democratic systems like a contagion. The result is a world where the wrong things work and the right things break down.
