## Regional Lead (post-editorial chain)

Germany's president declared Iran's war "illegal" this week, challenging what allies agreed as Western Europe splits during the Iran crisis. Frank-Walter Steinmeier's statement contradicts commitments to Israeli security, while Italy denied US bomber access to the Sigonella base and Norway criticised NATO leadership. Spain built opposition through "No to War" campaigns, and France pushed independence during an Asia tour. Each country chose different justifications and methods rather than presenting a joint EU alternative.

The fracturing shows the limits of working together when Europe and America see different threats. Europe's largest powers are defying what America expected on their own rather than offering a joint alternative. Germany uses presidential statements, Italy uses base access controls, Norway follows different diplomacy, while Spain builds public opposition. None consulted the others, and none worked through EU institutions.

Even as unity among allies crumbles, pressure from insurgent parties grows across Europe. Germany's governing coalition has hit historic approval lows as the Alternative for Germany overtakes the Christian Democratic Union in polls. France is moving towards a likely Jordan Bardella-Édouard Philippe runoff in 2027, with Marine Le Pen's National Rally taking symbolic anti-EU actions. Sweden brought the far right into government by promising the Sweden Democrats cabinet positions.

The twin pressures—the split among allies and domestic instability—constrain Europe's ability to work together as political survival becomes the main concern for mainstream parties. Rebel parties are either gaining power directly, as in Sweden, or threatening governing stability across major European democracies. This limits Europe's ability to respond jointly to external crises, even when national interests align against US policy.
