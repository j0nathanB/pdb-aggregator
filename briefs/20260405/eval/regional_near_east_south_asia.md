## Card Summary

Iran's shift to economic warfare forced Gulf states into crisis responses, with the UAE injecting emergency liquidity and Saudi Arabia cancelling major development projects.

## Regional Lead (post-editorial chain)

Iran has shifted from military to economic warfare, hitting petrochemical plants in the UAE while keeping Saudi Arabia under missile fire. Tehran now targets Gulf infrastructure rather than just military assets — attacking the UAE's Borouge petrochemical facility and ADNOC Habshan plant. Saudi Arabia has intercepted over 799 drones and 95 missiles since March 3, forcing both countries into crisis mode.

The campaign is forcing financial responses across the Gulf. The UAE's central bank injected 31 billion dirhams in emergency liquidity while Saudi Arabia cancelled the $4.7 billion NEOM Trojena resort and withdrew from hosting the 2029 Asian Winter Games. Iran's economic warfare is working — Gulf states must now choose between development plans and immediate security needs.

The crisis shows the cost of playing multiple sides. Pakistan's growing role as mediator between Washington and Tehran has created tensions with the UAE, which threatened to withdraw financial support. Beyond the Gulf, India maintains partnerships with both the West and Russia, while Turkey preserves its broker role between Putin, Zelensky and NATO.

Pakistan, India and Turkey show that countries can still stay independent under external pressure. But Iran's escalation shows that success with one partner increasingly creates friction with another. The cost of keeping options open has risen as regional conflicts force countries to pick sides.

## Gap Paragraphs

Regional institutions failed to coordinate responses to Iran's economic attacks through either the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) or Arab League. The absence of joint action during sustained attacks on Gulf infrastructure suggests either institutional weakness or countries' preference for handling the crisis separately rather than together.
