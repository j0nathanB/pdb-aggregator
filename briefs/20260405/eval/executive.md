## Executive Brief (post-editorial chain)

The old ways of cooperation are breaking down, but countries are not going it alone. Instead, they are building new networks through channels that bypass established institutions entirely. This is not the end of cooperation but its shift to new structures escaping the constraints of the old system.

Five Western European allies showed this by rejecting American demands for escalation against Iran through a coordinated campaign that used national institutions rather than European ones. Within days of each other, Joachim Gauck, Germany's president, declared war with Iran illegal; Britain adopted a defensive-only military posture; Italy denied the United States access to its bases for Iran operations; Spain launched a 'No to War' campaign; and Norway criticised NATO leadership. Each country used different methods, but the timing was deliberate — the most coordinated European push for independence since the 2003 Iraq War. Countries synchronised through parallel channels rather than joint European Union action, letting each government maintain plausible independence while delivering collective punch against American pressure.

The same pattern appears in economic crisis response, but in reverse. All regions now respond to identical shocks through purely national measures without consultation. Middle East energy disruptions triggered separate interventions across Eastern Europe: Estonia cut growth forecasts, the Czech Republic intervened in fuel markets, and Latvia provided emergency aviation loans. Asia-Pacific countries acted separately — South Korea's 26.2 trillion won budget, Japan's reserve deployment, Australia's fuel crisis response, Indonesia's kept subsidies — but none consulted the others despite facing the same pressures. This shows the complete collapse of the economic cooperation machinery that managed crises after 2008. Countries have the means to respond but no framework to coordinate their use.

France has spotted the opportunity and moved to fill it. Both Japan and South Korea made France a strategic partner this week through remarkably similar deals. Japan's state visit for Emmanuel Macron yielded rare earth cooperation agreements and Strait of Hormuz security coordination. South Korea won a Global Strategic Partnership with identical Strait of Hormuz provisions and supply chain diversification. The partnerships share similar structures and geographic focus despite separate bilateral negotiations, revealing France's drive to become a 'third pole' alternative to American-Chinese competition. By offering European diversification beyond traditional alliance structures, France is creating alternative networks that give middle powers new options amid great power competition — precisely what the collapsing traditional system no longer provides.
